\chapter{Sources, Method, and Reproducibility}
\label{chap:sources}
\markboth{Sources, Method, and Reproducibility}{Sources, Method, and Reproducibility}

\noindent This handbook marks every number with a two-axis evidence label so a reader never has to guess the provenance or the epistemic status of a figure. Labels of the form \textsc{[provenance][epistemic-status]} combine one item from each axis.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Axis 1 --- Source provenance:} where the number comes from.

\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item \textbf{\textsc{[1P]} First-party / primary source.} A vendor technical report, model card, or official documentation. These are the model's own stated parameters, benchmarks, and limits.
\item \textbf{\textsc{[canonical scenario]}} The book's own internally-defined canonical workload and model (Ch 4, Table 4-3). This is an \emph{internal} source the book sets up so its arithmetic is anchored and reproducible; it is author-defined, \emph{not} external first-party evidence. Used to distinguish "where this number comes from inside the book" from "\textsc{[1P]}" (a real vendor source).
\item \textbf{\textsc{[2°]} Secondary source.} A third-party measurement, survey, or synthesis (e.g. an independent benchmark or a community write-up). Requires independent verification.
\item \textbf{\textsc{[LAB]}} Measured on the author's own hardware. These are home-lab measurements, not production hardware, and are used to illustrate mechanisms and orders of magnitude.
\item \textbf{\textsc{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}} A worked-example or scenario input chosen to demonstrate a calculation, not a reported measurement. It is a number we assumed so the arithmetic would be concrete.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Axis 2 --- Epistemic status:} what the number claims to be.

\begin{itemize}\tightlist
\item \textbf{\textsc{[FACT]}} Stated by the source as a fact (e.g. a parameter count or a vendor benchmark).
\item \textbf{\textsc{[DERIVED]}} Computed by arithmetic from stated inputs. The derivation is our work; the inputs come from a source item.
\item \textbf{\textsc{[ASSUMPTION]}} A deliberately chosen scenario input (a number we picked so a worked example would be concrete), distinct from a hypothesis because it is not a claim about the real workload at all. It pairs with a provenance of \textsc{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}: \textsc{[ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]}.
\item \textbf{\textsc{[HYPOTHESIS]}} A reasoned expectation that has not yet been validated. Deliberately kept, because it distinguishes what we know from what we only expect.
\item \textbf{\textsc{[UNRESOLVED]}} An open item that could not be pinned to a first-party source at the time of writing. Such items are flagged in the text and \emph{must be re-checked against current vendor documentation before any procurement or deployment decision}; they are precisely the numbers most likely to have drifted.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Scope of the labeling.} The two-axis label is mandatory for every \emph{load-bearing} quantitative claim --- a number that a later calculation, sizing decision, or comparison depends on. Such a claim carries the labels directly (inline) or via an identified table or section that states them for every row. Purely illustrative or rhetorical numbers that no later calculation depends on (e.g.\ a round figure used only to make a sentence concrete) may carry the labels in the surrounding prose rather than inline. This is the unambiguous exception the preface refers to: the test is whether a downstream number depends on it.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Reading a two-axis label.} \textsc{[1P][FACT]} means a vendor-stated fact; \textsc{[1P][DERIVED]} means arithmetic we computed from a first-party input; \textsc{[LAB][FACT]} means a home-lab measurement; \textsc{[ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]} means a number we chose so the worked example would be concrete — it is not evidence of anything about the real workload. When only one axis is shown, the other is implied by the surrounding sentence (e.g. a bare \textsc{[DERIVED]} carries the status, with the provenance stated in prose).

\noindent\textbf{The \textsc{[S\#]} keys are a bibliography index, not a provenance.} A label such as \textsc{[S6][1P]} combines a source \emph{reference key} (\textsc{[S6]}, pointing to an entry in the References chapter) with a provenance on that source (\textsc{[1P]}). The \textsc{[S\#]} key is not a fifth provenance item; it merely says \emph{which} reference the first-party or secondary evidence comes from. A composite label always carries at least one provenance item in addition to any \textsc{[S\#]} key.

\noindent\textbf{Prices, models, and benchmarks are a moving target.} The model names, parameter counts, context limits, prices, and throughput figures in this book are a dated snapshot. They vary with the provider, region, commit/on-demand/spot status, hardware SKU, precision and sparsity mode, quantization, batch and concurrency, software stack, GPU count and topology, kernel version, warmup procedure, and percentile method used. \textbf{Re-verify before acting.}

\noindent\textbf{Reproducibility.} The book's quantitative examples are worked from the stated inputs in the surrounding text; the home-lab measurements were made on the author's own hardware under the configuration described in the chapter. Where a number is flagged as unresolved, the workspace did not have a first-party source at hand, and we say so rather than present an estimate as fact.

\noindent\textbf{Home-lab scope.} The author's lab is a CPU-only workstation (Intel i5-3210M, four cores, 15\,GB RAM). Measurements reflect that environment and the reduced-precision / small-model regime; they are used to \emph{illustrate} mechanisms and orders of magnitude, not to characterize production hardware. Kernel-performance and throughput figures from public sources are to be treated as vendor-reported until re-measured on the target cluster. \textbf{No H100/H200 production-performance claim in this book should be interpreted as a home-lab measurement unless explicitly labeled as such.}

\noindent\textbf{Code and data availability.} The build scripts that produce this book (text $\to$ LaTeX $\to$ PDF, plus the figure generators) are in the accompanying repository. The figures are generated where possible (matplotlib and a hand-drawn diagram pipeline), so the arithmetic and layouts are inspectable rather than opaque artifacts.

\noindent\textbf{Claim-to-source ledger.} Every load-bearing number traces to a source by a fixed path, so a reader can re-derive it without guessing: (1) a \textsc{[S\#]} key resolves to the matching entry in the References chapter, which gives the work, venue, and a stable identifier (arXiv id or first-party URL); all such sources were accessed as of the build date (v20260913). (2) a number derived from the canonical scenario traces to Ch 4, Table 4-3 (the single set of inputs every chapter cites). (3) the calculation itself is either the in-text arithmetic (stated with its operands) or the figure's generator script in the repository (\texttt{render/fig\_*.py}), which is reproducible from the stated inputs. A \textsc{[1P]} first-party claim is cited to its source but is a vendor statement, not an independent re-measurement; per the ``moving target'' note above, re-verify it against the current source before acting. This ledger is the mechanism; it does not substitute for the independent re-derivation and print proof that the release gates require.

\noindent\textbf{Canonical parameter-and-unit ledger.} Every derivative fleet/TCO/memory table cites this single ledger for its geometry, precision, and byte conventions, so a number is never silently re-based between the raw tensor overhead, the cache-block allocation, and the serving-engine observed footprint. The canonical scenario (Ch 4, Table 4-3) is full multi-head attention (MHA), 80 layers, hidden width 8192, BF16/FP16 KV, on an 8\,$\times$\,H100 host. The ledger is split into two blocks: the \emph{geometry and byte arithmetic} and the \emph{planning and engine observations}.

\noindent Two conventions apply throughout. (1) ``KV raw per token'' always means the un-allocated array product, stated in base-2 (MiB) and base-10 (MB) as appropriate; ``KV allocated'' or ``measured footprint'' includes the metadata and cache-block overhead. Do not conflate them. (2) A precision adjective is always attached to its subject (``FP16 \emph{weights}'' vs ``FP8 \emph{KV}''), never implied. (3) An architecture-specific advantage (GQA $\sim$8$\times$, MLA, hybrid) is a sensitivity for that configuration only and is not inherited by a different model.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement-status hierarchy.} A number in this book can fall into one of four stages, and they are not interchangeable. Reading ``ceiling'' as ``achieved'' is the single most consequential mistake an architect can make with these figures. The same convention is applied in the fleet and TCO synthesis tables (Chs. 12, 17, 20, 22).

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{1.75in} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{What it is / what it is not}\\
\midrule
\textbf{ANALYTICAL} & an arithmetic or physical upper bound from stated inputs (e.g.\ KV occupancy ceiling $C$, or the per-host prefill rate). Valid for \emph{planning}; \emph{not} an achieved rate.\\
\textbf{ILLUSTRATIVE} & a worked-example value on stated assumptions, chosen to make the method concrete; not a measurement or a vendor claim.\\
\textbf{MODELED (offered-load)} & the result of a simulation or queueing model on a synthetic or recorded load curve; reflects the model, not a measured system.\\
\textbf{MEASURED (SLO-goodput)} & the only stage that demonstrates a deployed system meets a rate and a tail-latency SLO on real hardware. Requires a load test with a recorded trace.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\noindent In the text and figures, an \textsc{[ANALYTICAL]}, \textsc{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}, \textsc{[MODELED]}, or \textsc{[MEASURED]} tag states the stage; where the stage is not yet determined, the value is marked \textsc{[UNMEASURED]} and cannot be treated as achieved throughput. A capacity ceiling becomes a deployment claim only after the \textsc{[MEASURED]} stage is reached. Author-run probes (a one-off API call to probe a capability) are labeled \textsc{[LAB]} and are evidence of \emph{one} functional path, not a model benchmark or an [1P] vendor claim; they never double as a vendor validation.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Presentation of evidence tags.} Inline provenance and status tags are carried on a number because a later calculation or decision depends on it (load-bearing). When the \emph{same} tag applies to a contiguous run of related values or an entire section, the book does not repeat it on every line: it states the tag once in a boxed per-section \emph{Assumptions \& evidence} note (or in a table footnote that names the whole group), and the reader applies it to every value the note governs. A tag is always shown inline on a number that carries a \emph{different} status from its neighbours, so the reader can spot a status change without re-reading the surrounding prose. Never strip a source/status control; group it, so it stays legible rather than fragmenting the sentence.
