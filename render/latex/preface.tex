% Preface — From Token to Fleet: An AI Solution Architect's Handbook

For a long time I wanted the systematic knowledge of what it means to work
as an AI Solution Architect: how to reason across the layers of an AI system
--- from tokens and models to workloads, systems, infrastructure and fleets
--- so that a vague requirement becomes a defensible architecture. I kept
looking for the resource that would lay all of that down in one coherent
thread; what I found were deep dives into single layers --- models, serving,
infrastructure, agents --- but no single spine connecting them into the one
thing an architect actually does: chaining a real workload to a defensible
solution.

That feedback loop --- curiosity, question, research, model, write --- is the
engine of this book: writing the reasoning down forces every assumption,
quantity and trade-off into the open and exposes the gaps that deserve
further measurement. The field moves fast and I am still learning too; this
handbook is a continuously evolving reference, shared layer by layer,
decision by decision, from the token up to the fleet. If it helps others
build the same systematic mental model, it has outdone what I planned it for.

\section*{The thesis}

The book has one thesis, and it is the spine of every chapter:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Given an ambiguous problem, how do I systematically arrive at a
defensible AI architecture?}
\end{quote}

The canonical method --- the decision loop Chapter~22 makes explicit --- is
previewed here and then assembled piece by piece. Chapters~1--21 acquire the
tools: tokens, workloads, model structure, arithmetic intensity, memory,
parallelism, serving, benchmarking, TCO, fleet operations and the factory
gate. Chapter~22 synthesizes them into the professional reasoning loop that
runs through the whole book --- placed late not because it belongs at the
end but because the reader needs the technical vocabulary first to see it
operate on a real workload.

That loop --- echoed by every chapter --- runs from requirements and
workload characterization, through constraints and candidate architectures,
to benchmarking, bottleneck analysis, optimization, total-cost reality
checks and a final red-team challenge. Parts~I--V walk the technical
substrate layer by layer; Part~VI turns to the discipline itself: how one
learns to reason across those layers.

\section*{How the book is organized}

The six parts mirror the layers an architect reasons across:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Part I --- The Token.} Models and mechanisms, from a single
token to the memory and compute arithmetic that prices every decision.
  \item \textbf{Part II --- The Workload.} The job the system does: workload
characterization and measuring what matters.
  \item \textbf{Part III --- The System.} Memory, compute, communication,
parallelism, and serving.
  \item \textbf{Part IV --- The Architecture.} Designing, benchmarking,
engineering, and costing a system.
  \item \textbf{Part V --- The Fleet.} Operating AI at scale, and agents.
  \item \textbf{Part VI --- The Architect.} The spine made explicit, plus a
patterns library.
\end{itemize}

A worked canonical scenario --- an enterprise retrieval-augmented
question-answering workload on a 70B-class model --- runs through the book,
so every mechanism is anchored in reproducible arithmetic. One caveat, so the
book is not misread: the scenario is a \textbf{teaching instrument}, not a
claim about what an AI workload \emph{is}. Real workloads span a wide design
space --- long-context RAG (KV/memory-bound), high-throughput chat
(decode-bound), batch inference (utilization/cost-bound), reasoning
(test-time-compute-bound), agentic (concurrency/variance-bound),
embeddings, vision, fine-tuning, training --- and each shifts which quantity
dominates. The scenario picks one tractable, input-heavy RAG case and carries
it through so the reasoning stays continuous; a real deployment sits
somewhere else in that space, and the chapters give the arithmetic to
re-derive the bounds for \emph{your} point.

\section*{How to use this book}

The chapters build on one another, but not every reader needs them in order.
A few reading paths, depending on where the reading begins:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{New to inference systems.} Parts I $\to$ II $\to$ III sequentially —
the token, then the workload, then the system. This is the path that builds
the vocabulary the later chapters assume.

\textbf{Practicing architect.} Start with Ch.~22, the reasoned loop, then use
Parts I–V as the quantitative reference it calls on.

\textbf{Infrastructure specialist.} Parts III–V (system, architecture, fleet)
directly; skim I–II for the workload framing.

\textbf{Customer-facing solution architect.} Parts II, IV, and VI — scoping a
workload, designing an architecture, and the professional discipline.

\textbf{Frontier-model reader.} Appendix A alone, but only after Chs.~3, 7–11,
so the total-vs-active, KV, and serving mechanism the appendix leans on are
already in hand.
\end{quote}

\subsection*{The operational decision checklist}

The full reasoning loop is earned in Chapter~22, but its shape is useful from
the first page: the same six questions recur in the same order, and the
chapters that follow each answer one of them. Keep this list in view while
reading --- it is the spine made operational.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{1. What is the workload?} — tokens in/out, rps average and peak,
concurrency, latency SLOs. (Ch.~4)\\
\textbf{2. What fits on one host?} — weight residency, KV per token, the
concurrency floor. (Chs.~3, 7)\\
\textbf{3. Which phase binds?} — prefill compute or decode bandwidth, at this
operating point. (Chs.~2, 8)\\
\textbf{4. How many hosts?} — the fleet sized from the per-host bound, with
headroom and failover. (Chs.~16–17)\\
\textbf{5. Who serves it, and how?} — batching, parallelism, P/D
disaggregation, scheduling. (Chs.~10–11, 20)\\
\textbf{6. Is it the right call?} — TCO, the red-team challenge, the recorded
decision. (Chs.~16, 22, 25)
\end{quote}

The six questions are cross-cutting --- Q1 in Part~II, Q2–3 in Parts~I/III,
Q4–5 in Parts~III–V, Q6 in Parts~IV/VI. Chapter~22 gives the technical
justification for why the loop has this shape and where it breaks; this
checklist is the operational view of that same loop.

\section*{The Architect's Evidence Discipline}

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.94\linewidth}{\textbf{Evidence boundary --- four registers, and where each is used.}
\textbf{MEASURED} --- an instrumented run under the Chapter~14 protocol; used only where the book reports a benchmark it actually ran, never for a planning screen.
\textbf{ANALYTICAL} --- arithmetic on stated inputs (roofline, KV-residency and capacity screens, the $C/W$ per-host bound and the host counts derived from it): a bound, not an observed rate --- not a measured throughput, not a lower guaranteed rate, not SLO-goodput, no p95/p99 guarantee, each conditional on its stated assumptions (e.g. the assumed mean KV residency time).
\textbf{ILLUSTRATIVE} --- a chosen scenario input or worked example (\textbf{[canonical scenario]} or \textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}, paired with \textbf{ASSUMPTION} or \textbf{DERIVED}), on the book's teaching geometry, to be re-derived per deployment.
\textbf{HYPOTHESIS} --- reasoned but not yet validated (with \textbf{UNRESOLVED} for questions the book leaves open), used where mechanism is argued without measurement.
Repeated inline caveats in the chapters are one-sentence reminders of this panel; the full label rule set lives in the Sources, Method, and Reproducibility chapter.}}
\end{center}

Every \emph{load-bearing} quantitative claim carries Axes 1 and 2 as an
inline pair (e.g.\\\textbf{[1P][DERIVED]}); Axis~3, measurement status, rides
along where a claim's status is known. A claim carries the labels
\emph{directly} (inline) or \emph{via an identified table or section} that
states them (e.g.\ a worked-example table whose header or footnote gives
provenance and status for every row); purely illustrative or rhetorical
numbers that no later calculation depends on may carry them in the
surrounding prose --- exceptions are defined unambiguously in the Sources,
Method, and Reproducibility chapter.

\textbf{Provenance (Axis 1)} records where the number came from:
\textbf{[1P]} first-party (the model vendor's own spec), \textbf{[canonical
scenario]} the book's own internally defined workload and model (Ch.~4,
Table 4-2), \textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE]} a non-canonical worked-example or
scenario input chosen to make a calculation concrete, \textbf{[2°]}
reputable second-hand (textbook, survey, independent benchmark), or
\textbf{[LAB]} measured on the author's own home-lab hardware (illustrative
of mechanism, not production).

\textbf{Epistemic status (Axis 2)} records what was done to it:
\textbf{FACT} directly stated by the source, \textbf{DERIVED} computed by
arithmetic from stated inputs, \textbf{ASSUMPTION} a deliberately chosen
scenario input (paired with a scenario provenance --- \textsc{[canonical
scenario]} or \textsc{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}), \textbf{HYPOTHESIS} reasoned but not
yet validated, or \textbf{UNRESOLVED} a question the book leaves open. A
label pair always takes one item from each of the first two axes, e.g.\
\textbf{[1P][DERIVED]} or \textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]}. The third
axis --- \textbf{measurement status} (ANALYTICAL / ILLUSTRATIVE / MODELED /
MEASURED, or UNMEASURED where the stage is not yet determined) --- rides with
that pair or stands alone.

One mapping is easy to get wrong, so it is stated explicitly: the canonical
scenario (Ch.~4, Table~4-2) keeps its own \textbf{[canonical scenario]}
provenance, and within it the epistemic-status axis splits every value into
exactly two cases. An \emph{input or parameter assumption} --- a traffic
level, a latency target, a price --- is \textbf{[canonical
scenario][ASSUMPTION]}; a value \emph{arithmetically derived} from those
assumed inputs --- an execution occupancy, a tokens-per-second rate, a
cost-per-request --- is \textbf{[canonical
scenario][DERIVED]}. A vendor hardware constant (e.g.\ the H100 footprint) is
\textbf{[1P][FACT]}, never \textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}. \textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}
is reserved for a non-canonical \emph{hypothetical} worked example: its
inputs are \textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]} and its derived results
\textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED]}. ASSUMPTION/DERIVED says whether a number
was chosen or computed; \textbf{[canonical scenario]}/\textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}
says whether it is the book's fixed reference set or a one-off what-if.

Two habits matter most. Arithmetic belongs on the epistemic-status axis, not
the provenance axis: a worked example computed from a model card is
\textbf{[1P][DERIVED]}, never \textbf{[2°]}. And an architect's conclusions
are only as defensible as the units behind them, so every major worked
example ends with a unit check and a sanity check (does this result stay
inside the physically possible?). How each label is verified is set out in
the Sources, Method, and Reproducibility chapter.

\textbf{Evidence cutoff and edition date.} This edition is build
\textbf{v20260913} (2026-09-13) with a single evidence cutoff of
\textbf{2026-08-30}: model, vendor and hardware claims are stated as of that
date, and the ``As of 2026-08'' markers in the body refer to this same
cutoff. Primary sources (arXiv reports, model cards, vendor pages) were
accessed as of the build date and each is cited with a stable identifier in
the References chapter; fast-moving model and vendor assertions (Appendix~A
in particular) are dated individually against their primary sources.
\emph{Post-cutoff policy:} any model, price or spec that changed after
2026-08-30 is not silently updated in this edition; a later build revalidates
the fast-moving claims against current vendor documentation and flags any
post-cutoff change separately rather than inheriting the prior value.

Dated addenda sit outside that snapshot. Appendix~A, A.6 carries a boxed
2026-09-27 capability probe under revision
\texttt{v20260913+addendum-20260927}; the separately dated 2026-10-28
technology notes in the body and Appendix~A carry revision
\texttt{v20260913+addendum-20261028}; and a superseding re-verification of
the DeepSeek model-service status (Appendix~A, A.3) is carried under
\texttt{v20260913+addendum-20261101}, recording the affected claims and the
source access date and revising only that current-status note, not the August
architectural snapshot. These notes distinguish inspected sources and code
from locally reproduced measurements; neither addendum silently updates the
original August comparisons or the First-hand Sources list.

\emph{Build identifier and generation.} The build identifier v20260913 is the
\emph{content} edition: it names the source snapshot and evidence cutoff the
text reflects. Re-rendering that same source to a PDF on a later date (e.g.\
a proof generated in October) changes neither the content edition nor the
evidence cutoff --- only the PDF's generation timestamp, which is recorded in
the document metadata and is not evidence. A change to the \emph{content}
requires a new build identifier, which invalidates the prior edition and is
recorded in the release log --- except a dated post-cutoff addendum carried
under its own revision identifier and listed in the Appendix addenda row.

\emph{Release log.} The revision that produced this PDF is recorded at the
end of the Sources, Method, and Reproducibility chapter, in the ``Edition
history'' panel: source revision (commit), build identifier, evidence
cutoff, appendix addenda and generation date.

\section*{Reading conventions}

Three conventions keep the book consistent and honest about its own limits.

\textbf{Terminology.} GPU memory and VRAM are used generically for
accelerator-local memory; HBM means high-bandwidth-memory specifically,
which is what the worked examples assume. TTFT and time-to-first-token are
used interchangeably; so are RAG and retrieval-augmented-generation.
\textbf{Prefill latency is not interchangeable with TTFT:} prefill is a
\textbf{component of, not identical to}, the user-observed TTFT. In the
book's convention (Ch.~2, 4), $\text{TTFT}_{\text{e2e}} = \text{retrieval} +
\text{admission/queue} + \text{tokenization/transfer} + \text{prefill} +
\text{first decode step}$, and the canonical 1.2~s budget names the two
dominant modeled terms (retrieval $\sim$120~ms + prefill $\sim$1.08~s). The
admission/queue, tokenization/transfer and first-decode-step terms are
separate and positive, so the $\sim$1.08~s prefill figure is a
\textbf{two-term-only upper reach, not a feasible deployed prefill target}:
write it as $\text{budget\_prefill} \le 1.08\,\mathrm{s} - B_\text{other}$
until measurement supplies $B_\text{other}$, and read any ``meets 1.2~s''
statement as conditional on a named tested workload and end-to-end timing
(S-01). The phrase ``prefill latency'' therefore names one term of that sum,
never the whole TTFT. Where a distinction is load-bearing --- prefill vs
decode, total vs active parameters, model vs system capacity, prefill vs
TTFT --- the book holds it sharply: synonyms are a readability concession,
and load-bearing contrasts are never relaxed (Ch.~2, 4, 8, 17).

\textbf{Hardware is a generic stand-in.} The worked arithmetic is anchored to
concrete constants --- for example, an H100 GPU's ~0.989 PFLOPS BF16 dense
tensor-core peak and ~3.35 TB/s HBM bandwidth, with an 8\texttimes H100 host
used as the canonical deployment unit --- because arithmetic needs concrete
anchors. Those values are a \emph{current} high-end accelerator, presented
as an example, not a prophecy: the H200, B100/B200 and later generations
change the constants, not the method. Re-derive the constants from the
current hardware sheet; the roofline (Ch.~8) and the canonical arithmetic
(Ch.~7) keep the method independent of any single generation. Forward-looking
numbers are marked ILLUSTRATIVE.

\textbf{Quick reference.} For a day-to-day cheat-sheet: Chapter~26 (\S2.1)
carries a workload-to-strategy decision matrix, and Chapter~25's Architecture
Decision Record is a reusable template to copy into a repository; re-read
those, plus the evidence discipline above, whenever a chapter feels dense.

\section*{A note on currency}

The mechanisms in this handbook --- tokens, memory floors, rooflines,
parallelism, KV arithmetic --- are durable; the frontier is not, and a
handbook for architects should carry the latest technology information. The
Appendix therefore
surveys the frontier open-weight architectures of 2026, mapped back to the
mechanisms and decisions explored in the body of the book --- and closes on
the question that is more durable than any single architecture: \emph{where
should the intelligence live}.
