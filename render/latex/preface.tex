% Preface — From Token to Fleet: An AI Solution Architect's Handbook

For a long time I wanted to build the systematic knowledge of what it means
to work as an AI Solution Architect: how to reason across the layers of an
AI system --- from tokens and models to workloads, systems, infrastructure,
and fleets --- so that a vague requirement can be turned into a defensible
architecture. I kept looking for the resource that would lay all of that
down in one coherent thread. What I found were deep dives into single
layers --- models, serving, infrastructure, agents --- but no single spine
that connected them into the one thing an architect actually does: chaining
a real workload to a defensible solution.

That feedback loop --- curiosity, question, research, model, write --- is the engine of this book: writing the reasoning down forces every assumption, quantity, and trade-off to become explicit, and exposes the gaps that deserve further measurement. The AI field moves fast, and I am still learning too. This handbook is a continuously evolving reference — developed and shared layer by layer, decision by decision, from the token up to the fleet. If it helps others build the same systematic mental model, then it has outdone what I had planned it for.

\section*{The thesis}

The book has one thesis, and it is the spine of every chapter:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Given an ambiguous problem, how do I systematically arrive at a
defensible AI architecture?}
\end{quote}

The canonical method --- the decision loop that Chapter~22 makes explicit
--- is previewed in the Preface, then assembled piece by piece. Chapters
1--21 acquire the tools: tokens, workloads, model structure, arithmetic
intensity, memory, parallelism, serving, benchmarking, TCO, fleet
operations, and the factory gate. Chapter~22 then synthesizes those tools
into the professional reasoning loop that runs through the whole book,
placed late not because it belongs at the end but because the reader needs
the technical vocabulary first to see it operate on a real workload.

That loop --- which every chapter echoes --- runs from requirements and
workload characterization, through constraints and candidate architectures,
to benchmarking, bottleneck analysis, optimization, total-cost reality
checks, and a final red-team challenge. Parts~I--V walk through the
technical substrate, layer by layer. Part~VI turns to the discipline
itself: how one learns to reason across those layers.

\section*{How the book is organized}

The six parts mirror the layers an architect reasons across:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Part I --- The Token.} Models and mechanisms, from a single
token to the memory and compute arithmetic that prices every decision.
  \item \textbf{Part II --- The Workload.} The job the system does: workload
characterization and measuring what matters.
  \item \textbf{Part III --- The System.} Memory, compute, communication,
parallelism, and serving.
  \item \textbf{Part IV --- The Architecture.} Designing, benchmarking,
engineering, and costing a system.
  \item \textbf{Part V --- The Fleet.} Operating AI at scale, and agents.
  \item \textbf{Part VI --- The Architect.} The spine made explicit, plus a
patterns library.
\end{itemize}

A worked canonical scenario --- an enterprise retrieval-augmented question-answering
workload on a 70B-class model --- runs through the book, so every mechanism is
anchored in concrete, reproducible arithmetic rather than abstraction. One caveat, so
the book is not misread: this scenario is a \textbf{teaching instrument}, not a claim
about what an AI workload \emph{is}. Real workloads span a wide design space ---
long-context RAG (KV/memory-bound), high-throughput chat (decode-bound), batch
inference (utilization/cost-bound), reasoning (test-time-compute-bound), agentic
(concurrency/variance-bound), embeddings, vision, fine-tuning, training --- and each
shifts which quantity dominates. The canonical scenario picks a single, tractable,
input-heavy RAG case and carries it through so the reasoning stays continuous; a real
deployment will sit somewhere else in that space, and the chapters are written to
give the reader the arithmetic to re-derive the bounds for \emph{their} point.

\section*{How to use this book}

The chapters build on one another, but not every reader needs them in order.
A few reading paths, depending on where the reading begins:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{New to inference systems.} Parts I $\to$ II $\to$ III sequentially —
the token, then the workload, then the system. This is the path that builds
the vocabulary the later chapters assume.

\textbf{Practicing architect.} Start with Ch.~22, the reasoned loop, then use
Parts I–V as the quantitative reference it calls on.

\textbf{Infrastructure specialist.} Parts III–V (system, architecture, fleet)
directly; skim I–II for the workload framing.

\textbf{Customer-facing solution architect.} Parts II, IV, and VI — scoping a
workload, designing an architecture, and the professional discipline.

\textbf{Frontier-model reader.} Appendix A alone, but only after Chs.~3, 7–11,
so the total-vs-active, KV, and serving mechanism the appendix leans on are
already in hand.
\end{quote}

\section*{The Architect's Evidence Discipline}

Every quantitative claim in this book carries two orthogonal labels, and
keeping these axes separate is itself a discipline. The \textbf{provenance}
axis records where the number came from: \textbf{[1P]} first-party (the model
vendor's own spec), \textbf{[canonical scenario]} the book's own internally
defined workload and model (Ch.~4, Table 4-3), \textbf{[2°]} reputable
second-hand (textbook, survey, independent benchmark), \textbf{[LAB]} measured
on the author's own home-lab hardware (illustrative of mechanism, not
production), or \textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE]} a worked-example or scenario input
chosen to make a calculation concrete. The \textbf{epistemic-status} axis
records what was done to it: \textbf{FACT} directly stated by the source,
\textbf{DERIVED} computed by arithmetic from stated inputs,
\textbf{ASSUMPTION} a deliberately chosen scenario input (paired with a
provenance of \textsc{[ILLUSTRATIVE]}), \textbf{HYPOTHESIS} reasoned but not
yet validated, or \textbf{UNRESOLVED} a question the book leaves open. A label
always combines one item from each axis, e.g. \textbf{[1P][DERIVED]} or
\textbf{[ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]}.

Two habits matter most. First, arithmetic belongs on the epistemic-status axis,
not the provenance axis: a worked example computed from a model card is
\textbf{[1P][DERIVED]}, never \textbf{[2°]} just because arithmetic was done.
Second, the conclusions an architect draws are only as defensible as the units
behind them --- so every major worked example ends with a unit check and a
sanity check (does this result stay inside the physically possible?). That
habit is part of the discipline, not an appendix. The full statement of this
scheme, including how each label is verified, is in the Sources, Method, and
Reproducibility chapter.

\section*{Reading conventions}

Three conventions keep the book consistent and honest about its own limits.

\textbf{Terminology.} For the capacity arithmetic in this handbook, GPU
memory and VRAM are used generically for accelerator-local memory; HBM refers
specifically to high-bandwidth-memory implementations, which is what the
worked examples assume. TTFT, time-to-first-token and prefill latency
likewise; RAG and retrieval-augmented-generation are the same. Where a
distinction is architecturally load-bearing --- prefill versus decode, total
versus active parameters, model capacity versus system capacity --- the book
holds it sharply. Synonyms are a readability concession; load-bearing
contrasts are never relaxed (Ch.~2, 8, 17).

\textbf{Hardware is a generic stand-in.} The worked arithmetic is anchored to
concrete constants (e.g. an 8\texttimes H100 host at roughly 1 PFLOPS FP16
and ~3.4 TB/s HBM) because arithmetic needs concrete anchors. Those values
are a \emph{current} high-end accelerator, presented as an example, not a
prophecy: the H200, B100/B200 and later generations change the constants, not
the method. Re-derive the constants from the current hardware sheet; the
roofline (Ch.~8) and the canonical arithmetic (Ch.~7) keep the method
independent of any single generation. Forward-looking numbers are marked
ILLUSTRATIVE.

\textbf{Quick reference.} For a day-to-day cheat-sheet: Chapter~26 (\S2.1)
carries a workload-to-strategy decision matrix, and Chapter~25's Architecture
Decision Record is a reusable template to copy into a repository. Those, plus
the evidence discipline above, are worth re-reading first whenever a chapter
feels dense.

\section*{A note on currency}

The mechanisms in this handbook --- tokens, memory floors, rooflines,
parallelism, KV arithmetic --- are durable; they do not change as the
industry moves. But the frontier does move, and a handbook for architects
should carry the latest technology information. The Appendix therefore
surveys the frontier open-weight architectures of 2026, mapped back to the
mechanisms and decisions explored in the body of the book --- and closes on
the question that is more durable than any single architecture: \emph{where
should the intelligence live}.
