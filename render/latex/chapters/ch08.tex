\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

After chapters on tokens, workloads, and memory, the architect naturally
asks: \emph{how much compute does this actually require, and at what
point does the hardware cease to be the limiting factor?} This chapter
gives us the FLOP-scale arithmetic, the arithmetic-intensity roofline,
and the utilization framework that lets us answer that question without
guessing. We will move from per-token FLOPs to prefill PFLOP counts to
sustained MFU on real hardware, and then to the roofline that tells us
whether a given layer is compute-bound or memory-bound --- the
difference that decides whether we should reach for batching,
quantization, or a different hardware generation. Throughout, the
arithmetic anchors to the canonical enterprise-Q\&A RAG scenario (Ch 4,
\hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}): \textasciitilde10 rps average / \textasciitilde40 rps peak
traffic, with 70B FP16 weights and an 8×H100 host. KV cache per-token
figures are referenced at FP16 (\textasciitilde2.62 MB/token, i.e.~2.5
MiB) unless an 8-bit (\textasciitilde1.31 MB/token, 1.25 MiB) variant is
explicitly stated.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


Compute in a language model is priced in floating-point operations, or
FLOPs. Every matrix multiplication, every attention weight update, every
activation function evaluation contributes to the total. The architect's
first principle is that FLOPs scale linearly with parameter count and
context length, but the \emph{efficiency} with which those FLOPs are
executed depends on the hardware ridge point and the arithmetic
intensity of the kernel.

For a dense transformer layer, the dominant cost is the matrix
multiplication --- query-key, value-aggregation, and MLP projections. In
FP16, a single multiply-add counts as two FLOPs. The per-token cost is
therefore approximately 2 ×N FLOPs where N is the number of active
parameters. This is the arithmetic baseline: every token processed costs
roughly two floating-point operations per parameter.

\subsection{Fewer sequential evaluations can mean more total
work}\label{fewer-sequential-evaluations-can-mean-more-total-work}

\emph{Implementation observations: 2026-10-28. The work counters below
are illustrative, not measured performance.}

For an iterative language generator, the approximately 2N
projection-cost rule applies to processed positions, not just final
output tokens. Let \(P_j\) be the active parameter count and \(Q_j\) the
positions evaluated in network evaluation \(j\). A projection-dominated
work proxy is

\[F_{\text{projection}} \approx \sum_{j} 2 P_j Q_j.\]

The trace also needs attention pair counts, output-head work, routing,
sampling, refresh and verification. For a full dense-attention
evaluation over length L, attention work grows quadratically with L. For
K active queries over a cached prefix of length C, the attention main
term grows with K(C + K), before the architecture's head and layer
factors. Reducing the number of recomputed query positions does not
eliminate reads of a long prefix. \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

Consider an explicitly illustrative 7B-active-parameter model that emits
256 tokens in 32-token blocks and uses eight decoder evaluations per
block. There are eight blocks, hence 64 sequential decoder evaluations.
The decoder nevertheless evaluates 256 × 8 = 2,048 output positions. Its
projection proxy is 2 × 7B × 2,048 = 28.672 TFLOP. A simplified
output-only AR counter evaluates 256 positions and gives 3.584 TFLOP.
The block example has one quarter as many sequential evaluations but
eight times as much output-side projection work. These are different
quantities, not contradictory results.
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

This arithmetic omits prompt encoding, context attention,
block-finalization encoding, the output head, refresh, classifier-free
guidance, verification and communication. It also simplifies the book's
first-token-from-prefill convention. It compares counters, not
equal-quality models, and predicts neither a fourfold speedup nor an
eightfold end-to-end slowdown. A network-evaluation count must include
extra verification passes and the shapes of any combined guidance
branches, rather than count only outer sampler rounds. \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

Why might the higher-work path still finish earlier? Processing more
positions together can reuse loaded weights across more matrix rows and
improve low-batch utilization. Why might it lose under load? Optimized
AR request batching can already supply those rows, while refinement
continues to repeat work. Distributed execution also repeats
tensor-parallel collectives and any expert dispatch required by each
evaluation. The number of sequential rounds and the total communicated
bytes can move in opposite directions. \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} The low-concurrency
case is vendor-reported rather than re-measured here: Google's
DiffusionGemma card reports low-batch generation speed while warning of
quality and high-QPS limits
(\href{https://huggingface.co/google/diffusiongemma-26B-A4B-it/raw/f7f5b7f5fa82ffc52addd066915886d497f5517b/README.md}{DiffusionGemma
card}). \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}FACT{]}}

MoE adds a working-set issue. Active parameters per token do not equal
the union of experts touched by all positions in a parallel block.
LLaDA2.2 explicitly limits a block's expert pool before per-token
routing
(\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/inclusionAI/LLaDA2.X/main/LLaDA2_2_tech_report.pdf}{LLaDA2.2
technical report}, retrieved 2026-10-28). That bounds an execution
working set; it does not make the other resident experts disappear. The
useful measurements are expert-union size, dispatch bytes, load
imbalance and collective time, alongside active FLOPs.
\mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}FACT{]}}; measurement consequence \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

The resulting fleet decision follows measured service demand. At a fixed
task-quality and latency target, GPU-seconds, successful-request
throughput and resident state determine capacity. Output tokens divided
by denoising steps do not determine the number of hosts. \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

\subsection{The roofline diagnostic}\label{the-roofline-diagnostic}

But FLOPs alone do not tell the full story. The hardware can only
execute FLOPs if data is available --- weights, activations, and KV
cache all compete for the same HBM bandwidth. The arithmetic intensity
(FLOP/byte) determines whether a kernel is compute-bound (intensity
above the ridge point) or memory-bound (intensity below). This roofline
model is the central diagnostic tool of this chapter: it tells us, for
any given layer and precision, whether increasing FLOPs will actually
reduce latency or whether we are already starved for bytes.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Three different ``bounds'' --- keep them apart.} Throughout this
book, three words that all contain ``bound'' mean three different
things, and conflating them is a real error. \textbf{Compute-bound}
means performance is limited primarily by arithmetic throughput (the
kernel sits above the ridge; it is starved for FLOPs).
\textbf{Memory-bandwidth-bound} (what the roofline calls
``memory-bound'') means performance is limited by memory-transfer
bandwidth (the kernel sits below the ridge; it is starved for bytes
moving per second). \textbf{Memory-capacity-constrained} (equivalently
KV-capacity-constrained) means feasible concurrency or context is
limited by \emph{how much data fits in HBM} --- not by how fast it
moves. The canonical RAG workload illustrates all three without overlap:
prefill is compute-bound (high intensity), decode is bandwidth-bound
(low intensity), and long input raises KV residency, which
capacity-constrains how many requests can be resident at once.
``Input-heavy'' does \textbf{not} by itself mean ``memory-bound'' --- it
tends to raise prefill compute \emph{and} KV-capacity pressure, two
distinct effects. When the book says ``memory-bound'' without
qualification, it means \emph{bandwidth-bound} in the roofline sense;
capacity pressure is stated explicitly.
\end{quote}

\subsection{A resource model survives a backend change; an
implementation may
not}\label{a-resource-model-survives-a-backend-change-an-implementation-may-not}

\emph{Backend-documentation observations: 2026-10-28. They do not
establish feature availability at the edition's 2026-08-30 cutoff.}

The compute and memory equations apply across accelerator vendors. Their
constants belong to a specific device and execution path. A peak INT8
dense TOPS figure does not describe BF16 throughput, a sparse peak does
not describe a dense workload, and aggregate device memory is not one
allocation pool. The useful comparison starts with the model's
operators, resident bytes and communication pattern. It then asks which
kernels implement those operations on the candidate hardware

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


FLOPs are like the distance a car can travel on a gallon of fuel: it
tells us the system's capacity, not how long the trip will take. The
trip time is determined by the ridge point: if the arithmetic intensity
is below the ridge, adding more FLOPs won't help because we are waiting
for data. If we are above the ridge, we are limited by how fast the
compute can chew through operations. The roofline chart --- peak TFLOPS
on the vertical axis, FLOP/byte on the horizontal --- is the map that
resolves this. Where our kernel lands on that chart decides whether we
should profile memory bandwidth or compute throughput.

\emph{Read this ladder together with the roofline:} the roofline's
vertical axis (peak TFLOPS) and horizontal axis (FLOP/byte) are both
determined by \emph{where} on this hierarchy the work sits. A kernel is
compute-bound when its FLOPs run on the tensor cores and data fits in
on-chip memory (high arithmetic intensity); it is bandwidth-bound when
it must stream large tensors from HBM faster than the bytes can move
(low arithmetic intensity). So the ``bound'' classification of Ch. 8 is
not a property of the model alone --- it follows from where the model's
weights and the KV cache happen to be resident, and how much of the
computation a kernel can keep on-chip. Ch. 15's FlashAttention
discussion is exactly this ladder in action: keep more of the tile
on-chip, move fewer bytes between HBM and the compute units.

\ntsection{Worked Example: Canonical Scenario
Arithmetic}\label{worked-example-canonical-scenario-arithmetic}


The canonical scenario (Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}) is an enterprise Q\&A system:
the canonical 80-layer, hidden-8192, full-MHA 70B reference (a 70B-class
dense model), FP16 weights (\textasciitilde140 GB), 1 host with
8×H100-class GPUs (80 GB each), average prompt 1,200 tokens + 8K
retrieved context (\textasciitilde9.2K input), 300-token output, TTFT
budget 1.2 s (retrieval \textasciitilde120 ms + prefill), TPOT budget
\textasciitilde25 ms/token.

\emph{Scope.} ``1 host with 8×H100'' here is a \textbf{per-host /
per-request} feasibility container (does one host meet one request's
latency and memory SLO). It is \textbf{not} a claim that one host
absorbs the 40 rps peak arrival rate --- as Ch 17 derives, that peak
puts in-flight concurrency λ·W ≈ 344 at the \textasciitilde8.6 s service
time, far above one host's KV-residency ceiling (C ≈ 17.5, conservative
integer floor 17), so the \emph{fleet} needs \textasciitilde21 hosts (Ch
17's floor-17 screen). The compute/roofline arithmetic of this chapter
answers the per-request and per-host-shard question; the fleet question
is a separate multiplication that Ch 17 performs (with the cost basis in
Ch 16). We keep them distinct because each requires measuring against a
different quantity (a latency budget vs an arrival rate).

\subsection{FLOPs per token}\label{flops-per-token}

For a 70B-parameter dense model in FP16, the per-token forward FLOP
count is approximately:

\[
\text{FLOP/}_{\text{token}} \approx 2 \times N = 2 \times 70 \times 10^9 \approx 140 \text{ GFLOP/token}
\]

{[}DERIVED: 2 FLOPs/parameter × 70 ×10⁹ params; standard dense-forward
arithmetic, which is the same 2×N rule that gives, e.g., a 175B dense
model 2 × 175 × 10⁹ ≈ 0.35 TFLOP/token{]}

This applies to both prefill and decode: each token that enters the
forward pass costs \textasciitilde140 GFLOP. The distinction between
prefill and decode is not in the per-token FLOP count but in the data
movement pattern --- prefill reads the full context once, while decode
re-reads weights per token.

The implementation examples in this paragraph were inspected on
2026-10-28. The 2P estimate (two FLOPs per parameter --- the 2 ×N rule
above) applies to the parameterized matrix work in a specified execution
path. It does not account for all history-dependent attention, indexing,
routing, or repeated-depth computation. A shared core executed several
times reuses weights but repeats arithmetic. A phase-dependent
architecture can also activate different paths during prefill and
decode. The parameter estimate therefore needs a phase and an execution
count before it enters the roofline analysis.
(\href{https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash/resolve/main/DeepSeek_V41_Tech_Report.pdf}{DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
technical report};
\href{https://huggingface.co/tomg-group-umd/huginn-0125/raw/main/README.md}{Huginn
model card} and \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171}{Geiping et al.,
arXiv:2502.05171} for recurrence-aware cache and per-token adaptive
compute). \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}FACT{]}} for the phase-dependent mechanisms; the
execution-count qualification is \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}.

\subsection{A small execution-budget
example}\label{a-small-execution-budget-example}

An illustrative work model makes the distinction precise. Let \texttt{C}
be fixed work outside a repeated core, \texttt{F} the work of one core
pass, and \texttt{r} the executed pass count. Ignore decision overhead
for this first estimate. Total work is \texttt{C\ +\ rF}; the unique
core weight inventory remains unchanged as \texttt{r} changes. If
\texttt{C\ =\ 1} work unit and \texttt{F\ =\ 2} work units, four passes
cost 9 units and three passes cost 7. Omitting one of four passes
removes 25\% of core work but only \texttt{2/9}, approximately 22.2\%,
of total work. The units are illustrative operation-budget units, not
milliseconds, FLOPs measured on a device, or a model benchmark.
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

Selecting pass 3 after executing pass 4 still costs 9 units in this
model. A real stopping check adds overhead, and lower work does not
guarantee proportionally lower wall time. Small batches can underfill
kernels. State movement, synchronization and memory stalls can dominate.
A quality-preserving reduction in actual core executions is necessary
evidence for a compute saving; a selected exit index alone is
insufficient. \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

Communication follows the executed path as well. If each core pass uses
tensor-parallel collectives, repeating the core repeats those
collectives unless a specific implementation changes the schedule.
Weight sharing does not share their results across different hidden
inputs. If MoE routing is also present, expert dispatch adds a separate
traffic term; it cannot be inferred from the number of depth passes
alone. Neither a shared parameter count nor a lower active-parameter
count establishes the communication time. The deployment record must
identify placement, parallel strategy, tensor sizes and the actual
overlap achieved. \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

\subsection{Prefill PFLOP for 9.2K
input}\label{prefill-pflop-for-9.2k-input}

For a single request with 9.2K input tokens, the prefill FLOP cost is:

\[
\text{prefill FLOPs} \approx 2 \times N \times L = 2 \times 70 \times 10^9 \times 9.2 \times 10^3 \approx 1.29 \text{ PFLOP}
\]

{[}DERIVED: 2 × N × L where N=70B, L=9,200; the figure is large because
the full context is attended to, but it is a one-time cost per request,
not a sustained rate{]}

\emph{Accuracy of the 2 ×N × L approximation.} This linear prefill count
omits the quadratic attention term,
\(\approx 4 \times n_\text{layers} \times L^2 \times d\). At the
canonical 9.2K context that correction is small --- roughly
\textbf{+17\%} of the 1.29 PFLOP --- so the 2NL roofline is a sound
teaching baseline there. But the omission grows with context: at 32K the
attention term is \textasciitilde{}\textbf{+60\%} of the linear term and
at 128K it \emph{dominates} (\textasciitilde2.4× the linear term,
≈+240\%). An architect sizing long-context prefill must add the
quadratic term or measure it; the Unknowns section returns to this.

At 10 requests/s, the sustained prefill throughput demand is:

\[
\text{prefill demand} = 1.29 \times 10^{15} \text{ FLOP} \times 10 \text{ rps} \approx 12.9 \text{ PFLOP/s}
\]

\subsection{Sustained vs.~peak: H100
MFU}\label{sustained-vs.-peak-h100-mfu}

NVIDIA H100 SXM5 lists \textasciitilde1,979 TFLOPS FP16 Tensor Core peak
``with sparsity'' and \textasciitilde989 TFLOPS FP16 peak without
sparsity {[}1P: NVIDIA H100 datasheet{]}. In practice, sustained model
FLOPs utilization (MFU) for a well-tuned transformer workload typically
runs at 30--40\% of peak on H100 {[}2°: industry benchmark reports,
derived from real kernel profiles{]}. At 35\% MFU:

\[
\text{sustained} = \text{peak} \times \text{MFU} = 989 \text{ TFLOPS} \times 0.35 \approx 346 \text{ TFLOPS}
\]

\mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}2°{]}}{[}DERIVED: vendor peak (1P) × typical MFU (2°) --- the
MFU range is a secondary, empirical figure, so the sustained value
inherits the provenance of its weakest input, the 2° MFU, while the peak
is first-party{]}

\textbf{Scope matters here.} The 346 TFLOPS figure is the sustained rate
of a \emph{single} H100, and the 2,471 tokens/s derived from it is
therefore a \textbf{per-GPU} number. The canonical host is an
\emph{8×H100} node, so we must not multiply demand by a per-GPU rate as
if it were per-node. Track the scope explicitly as we scale.

Per \textbf{GPU} (one H100 at 35\% MFU):

\[
\text{prefill tokens/s (per GPU)} = \frac{346 \text{ TFLOPS}}{140 \text{ GFLOP/token}} \approx 2{,}471 \text{ tokens/s}
\]

{[}DERIVED: sustained FLOPS ÷ per-token FLOPs; \textbf{per-GPU scope}{]}

Per \textbf{host} (8×H100, idealized before TP communication, imbalance,
and kernel/system overhead):

\[
\text{prefill tokens/s (per 8×H100 host)} = 8 \times 2{,}471 \approx 19{,}800 \text{ tokens/s}
\]

or, equivalently, \texttt{8\ ×\ 346\ TFLOPS\ ≈\ 2.77\ PFLOPS} sustained
÷ 140 GFLOP/token. {[}DERIVED: per-GPU × 8; \textbf{idealized aggregate,
first-order only}{]}

For 10 rps with 9.2K input tokens each, the prefill demand is
\textasciitilde92,000 tokens/s (from the canonical workload). At
\textasciitilde19,800 tokens/s per host, we would need roughly:

\[
n_\text{hosts} = \frac{\text{tokens/s demand}}{\text{tokens/s per host}} = \frac{92{,}000}{19{,}800} \approx 4.6 \to \sim 5 \text{ hosts}
\]

{[}DERIVED: token demand ÷ per-host prefill throughput;
\textbf{idealized aggregate}{]}

At the peak 40 rps (368,000 tokens/s) the same arithmetic gives
\textasciitilde18.6 → \textbf{\textasciitilde19--20 hosts}. This aligns
--- independently --- with the KV-residency / Little's-Law fleet sizing
derived in \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}, with the cost basis in \hyperref[chap:16]{Chapter 16}
(\textasciitilde21 hosts at peak, \textasciitilde\$155,816.93/mo
self-host ≈ \$24.97/1K --- illustrative included-cost, Ch 16), which is
a useful cross-check that the two fundamentally different constraints
(prefill FLOP throughput vs.~KV residency/service time) point at the
same order of fleet.

\textbf{Honest boundary on both numbers.} The per-GPU
\texttt{2,471\ tokens/s} is itself an \emph{analytical} figure at an
assumed 35\% MFU --- not a measured throughput. The \texttt{8×}
aggregate (\texttt{\textasciitilde{}19,800}/host) is a
\textbf{theoretical first-order estimate} that assumes perfect
tensor-parallel scaleout: it does \textbf{not} account for TP
communication, per-rank load imbalance, kernel inefficiency under a
given batch, or scheduling/system overhead. It is an upper-bound
analysis value, not a servable goodput. Actual reachable prefill
throughput must come from benchmark goodput under the target workload
(\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}). Treat both numbers as scope-disciplined \emph{models},
never as measured capacity.

This rough sizing illustrates that prefill is FLOP-bound at this scale
--- the compute requirement is the primary constraint, and memory
bandwidth (HBM at 3.35 TB/s per H100) is sufficient to keep the compute
fed if the kernel is efficient. The ridge point for dense FP16 on H100
is \textasciitilde295 FLOP/byte (989 ÷ 3.35); the arithmetic intensity
of a mature attention kernel sits near or above this ridge, meaning the
kernel is compute-saturated rather than bandwidth-starved once the batch
is large enough.

For decode, the per-token FLOP cost is the same \textasciitilde140
GFLOP, but the arithmetic intensity drops sharply. Each decoded token
re-reads all 70B weights from HBM (a \textbf{batch-1-equivalent}
weight-streaming read). A 70B FP16 weight matrix is 70 ×10⁹ × 2 B = 140
GB --- one full pass reads \textbf{140 GB} (batch-1-equivalent), not 280
GB. (The naïve ``2 ×70 ×10⁹ × 2 B ≈ 280 GB'' double-counts the FP16
footprint: the leading ``2'' is already inside the 2-bytes-per-element,
so writing 2 ×70 ×10⁹ × 2 B counts the weight bytes twice. Any
KV/output-projection reads are a separate, smaller term on top, not a
second full weight footprint.) The effective arithmetic intensity is
therefore:

\[
\text{arithmetic intensity}_{\text{decode}} \approx \frac{140 \text{ GFLOP}}{140 \text{ GB}} \approx 1.0 \text{ FLOP/byte}
\]

{[}DERIVED; a lower-bound bandwidth model --- real kernels also read
activations and KV, so measured intensity is typically below this and
heavily dependent on fusion and cache residency{]}

This is well below the H100 dense-FP16 ridge of \textasciitilde295
FLOP/byte, meaning single-stream decode is overwhelmingly
memory-bandwidth-bound. Throughput improves with batching because the
weight-reread amortizes across tokens --- decode intensity ≈ batch size
(weight-only), so at batch=32 the effective intensity is
\textasciitilde32 FLOP/byte, still \textasciitilde9× below the 295 ridge
(compute-bound decode needs batch ≈ 295, or quantized weights, which
halve bytes and double intensity), and TFLOP/s utilization increases
accordingly. \textbf{KV-read caveat:} the weight-only intensity above
omits the per-sequence KV re-read (\textasciitilde24 GB at the canonical
9.2K context, FP16 full-MHA). Including it, the decode intensity is
(140·B)/(140 + 24·B) FLOP/byte, which \emph{saturates} at
\textasciitilde5.8 FLOP/byte as B→∞ --- far below the 295 ridge. So with
the KV term, decode stays bandwidth-bound at every batch size; the
``batch ≈ 295'' figure is the weight-only first-order estimate (valid
when the KV re-read is small relative to the weight stream, e.g.~short
contexts or KV quantization). This is why continuous-batching systems
(Orca, vLLM) prioritize batch growth: it is the primary lever for
pushing decode arithmetic intensity toward the ridge (on the weight-only
term).

\emph{Scope note on the single-device roofline here:} the 140 GB
weight-read and the per-GPU \textasciitilde3.35 TB/s HBM bandwidth are
single-GPU quantities, so the \textasciitilde1.0 FLOP/byte intensity and
the ``memory-bandwidth-bound'' label above are a \emph{per-kernel /
single-device roofline} argument. It is \textbf{not} a verdict on the
canonical 8×H100 host's actual bandwidth adequacy --- under tensor
parallelism the 140 GB weight matrix is sharded across GPUs, so each GPU
streams only its own shard (\textasciitilde0.70 TB/s against its own
\textasciitilde3.35 TB/s, Ch 6). The classification (low-batch decode is
bandwidth-bound) is shard-invariant because it follows from the low
arithmetic intensity, not from the aggregate weight-read rate exceeding
one GPU's bandwidth.

\subsection{The execution path determines the applicable
roofline}\label{the-execution-path-determines-the-applicable-roofline}

Quantizing stored weights reduces one source of memory traffic, but the
selected kernel determines the arithmetic. In a fused
weight-dequantization path, a kernel reads compressed weights and
converts them for higher-precision multiplication. A native block-scaled
path instead requires compatible low-bit operands, scale layouts and
device instructions. An emulation path may reconstruct higher-precision
weights before GEMM. These paths can load related checkpoint
representations while having different traffic, compute and workspace
costs. Their operation counts and supported peak rates cannot be mixed
in one roofline. \hyperref[fig:7.3]{Fig 7.3} makes this execution gate explicit.
\mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

In the \hyperref[chap:7]{Chapter 7} mixed-weight sensitivity --- a teaching model storing
98\% of parameters in MXFP4 and 2\% in BF16 --- a dense 2N
useful-operation convention and one complete weight stream give about
3.567 FLOP/B at batch one, compared with 1 FLOP/B for BF16 weights. This
is a weight-only analytical intensity, not a 3.567× throughput
prediction. It excludes activation and KV traffic, conversion,
additional scale traffic and communication. At large batch or during
prefill, a GEMM may already be compute-bound; during long-context
decode, attention and KV access can dominate. A lower weight-byte count
therefore changes a candidate bottleneck, not the measured end-to-end
bottleneck by definition. \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

Communication needs a separate ledger. Compressed checkpoint loading can
reduce distribution bytes if compressed files are transferred. It does
not automatically compress activation all-reduces, expert all-to-all
traffic or optimizer communication. Fewer devices might become feasible
after a memory reduction, but that is a new parallelism configuration
with different compute, memory and interconnect limits. Communication
savings cannot be credited before the transmitted tensor types,
placement and collective schedule are known. \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} (The
distinction follows the weight-only scope of
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.17323v2}{GPTQ}.)

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


Three measurable quantities anchor compute sizing:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Per-token FLOP count.} Measure by flops profiling (e.g.~NVIDIA
  Nsight Compute) on the target model and precision. The 2×N rule is a
  useful prior, but actual count varies by layer depth, activation
  choice (SiLU vs.~ReLU), and whether kernel fusion combines matmuls.
\item
  \textbf{Sustained MFU.} Profile TFLOP/s achieved divided by peak
  TFLOP/s for the target hardware and precision. A well-tuned
  transformer on H100 FP16 typically lands in the 30--40\% range; below
  20\% suggests a memory or communication bottleneck that should be
  diagnosed before scaling.
\item
  \textbf{Arithmetic intensity (FLOP/byte).} Compute as achieved FLOPs
  divided by bytes transferred (weights + activations + KV read/write).
  Compare to the hardware ridge point (H100 dense FP16:
  \textasciitilde295 FLOP/byte; H200: \textasciitilde989 TFLOPS ÷ 4.8
  TB/s ≈ 206 FLOP/byte --- H200 keeps H100's GH100 die and its dense
  FP16 peak, trading a bigger/faster HBM3e for bandwidth; GB200: higher
  on both terms (faster dense BF16 peak and \textasciitilde8 TB/s HBM),
  so its BF16 ridge is higher, and its NVFP4 ridge higher again). If
  intensity is below the ridge, the kernel is memory-bound; above,
  compute-bound.
\end{enumerate}

A compute roofline does not include every exposed distributed stall. A
communication kernel can consume SM and HBM resources while appearing to
overlap a matrix multiplication. The joint trace must therefore
distinguish model computation, collective work, exposed waiting and
computation slowed by contention. Peak interconnect bandwidth cannot be
added to peak compute to predict the combined schedule; \hyperref[chap:9]{Chapter 9}
separates their mechanisms and measurement boundaries. \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Assuming 2×N FLOPs/token is exact.} It is a baseline
  derivation; actual per-token FLOPs vary with layer depth, activation
  fusion, and kernel fusion. Profile before quoting.
\item
  \textbf{Treating prefill and decode as having the same FLOP/byte
  profile.} Prefill reads the full context once and is FLOP-bound;
  decode re-reads weights per token and is memory-bound. The
  arithmetic-intensity roofline distinguishes them.
\item
  \textbf{Using peak TFLOP/s as sustained throughput.} Peak includes
  sparsity or BF16/FP8 variants that may not be available in the chosen
  precision. Use the FP16 number without sparsity (≈989 TFLOPS on H100)
  as the baseline for dense compute sizing.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring the batch-size dependence of arithmetic intensity.} A
  single-token decode batch has very low intensity; batching raises it.
  Always state the batch assumption when reporting TFLOP/s or tokens/s.
\item
  \textbf{Confusing H100 sparsity-inclusive peak (1,979 TFLOPS) with
  dense peak (989 TFLOPS).} The 2× sparsity figure appears in vendor
  data sheets but most transformer workloads do not activate sparsity.
  Quote the dense figure unless the kernel is specifically
  sparsity-enabled.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


The compute arithmetic and roofline have direct consequences for system
design:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{If prefill is FLOP-bound} (as it is for 70B+ models with long
  contexts), the primary optimization is batching --- larger prefill
  batches do \textbf{not} remove the per-request attention work (each
  sequence still has its own attention computation); they \emph{improve
  accelerator utilization} by forming larger matrix operations,
  amortizing weight and execution overhead across requests, and raising
  achieved FLOP/s. The relevant lever is the achieved efficiency (MFU)
  of the prefill kernel, not a change to the per-request FLOP count.
  System design should therefore provision for batchable prefill
  (continuous batching, Orca, vLLM continuous batching) as the
  first-order throughput lever.
\item
  \textbf{If decode is memory-bound} (single-stream, low batch), the
  primary optimization is batching --- decode arithmetic intensity ≈
  batch size, so even modest batch sizes (8--16) raise it to
  \textasciitilde8--16 FLOP/byte (batch 16 → \textasciitilde16, still
  \textasciitilde18× below the 295 ridge), improving TFLOP/s utilization
  without reaching compute-bound; compute-bound decode needs batch ≈
  295, or quantized weights, which halve bytes and double intensity. KV
  cache quantization (FP8, int8) also reduces bytes per token, raising
  effective intensity. P/D disaggregation (prefill on GPUs, decode on a
  separate pool) can rebalance: prefill's FLOP demand is served by
  compute-optimized GPUs, while decode's bandwidth demand is served by
  wide-memory GPUs or even CPU offload.
\item
  \textbf{If the workload's intensity crosses the hardware ridge}
  (e.g.~a workload at \textasciitilde250 FLOP/byte is memory-bound on
  H100's \textasciitilde295 ridge but compute-bound on H200, whose FP16
  ridge is \textasciitilde206 FLOP/byte --- 989 TFLOPS ÷ 4.8 TB/s ---
  from its higher HBM3e bandwidth), the same workload may shift from
  memory-bound to compute-bound, changing the optimal hardware choice.
  The shift comes from the \emph{lower} ridge, not from precision: a
  higher ridge moves the boundary right and makes compute-bound harder
  (H200's \textasciitilde412 FLOP/byte ridge in \emph{dense} FP8 ---
  \textasciitilde1.98 PFLOPS ÷ 4.8 TB/s, never the sparsity-inclusive
  \textasciitilde4 PFLOPS (§8.5) --- would do the opposite), and
  precision scaling moves intensity and ridge together (bytes halve →
  intensity doubles; peak doubles → ridge doubles), so FP8 alone does
  not change the classification. This is why the H200 represents an
  inflection point where inference and training hardware lines begin to
  converge {[}1P: NVIDIA H200 SXM datasheet{]}.
\end{itemize}

In practice, the architect measures arithmetic intensity for the target
workload and precision, locates the kernel on the roofline chart, and
then selects hardware and batching strategy accordingly. The roofline is
the diagnostic; the architecture decision follows.

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Arithmetic intensity of fused kernels.} Most roofline
  estimates treat attention or MLP as separate matmuls, but in practice
  frameworks fuse multiple operations into a single kernel. The
  effective FLOP/byte of a fused attention+MLP kernel is not well
  cataloged in primary sources and would require profiling on the target
  hardware to anchor.
\item
  \textbf{MFU across precisions and sparsity.} The 30--40\% H100 FP16
  figure is a rule of thumb; actual utilization depends on architecture,
  kernel quality, and batch config. A systematic MFU atlas across
  H100/H200/GB200, FP16/BF16/FP8, dense/MOE would be a valuable
  reference.
\item
  \textbf{Arithmetic intensity of emerging attention mechanisms.} Linear
  attention (DeltaNet, MLA), compressed attention (CSA, HCA), and hybrid
  sparse patterns have different FLOP profiles and data movement. Their
  roofline position is not yet established in primary sources.
\item
  \textbf{Frontier 2026 has begun to answer this.} DeepSeek-V4's hybrid
  CSA+HCA attention reports that, at 1M-token context, single-token
  inference drops to \textasciitilde27\% of the FLOPs (Pro) /
  \textasciitilde10\% (Flash) and KV cache to \textasciitilde10\% /
  \textasciitilde7\% of DeepSeek-V3.2 {[}1P: arXiv 2606.19348{]}. In its
  \href{https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.3-flash}{2026-08-26 release article},
  Z.ai compares GLM-5.3-Flash with GLM-5.3: it reports 3× less attention
  compute \textbf{per head per layer} and a 4.4× smaller \textbf{average
  BF16 KV cache per layer}. These are normalized attention metrics, not
  whole-model FLOP or total-cache ratios. These are \mbox{{[}1P{]}} first-party
  vendor figures, not yet independently reprofiled on our own hardware
  --- which is exactly the arithmetic-intensity measurement an architect
  should still do before trusting a vendor's roofline claim for their
  own workload.
\item
  \textbf{How quantization shifts the ridge.} Moving from FP16 to
  FP8/int8 changes peak TFLOP/s and bytes-per-operand, shifting the
  ridge point. The net effect depends on the quantization scheme
  (element-wise vs.~per-tensor, dynamic vs.~static) and whether the
  kernel is re-tuned for the lower precision.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: Continuous Deployment
Scenario}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-continuous-deployment-scenario}

An architect is brought into an ongoing deployment of a 70B-class Q\&A
system on 8×H100 GPUs. The system is serving \textasciitilde10
requests/s average with \textasciitilde9.2K input + 300 output tokens
per request, and the TTFT budget is being missed: p95 TTFT is 2.8 s,
exceeding the SLO of 2 s. The TPOT of 28 ms/token is within spec, but
the prefill delay is the bottleneck.

The temptation is to reach for a roofline and ``prove'' prefill is
memory-bound. That reasoning is wrong here, and the error is worth
naming: it is spread by treating prefill like token-by-token decode. In
decode, each new token re-reads the weight matrix from HBM, so the
arithmetic intensity is \textasciitilde1 FLOP/byte --- genuinely
memory-bound. In prefill, the weight matrix is read \emph{once} and
reused across the whole sequence; the sequence dimension supplies the
matrix-matrix reuse. Prefill's effective intensity is
\texttt{(2\ ×\ N\ ×\ L)\ /\ (N\ ×\ 2\ bytes/param)} = \texttt{L} =
\textbf{9,200 FLOP/byte} at the canonical 9.2K context ---
\textasciitilde1.5 orders of magnitude above the \textasciitilde295
FLOP/byte dense-FP16 ridge. At that operating point prefill is
compute-bound, exactly as the rest of this chapter teaches.

So a high TTFT is \emph{not} evidence of a bandwidth problem. The
architect does the correct thing: profile the prefill kernel and
determine which of several distinct causes is actually limiting it,
rather than assuming memory. The profile separates four candidates:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Compute-bound} --- the GEMMs are saturated at high MFU; more
  FLOPs/ops per token would not help, but better kernel efficiency
  (flash-attention, fused MLP) would.
\item
  \textbf{Communication-bound} --- tensor/pipeline parallelism crosses
  the NVLink/Host fabric; data movement, not math, is the ceiling. The
  fix is a different parallelization or a smaller cross-GPU footprint.
\item
  \textbf{Launch / occupancy-limited} --- the kernel is too small or the
  grid under-fills the SMs; increasing batch or improving occupancy
  raises utilization without adding hardware.
\item
  \textbf{Attention / memory constrained (within prefill)} --- the
  quadratic attention term, not the linear weights, grows with context;
  at 128K+ contexts this term dominates prefill cost (at 32K it is
  already \textasciitilde+60\% --- the 4·n\_layers·L²·d term, \hyperref[fig:22.1]{Fig 22.1}),
  so the fix is to reduce the attention term (flash-attention,
  sparse/linear attention), not to batch harder.
\end{itemize}

The architect measures MFU to choose among these. If the prefill kernel
is running at 12\% MFU, the problem is occupancy or launch --- grow the
batch or fuse kernels. If MFU is already near \textasciitilde40\% and
the kernel is compute-saturated, then prefill \emph{is} compute-bound
and more batch just queues more work without reducing per-request TTFT;
the lever instead is kernel efficiency, attention-term control, or P/D
disaggregation so prefill's FLOP profile is served by compute-optimized
GPUs while decode's bandwidth profile runs on wide-memory GPUs.

The recommendation is therefore: profile first (MEASURE, do not assume),
and only then pick the intervention. Batching helps when prefill is
occupancy-limited; kernel fusion helps when GEMMs are under-saturated;
attention-term control and disaggregation help when the bottleneck is
the quadratic attention term or the compute/bandwidth mismatch between
phases. The workload's true roofline position, not an assumed
``memory-bound prefill,'' dictates the fix.
