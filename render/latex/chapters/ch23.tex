\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

After this chapter we should be able to sit with a customer or
stakeholder who says ``we want to build something with AI'' and turn
that vague ambition into a bounded, measurable, defensible scope. We
will apply the book's core loop in a conversational setting: ask the
questions that surface the real workload (Ch 4), the quality bar (Ch
3/5), the latency and cost constraints, and the operational reality ---
then restate the requirement in terms we can architect against. After
this chapter, ``let's go build an AI thing'' becomes ``here are the
bounds, the SLO, and the open questions'' --- and the customer knows
exactly what they asked for.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


Most AI projects fail in requirements, not in technology.
\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}} The root cause is a \textbf{category error}: the
customer and the architect mean different things by the same words.
``Fast,'' ``good,'' ``smart,'' and ``it should just work'' are not
contracts; they are vibes. The architect's first job is to convert vibes
into \textbf{quantified, testable bounds} using the disciplines of the
earlier chapters.

The conversion rests on one idea: most consequential architectural
decisions should trace back to an explicit number or constraint, many of
which the customer can see. Whether the model is 7B or 70B, whether we
self-host or use an API, whether we cache, quantize, or disaggregate ---
all of it is decided by six numbers we can extract from a customer
conversation:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The traffic} --- how many users, at what concurrency, with
  what peak-to-average ratio (Ch 4/Ch 17).
\item
  \textbf{The token profile} --- what inputs look like, how long
  contexts are, the input/output ratio (Ch 4/Ch 6).
\item
  \textbf{The latency/SLO} --- what ``fast'' means, the percentile and
  the budget (Ch 6).
\item
  \textbf{The quality bar} --- what ``good enough'' means on \emph{this}
  task, on \emph{their} data (Ch 3/Ch 5/Ch 14).
\item
  \textbf{The cost ceiling} --- the money they can spend, and whether
  that implies self-host or API (Ch 16).
\item
  \textbf{The operational reality} --- data privacy, geography,
  available staff, time-to-value (Ch 13/Ch 21/Ch 24).
\end{enumerate}

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


The customer conversation is really about \textbf{buying information,
not delivering answers.} We are not in the room to impress; we are in
the room to learn the six numbers. Three mental moves keep the
conversation productive:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The Socratic funnel.} Start broad (``what problem are we
  solving?'') and narrow through a scripted set of questions that each
  commit the customer to a concrete bound. Every ``vague'' answer gets
  followed by a request for magnitude: ``roughly how many users?''
  ``what's the slowest acceptable answer?''
\item
  \textbf{The mirror test.} Restate their words back as a quantified
  requirement and check they still agree. ``So: \textasciitilde2,000
  users, under 2 seconds to first token, on company-internal data ---
  did I get that right?'' The restatement is where scope actually gets
  locked.
\item
  \textbf{The two-window honesty.} Separate what the customer
  \emph{thinks} (goals) from what the \emph{system} can guarantee
  (SLOs). Goals are aspirations; SLOs are contracts. Confusing them
  breeds many fee disputes and scope fights.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


\subsection{The Requirements Dialogue: Turning ``an internal AI
assistant'' Into
Bounds}\label{the-requirements-dialogue-turning-an-internal-ai-assistant-into-bounds}

\emph{(Worked example dialogue --- illustrative, not reference.)} An
internal-platform lead says: ``We want an AI assistant for our sales
teams --- it should answer questions from our product docs and CRM data,
and it should be fast.'' \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} \emph{(Scenario provenance
--- a constructed worked-example dialogue, not a secondary source; the
provenance/status label axes are defined in the Sources chapter.)}

\textbf{Architect:} ``How many people will use it, and how often?''
\textbf{Lead:} ``\textasciitilde2,000 on the platform; maybe 5\% are in
it at once at peaks.'' \textbf{Architect:} ``So \textasciitilde100
concurrent at peak --- that's users with the tool open, not requests in
flight --- and realistically only a fraction of those are firing
requests at any moment. Roughly how many questions per second at the
busiest minute?'' \textbf{Lead:} ``If everyone on the sales floor asked
at once, maybe 40 a second.'' \textbf{Architect:} ``And an average
minute, not the busiest --- roughly how many?'' \textbf{Lead:} ``Maybe
10 a second on a normal day, with bursts around launches.'' \textbf{→
Bound 1: \textasciitilde2,000 users, \textasciitilde10 rps average,
\textasciitilde40 rps peak.} \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}} (Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2})

\textbf{Architect:} ``What does a question look like? Short or with long
context?'' \textbf{Lead:} ``They paste product specs and contracts, so
the prompt can be lengthy --- a few pages.'' \textbf{Architect:} ``How
many pages in a typical prompt, how many retrieved chunks, and how long
is the answer you'd send back?'' \textbf{Lead:} ``Specs run two to three
pages, maybe a handful of retrieved sections; the answer is a paragraph
or two.'' \textbf{→ Bound 2: long \textasciitilde9,200-token inputs with
retrieval, \textasciitilde300-token outputs.} (A page of prose is
roughly 500--700 tokens, so two to three pages plus retrieved sections
lands in the low thousands; the exact 9,200/300 split is the canonical
scenario imported from Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2} rather than elicited here, and
the pilot's tokenizer run replaces it.) \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}} (Ch 4,
\hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2})

\textbf{Architect:} ``What's `fast'? First-word delay, or time to full
answer?'' \textbf{Lead:} ``Feels slow if we wait more than two seconds
to see something start.'' \textbf{→ Bound 3: TTFT ≤ 2 s (p95),
\textasciitilde25 ms/token thereafter (mean decode budget; p95 TPOT ≤ 35
ms, Ch 4).} \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}} (Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2})

\textbf{Architect:} ``What does `good answer' mean for your team --- and
can we define a handful of sample questions to test against?''
\textbf{Lead:} ``It should be accurate on our docs and not make up
contract terms. We can give the validation team 50 real questions.''
\textbf{→ Bound 4: a 50-question retrieval-QA quality benchmark on their
actual data.} \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}

\textbf{Architect:} ``Data sensitivity --- can this touch a public
cloud, and how much can it cost?'' \textbf{Lead:} ``It's internal and
regulated; keep it on-prem if at all possible. And at this scale we'd
rather not pay per-token API fees --- keep it within a self-host
budget.'' \textbf{→ Bound 5: the regulated-data constraint makes the
local/server tier the preferred path --- on-prem is preferred ``if at
all possible,'' with cloud as the fallback if that preference yields (Ch
13).} \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} \textbf{→ Bound 6: cost ceiling
--- within a self-host budget (Ch 16).}
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}

\textbf{Architect (mirror):} ``So to lock it: \textasciitilde2,000 sales
users, \textasciitilde10 rps average / 40 peak, prompts with
\textasciitilde9,200-token retrieval context, answers starting inside
p95 TTFT ≤ 2 s, quality judged on 50 of your real questions, on-prem if
at all possible (cloud as the fallback if that preference yields)
because the data is regulated, and within a self-host budget. Have I
captured it?'' \textbf{Lead:} ``Yes --- that's exactly it.''

\textbf{Reading the result.} The vague ask collapsed into the exact
canonical workload this book has architected end-to-end --- a 70B-class
model (the candidate tier the quality bound points to, pending the
50-question benchmark --- Ch 5's selection rule is to pick the smallest
tier that clears the quality bar) served on 8×H100 hosts, a
\textasciitilde21--32-host fleet at the canonical peak (Ch 17/20/24) ---
\emph{becomes a candidate architecture} whose memory fit can be derived
and whose latency, throughput, quality, and peak-load behavior must then
be validated (Ch 2--16 derive the constraints; the deployment has to be
benchmarked and measured against them, as Ch 24's red-team pass shows).
The conversation did not sell a model; it \emph{exposed the numbers}
that then narrowed the candidate set, and left the actual validation to
the measurement phase. \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} \emph{(worked
example, not reference)}

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


The requirements dialogue has its own quality metrics:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bound coverage} --- did the conversation surface all six
  numbers (traffic, tokens, SLO, quality, cost, ops)? Missing one is a
  future scope surprise.
\item
  \textbf{Customer buy-in} --- did the mirrored restatement get an
  explicit ``yes''? An unconfirmed restatement is not a scope.
\item
  \textbf{Decision lead time} --- how quickly the six bounds let us
  produce a defensible draft architecture. The whole point is that
  extracting numbers, not more meetings, unlocks the design.
\item
  \textbf{Assumption log} --- every guess we made that the customer did
  not explicitly confirm, tracked as \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}} items, not silent
  defaults.
\end{enumerate}

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Selling before scoping.} Pitching a model or a price before
  extracting the six numbers commits us to an answer we cannot yet
  defend.
\item
  \textbf{Accepting aspirations as SLOs.} ``Make it fast'' is a goal;
  ``p95 TTFT under 2 s'' is a contract. Write the contract.
\item
  \textbf{Letting the customer choose the technology.} ``We want a 70B
  model'' is a solution, not a requirement; the architect derives the
  model from the bounds (Ch 5), not from the customer's guess.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring the operational constraint.} ``It has to be on-prem /
  private / no cloud'' often decides more than any performance number
  --- surface it early.
\item
  \textbf{Not logging assumptions.} Every silence becomes a hidden
  assumption that surfaces as a dispute later. Write them down as
  \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}} items.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


The requirements conversation is where the book's entire loop is
\emph{fed.} The six bounds it extracts are exactly the inputs Ch 1--17
consume: traffic feeds capacity (Ch 17), tokens feed KV/memory (Ch 7),
quality feeds model selection (Ch 5), latency feeds serving (Ch 11),
cost feeds TCO (Ch 16), and the operational constraint picks the tier
(Ch 13). A good requirements session is not a warm-up to architecture
--- it \emph{is} the first chapter of the architecture, and every
downstream decision traces back to the numbers it locked.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{How accurate the customer's own-told numbers are} (``40 rps
  peak'') is \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}}; peak estimates are notoriously optimistic
  and should be triangulated with real logs where possible.
\item
  \textbf{Whether the quality bar once defined stays stable} is
  \mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}; as users adopt the tool, their expectations and the
  data they query both shift.
\item
  \textbf{The right number of questions to lock quality} is
  \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}}; 50 worked here as a representative proxy but a
  larger, task-distributed set is stronger.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: The `Next Month' Customer Portal:
Requirements
Dialogue}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-the-next-month-customer-portal-requirements-dialogue}

A CTO emails the architect: ``We heard LLMs are great --- let's put one
on our customer portal to answer support tickets. Shipping it next
month.'' Rather than confirm the launch date, the architect books a
30-minute session and runs the funnel. It emerges the portal gets
\textasciitilde5 rps average but 200-rps bursts during outage events
(traffic), tickets are short (\textasciitilde500 tokens, not
retrieval-heavy), quality means ``resolve the ticket, not just answer''
(a capability that rules out an answer-only model --- in this scenario
the architect puts 7B-class forward as the candidate to test first, on
the Ch 5 rule of the smallest tier that clears the quality bar, with the
pilot's pass criterion stated as \textasciitilde60\% of baseline tickets
resolved without escalation (illustrative threshold, set with the
customer before the pilot); the short \textasciitilde500-token tickets
keep the KV footprint and per-host memory small, which is a memory
argument for the smaller tier, not a capability one), the SLO is 3 s
because users are already frustrated (latency), and --- critically ---
the portal sits on regulated payment data so nothing leaves the premises
(ops). The mirrored restatement turns ``let's ship AI next month'' into
``a 7B-class model on one on-prem GPU, resolving baseline tickets under
3 s, with a 200-rps burst absorbed by a queue-and-drain buffer over that
one GPU --- whose \textasciitilde21--28 rps total rate drains such a
burst in \textasciitilde6--8.5 s, so the burst is a best-effort
degraded-mode path, not a 3 s path --- quality-gated on real resolved
tickets --- and a two-week pilot on 5\% of tickets to confirm the
resolution quality before full rollout.''

\textbf{The burst buffer, spelled out.} The buffer is an arrival-rate
buffer: bursts of duration D queue and drain at rate r, a decode-only
ceiling of 100\% MFU (≤141 rps --- 7B FP16, \textasciitilde500 output
tokens/request, H100); with the \textasciitilde500-token prefill
included the \emph{total} request rate is \textasciitilde71 rps at 100\%
MFU (\textasciitilde21--28 rps at 30--40\% MFU). The worked example
below therefore uses r = 25 rps --- an optimistic total-rate
illustration (\textasciitilde35\% MFU, the top of that band, and still
well below the \textasciitilde71 rps total ceiling) --- and the measured
per-host rate must replace it before the burst budget is trusted. Max
queue wait ≈ D·(200−r)/r must stay inside the 3 s SLO --- so state D,
the buffer depth D·(200−r) requests, and choose: (a) add capacity, (b)
add admission control, or (c) scope the SLO to non-burst traffic.
Worked: D = 1 s at 200 rps → 200 requests arrive, 25 served during the
burst; queue depth at burst end = (200−25) × 1 = 175 requests; drained
at 25 rps in 175/25 = 7 s; max queue wait = 7 s, so the last-arriving
request completes at 7 s plus its own service time --- far past the 3 s
SLO; total time from burst start = 8 s (1 s burst + 7 s drain). The
whole 21--28 rps band fails the same test (at 28 rps the drain is 6.1 s
and the total 7.1 s), so burst-window requests miss the 3 s SLO and the
burst runs under a separate queue-and-drain degraded-mode SLO (option
c).

\textbf{Architect (mirror, closing):} ``So what we've locked: 3 s p95
SLO on non-burst traffic; above \textasciitilde25 rps --- the single
GPU's total-rate ceiling --- the burst is served under the
queue-and-drain degraded mode, with admission control capping the queue
at the depth the 3 s drain allows, and that degraded-mode target is
written into the contract as a separate SLO. Sign off on that split?''
\textbf{CTO:} ``Yes --- non-burst 3 s, bursts best-effort with the queue
visible. That's the scope.''

The customer's vague excitement became a defensible, bounded, shippable
scope --- the 3 s SLO scoped to non-burst traffic, the burst served
under the queue-and-drain degraded mode --- and the architect is already
architecting, not guessing.
