\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

As a RAG Q\&A workload scales from a single host to a fleet of serving
nodes, the architect must confront a new set of trade-offs: how does
aggregate throughput scale with added hardware, how does scheduling
latency affect user-perceived latency, and at what point does
autoscaling cease to be cost-effective? This chapter addresses these
questions through concrete arithmetic, not hand-waving.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


Fleet-level optimization treats the entire collection of serving hosts
as a single resource pool rather than independent islands. The central
insight is that aggregate system throughput is not merely the sum of
individual host throughputs; it is modulated by scheduling overhead,
network contention, and the cost of moving data between hosts and
storage. Two cost effects must be kept distinct. The \textbf{average}
cost per query declines only along a stated load path: as served demand
grows and fixed overhead (model loading, infrastructure, staff) is
spread across more SLO-good requests, cost per request may decline until
additional capacity steps dominate; adding idle hosts at fixed demand
increases unit cost. The \textbf{marginal} cost of each added host,
however, is roughly constant in this model --- one host buys
\textasciitilde2.0 req/s (17/8.6 = 1.977, \mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}) at
\textasciitilde\$20/hr, so the marginal cost stays near
\textasciitilde\$10 per req/s per hour (Ch 17 §7 frames the Nth host as
diminishing marginal \emph{benefit}, not falling marginal cost). The
economic lever is amortizing the fixed term, not the marginal one.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}

The key concepts are:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Aggregate throughput}: The total queries-per-second the fleet
  can sustain before latency SLA violations.
\item
  \textbf{Scheduling overhead}: The per-request cost of dispatching a
  query to an available host, including placement decisions and data
  movement.
\item
  \textbf{Autoscaling break-even}: The point at which growth in SLO-good
  requests covers the next host's fixed cost --- \emph{average} cost per
  query falls as fixed costs amortize, while marginal cost per query
  stays roughly constant (§2).
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


The mental model for fleet optimization replaces the single-host query
pipeline with a two-level hierarchy. At the lower level, each host
processes queries using its GPU/CPU pipeline. At the upper level, a
scheduler routes incoming requests across hosts, handling load
balancing, model parallelism where needed, and graceful degradation
under overload.

The scheduler's decisions have direct impact on the tail of the latency
distribution. Poor placement can cause some hosts to saturate while
others remain underutilized, inflating the 99th-percentile latency even
when aggregate throughput is ample. Conversely, intelligent
scheduling---such as consistent hashing for session affinity or
predictive placement based on historical patterns---keeps utilization
flat across the fleet and minimizes tail latency.

A critical assumption in this model is that the workload follows a known
arrival process. For the canonical scenario (Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}):
\textasciitilde10 rps average and \textasciitilde40 rps peak. With
\textasciitilde9,200 in-tokens and \textasciitilde300 out-tokens per
query, a \textasciitilde8.6 s service time per request (1.08 s prefill +
\textasciitilde7.5 s decode), and a 70B dense FP16 model running on
8×H100 (640 GB total HBM3), the fleet must sustain
\textbf{\textasciitilde40 requests/s at peak}, which --- by Little's law
--- corresponds to \textbf{λ·W ≈ 40 × 8.6 ≈ 344 requests in flight} (W
here is Ch 17's assumed mean KV-residency time E{[}W\_KV-resident{]} ≈
8.6 s, a conditional scenario proxy --- not the end-to-end W\_e2e, which
adds queue, tool and inter-turn time that, under the stated assumption,
holds no KV), not merely the \textasciitilde100 concurrent \emph{users}
or the nominal 40 rps. The distinction between ``concurrent users'' (a
population), the arrival rate (rps), and in-flight requests (λ·W) is
exactly what \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17} develops; it is the difference between sizing
for a population and sizing a fleet against a latency bound.

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


Take a fleet of N hosts, each with 8×H100 running a single 70B FP16
model instance. Two capacities must be reconciled ---
\textbf{throughput} (tokens/s) and \textbf{KV residency} (concurrent
requests) --- and neither can be reduced to a single ``rps'' constant,
because rps is workload-specific (per-request input/output tokens,
context length, batch).

\textbf{Latency floor first, then reconcile the two capacity bounds.} A
300-output-token request at a \textasciitilde25 ms/token decode rate
(\hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8}) takes \textasciitilde7.5 s of generation alone, plus 1.08 s
prefill, so the service time is \textasciitilde8.6 s per request (before
tool calls). Two separate ceilings bound how many requests a host can
\emph{serve per second}, and they must be reconciled --- they are not
interchangeable:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Throughput bound (tokens/s).} A host sustains an illustrative
  \textasciitilde35,000 tokens/s of \emph{total} (prefill+decode)
  serving throughput --- the conservative low end of the
  \textasciitilde35,000--56,000 tokens/s range in \hyperref[chap:15]{Chapter 15}
  \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} achievable range --- distinct from the
  \textasciitilde19,800 tok/s \emph{prefill-only} ideal in \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8} (8
  × 2,471 at 35\% MFU), which counts prefill compute alone.
  \textbf{These two are not directly comparable as single numbers:
  \textasciitilde35,000 is a phase-mixed \emph{prefill+decode} total and
  \textasciitilde19,800 is a \emph{prefill-only} figure. To compare them
  one must first phase-resolve both, then compare prefill with prefill
  (the presented workload's prefill leg is 2.0 req/s × 9,200 = 18,400
  input tok/s) and decode with decode (2.0 × 300 = 600 output tok/s);
  aggregating the two phases into one ``total'' rate requires an
  explicit cost/phase-equivalence model.} Dividing the total by the
  \textasciitilde9,500 tokens/request gives \textasciitilde3.7
  requests/s --- \textbf{but this is not achievable here}, because it
  would require \textasciitilde3.7 × 8.6 ≈ 32 concurrent requests in
  flight, roughly double the host's KV-residency ceiling (C ≈ 17.5,
  conservative integer floor 17, \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}). The token-throughput
  figure is thus a non-binding upper edge for this canonical workload.
\item
  \textbf{KV-residency / latency bound (Little's law).} The real ceiling
  is set by concurrency and KV-holding time together: a host holds 17
  whole concurrent full-context requests (the \textasciitilde17.5 KV
  ceiling, \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}, floored to whole contexts --- no fractional
  admission) each for an assumed mean KV residency E{[}W\_KV-resident{]}
  ≈ 8.6 s, so C/W = 17 / 8.6 = 1.977 ≈ \textbf{2.0 requests/s}
  \mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}} at steady state --- a \textbf{KV-slot-based
  analytical UPPER bound on admitted request completion rate,
  conditional on an assumed mean KV residency time}.
\end{itemize}

The binding per-host rate is the \emph{minimum} of the two --- here the
KV/latency bound. This is exactly why a token-throughput number must not
be divided by tokens-per-request to manufacture an ``rps'' here: the
units do cancel (tokens/s ÷ tokens/request = requests/s), but the
numerator is a phase-mixed prefill+decode total with no
cost/phase-equivalence model, and the \textasciitilde3.7 req/s answer
would need \textasciitilde32 concurrent requests against the 17-slot
host capacity. The per-host request rate is a \emph{concurrency ×
service-time} quantity --- what makes the division wrong here is what
its numerator mixes and its answer assumes, not its units.

\textbf{Call this quantity what it is --- one phrase, used identically
in Chapters 16, 17 and 20.} The \textasciitilde2.0 req/s figure is a
\textbf{KV-slot-based analytical UPPER bound on admitted request
completion rate, conditional on an assumed mean KV residency time} --- a
necessary capacity screen: not a measured serving throughput, not a
lower guaranteed rate, and not an SLO goodput. The \emph{lower} bound on
host count derives FROM this upper bound on the per-host rate (⌈λ/(C/W)⌉
= ⌈λ·W/C⌉); it is never a lower bound on the rate itself. It is
achievable only if serving C concurrent sequences does \emph{not}
inflate W beyond the assumed \textasciitilde8.6 s, and neither a
compute, bandwidth, nor scheduler constraint binds first. In real
continuous batching, W is a function of batch occupancy, scheduler
policy, prefill interference, decode batching, the arrival process,
tensor-parallel communication, and the context distribution --- so C and
W are not always independent constants. Treat \textasciitilde2.0 req/s
as an upper-bound model, and require benchmarked goodput (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14})
under the target arrival process for actual fleet sizing.
\textbf{Planning-bound caveat:} like the \textasciitilde19,800 prefill
tok/s/host figure (Ch. 8, idealized prefill only), the
\textasciitilde2.0 req/s/host figure is the \emph{KV-slot-based
analytical UPPER bound on admitted request completion rate, conditional
on an assumed mean KV residency time} --- model-derived, \textbf{not an
implied deployed rate}. \textbf{No p95/p99 performance guarantee has
been shown} for any of the host counts in this chapter; the p95/p99 tail
latency and achieved goodput must be measured on the target deployment
(\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}) before the 21/22-host sizing (21 = the KV/Little screen
under case (i); 22 = the ρ ≈ 0.95 derate of that screen --- and,
independently, the full-square case (ii) prefill compute screen, which
also gives 22, with 34 at the 70\% × 95\% + N−1 policy against 32 for
KV-only --- Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}, u and ρ independent) is treated as
validated.

\textbf{Simultaneous feasibility check (prefill + decode coexistence).}
The \textasciitilde2.0 req/s/host bound assumes prefill and decode run
on the same host at the same time. Evaluated at the displayed
\textasciitilde2.0 rps --- a deliberate stress screen, slightly above
the unrounded 17/8.6 = 1.977 --- the prefill demand is 2.0 × 9,200 =
\textbf{18,400 input tokens/s per host} (§3). The idealized prefill
ceiling is 8 × 0.989 PF/s × 35\% / (2 × 70e9) ≈ \textbf{19,780 tokens/s}
(\hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8}, 35\% MFU, prefill-only --- \textbf{case (i), linear-only
FLOP convention}). The ratio is 18,400 ÷ 19,780 ≈ \textbf{93\%} under
case (i); under case (ii) (full-square, ×1.1723) the ceiling falls to
≈16,870 linear-equivalent tok/s and the ratio becomes 18,400 ÷ 16,870 ≈
\textbf{109\%} (the same demand ÷ ceiling in PFLOP/s: 2 × 1.5099 ÷
2.7692, with 2.7692 PFLOP/s the \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8} 35\%-MFU host peak), and under
case (iii) (causal-triangle, ×1.0861), ceiling ≈18,210 tok/s, 18,400 ÷
18,210 ≈\textbf{101\%} --- above 100\% the prefill leg alone does not
fit at 2 req/s --- so the \textasciitilde93\% headroom is a property of
the linear-only convention, not of the hardware. Prefill must run at
\textasciitilde93\% of the idealized prefill ceiling under case (i).
This is \textbf{\mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}} (Axis-3 measurement status; defined
with the evidence taxonomy in the Sources, Method, and Reproducibility
chapter) --- a prefill-load check, not a measured rate. The
\textasciitilde93\% leaves very little headroom before decode
coexistence, interference, and the actual achieved MFU. Prefill and
decode share the same hardware. Batch scheduling and decode interleaving
compete for the same compute. The achieved MFU is below the 35\% ideal.
Therefore \textasciitilde2.0 req/s/host is an \textbf{analytical upper
bound}, \textbf{NOT a claim that a host serves 2 req/s at SLO}. Before
treating 2 req/s/host as deployable, measure the p95 TTFT, p95 TPOT, and
SLO-goodput on a representative host (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}).

\textbf{Fleet aggregate throughput (illustrative form).} Keeping the
illustrative per-host rps = Q for the algebra, aggregate throughput is

\[
\Lambda_\text{fleet} = N \times Q
\]

At the canonical peak of 40 rps, fleet size must satisfy \textbf{both}
the KV-residency and the throughput/latency bounds \emph{and whichever
binds first}. With the per-host rate at \textasciitilde2.0 req/s (17/8.6
= 1.977, the binding KV-slot upper bound on admitted rate above ---
whose \emph{reciprocal} gives the lower bound on host count), a fleet
for 40 rps starts at \textbf{21 hosts} on the Little-bound --- ⌈40 × 8.6
/ 17⌉ = ⌈344/17⌉ = ⌈20.24⌉ = 21. The KV-residency bound coincides here
--- by Little's law, the in-flight count at 40 rps is λ·W = 40 × 8.6 ≈
344, and ⌈344/17⌉ = \textbf{21 hosts} (17 whole contexts per host, no
fractional admission) --- so the Little and KV-residency formulations
\emph{agree} at the peak: they are the same identity in two views (λW/C
= λ/(C/W)), both giving 21, exactly as \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17} notes --- while the
token-throughput ceiling (⌈40 / 3.684⌉ = 11 hosts) stays non-binding
here; the ρ ≈ 0.95 fractional-throughput derate raises the requirement
to 344 / (0.95 × 17) = 344/16.15 ≈ 21.3 → \textbf{22 hosts}. Separately,
the independent prefill compute screen (Ch. 8, 35\% MFU) gives
\textbf{19 hosts under case (i)} (⌈18.60⌉, linear-only), \textbf{21
under case (iii)} (⌈20.21⌉, causal-triangle) and \textbf{22 under case
(ii)} (⌈21.81⌉, full-square conservative) --- note that case (ii)'s 22
is a \emph{different} 22 from the ρ-derate's 22 (one comes from a
FLOP-counting case, the other from a throughput-efficiency derate of the
KV screen); they coincide in value only by arithmetic accident. The
``far fewer hosts (\textasciitilde6)'' figure applies only to the 10-rps
average (⌈86/17⌉ = 6), not the 40-rps peak. The correct statement is:
the fleet must satisfy every named screen, and the \emph{larger}
requirement wins --- \textbf{21 hosts under case (i), 22 under case
(ii)}; with the standalone ρ = 0.95 derate applied to the prefill screen
exactly as the policy arithmetic applies it, case (ii) rises to ⌈21.81 /
0.95⌉ = \textbf{23 hosts} --- the largest requirement before the full
policy (ρ = 0.95 is a throughput-efficiency derate: dividing the host
requirement by ρ is the same as dividing each host's effective rate by
ρ, so it applies to any throughput-bound screen --- KV or prefill ---
and does not re-derive the 35\% MFU assumption itself); and at the 70\%
× 95\% + N−1 policy \textbf{34 under case (ii) versus 32 for KV-only}
(⌈21.81/0.665⌉+1 = 34; ⌈344/11.305⌉+1 = 32 --- Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}, u =
0.70 and ρ = 0.95 applied as two independent factors); \textbf{all of
19/21/22/23/32/34 are illustrative planning screens under their stated
FLOP/MFU assumptions, not measured SLO-goodput.} \textbf{Reading \hyperref[fig:20.1]{Fig 20.1}'s two gaps:} below saturation the \textasciitilde5\% gap between
C\_analytic(N) = N × 17/8.6 and C\_eff(N) = 0.95 × C\_analytic is the
assumed throughput-efficiency derate; above saturation C\_eff − S, with
S = min(40, C\_eff), is \emph{unused} capacity from insufficient offered
demand --- not scheduling overhead. Queueing and tail-latency penalties
are not determined by this two-line calculation and must be shown
separately if claimed.

\textbf{Scheduling overhead.} A lightweight round-robin scheduler adds
\textasciitilde0.5 ms of dispatch latency per request \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}
--- negligible next to a real 7.5 s decode, but it must stay under
\textasciitilde5\% of the \emph{measured} per-request time.

\textbf{Autoscaling economics.} Each 8×H100 host costs
\textasciitilde\$20/hour (cloud, \$2.50/GPU-hr canonical) or
\textasciitilde\$8/hour amortized (owned) --- \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}. The
marginal cost per additional rps is \$20 ÷ (rps gained per host); the
economic lesson (diminishing cost of a marginal host once fixed costs
dominate) is sound, but the exact per-rps figure is only meaningful
after the real per-host throughput is benchmarked.

Measurement methodology: use a distributed load generator (e.g., Locust
or a custom gRPC-driven pump) that sends queries at the target rps while
instrumenting each host's internal metrics---GPU occupancy, kernel
launch time, scheduler dispatch timestamp, and queue depth. Aggregate
these via a time-series database (InfluxDB or Prometheus) and compute
the above metrics over sliding 1-minute windows.

A practical pitfall: measuring only aggregate rps without tracking
per-host variance can mask saturation in individual nodes. Always report
both the fleet-wide and per-host metrics.

Placement also determines the communication and failure domain. A slow
or failed participant can delay an entire model-parallel group while
other replicas remain healthy. Group-level completion tails,
asynchronous failures and the affected requests therefore accompany
per-host utilization. A placement that improves average utilization but
increases cross-group contention can reduce SLO-goodput; replica
isolation and group recovery require measurement before they become
fleet-capacity assumptions.

The qualification gate in \hyperref[chap:18]{Chapter 18} applies before a configuration
enters any capacity pool. A fallback route consumes capacity too; its
quality, deadline and data-policy constraints remain part of the
placement contract.

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Treating hosts as independent}: Optimizing each host in
  isolation (e.g., tuning batch size per host) ignores the scheduling
  overhead that becomes dominant when the fleet is viewed as a unit. A
  suboptimal batch size on one host can increase dispatch frequency for
  the entire fleet.
\item
  \textbf{Overlooking data movement cost}: In RAG workloads, the
  retrieved-context portion of the \textasciitilde9,200 in-tokens per
  query must be fetched from a vector database or cache (the
  \textasciitilde300 out-tokens are generated locally; only their egress
  crosses the network). If every host pulls data independently, the
  network cost scales linearly with fleet size. A better approach is to
  colocate vector stores with serving hosts or use a shared cache layer
  that amortizes fetch cost across concurrent queries.
\item
  \textbf{Autoscaling too aggressively}: Adding hosts at the first sign
  of latency increase can be suboptimal if the bottleneck is scheduler
  throughput or cache contention, not compute capacity. Measure the
  marginal cost of adding a host before triggering a scale-out event.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring cold-start cost}: When a host is freshly provisioned
  or re-warmed after idle, loading the full model weights into HBM takes
  30--120 seconds \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} (a load-path assumption for a
  \textasciitilde140 GB model over a \textasciitilde10--40 Gb/s
  (≈1.25--5 GB/s) path --- 140 GB ÷ 30--120 s ≈ 4.7--1.2 GB/s,
  i.e.~≈37--9 Gb/s --- not a hardware benchmark; distinct from the much
  shorter KV-cache warm-up of \textasciitilde2 s discussed in \hyperref[chap:15]{Chapter 15}, which re-populates only the retained KV state). Autoscaling
  policies must account for this weight-load warm-up window when scaling
  out a fresh host; otherwise the fleet experiences a spike in error
  rate or timeout during scale-out events.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


Fleet-level optimization reshapes the serving architecture in three
ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Centralized scheduler}: A lightweight service (e.g., based on
  etcd or a custom placement engine) maintains real-time view of
  per-host utilization and makes dispatch decisions. This service must
  be highly available, and its dispatch overhead should stay a small
  fraction of the per-request SLO --- for the canonical
  \textasciitilde8.6 s service time, a sub-millisecond decision is a
  comfortable \emph{target} (the round-robin baseline adds
  \textasciitilde0.5 ms, §3), not a universal necessity: the binding
  constraint is that dispatch overhead not consume a meaningful share of
  the request/turn SLO, which depends on routing frequency and
  scheduling architecture.
\item
  \textbf{Data colocation}: Vector indexes and model weights are stored
  close to the hosts that consume them. This may mean running a local
  Qdrant or Milvus instance per host, or using a shared object store
  (e.g., MinIO) with read-replicas per availability zone. The goal is to
  minimize the distance---measured in network hops---between the
  scheduler's placement decision and the data fetch. But colocation is a
  trade, not a free win: a per-host index multiplies memory/storage,
  consistency, ingestion, and operational cost across the fleet, and
  model-weight locality is a different problem from vector-index
  locality. The right placement depends on the workload --- compare
  centralized retrieval, AZ-local retrieval, replicated indexes, and
  cached results, and identify the traffic/latency point at which
  colocation's access-latency saving outweighs its replication cost. Do
  not conflate the two locality questions.
\item
  \textbf{Graceful degradation}: The fleet should have a mode where,
  under extreme overload, it serves stale or truncated answers rather
  than failing entirely. This could mean returning the top-k results
  from a cached embedding rather than recomputing, or surfacing a ``try
  again'' message with an estimated wait time. The architectural
  contract must make this trade-off explicit so product teams can set
  appropriate SLOs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The useful completion is an accepted
clip}\label{the-useful-completion-is-an-accepted-clip}

The same accepted-completion goodput accounting applies to any
fixed-output-contract workload; a video-generation fleet is the clearest
parallel beyond RAG. For a fixed output contract, a video fleet can
report completed clips/s and generated frames/s together. Frames/s alone
is ambiguous: larger frame counts can increase reported output work
while reducing completed jobs. Across changing durations and frame
rates, generated clip-seconds/s also needs a stated denominator and does
not normalize the nonlinear attention cost.

A useful goodput definition counts clips that satisfy the completion
deadline, output-integrity checks, quality gate, and safety requirements
in the same observation window. Rejections, OOMs, cancellations,
retries, and unusable outputs remain visible as separate counters. The
cost per accepted clip divides the total allocated cost of that
window---including idle capacity and failed attempts---by the
accepted-clip count. Storage, delivery, encoding, and license costs
enter explicitly rather than disappearing into a GPU-seconds estimate.
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} proposed service accounting.

If no clips pass, accepted-clip goodput is zero and cost per accepted
clip is undefined; the report retains the total cost and rejection
counters rather than reporting zero unit cost.

Shape-specific measured service time and peak memory determine
placement. Warm replicas trade idle cost for avoiding checkpoint
downloads, initialization, compilation, and encoder loading on the
request path. Heterogeneous pools can separate preview, denoising, and
reconstruction contracts where measurement justifies the transfer.
Replicas add independent job capacity; sequence-parallel groups can make
a larger job feasible or reduce its service time at a communication
cost. These are different scaling actions. The existing distinction
between analytical capacity bounds and measured SLO-goodput remains
unchanged.

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


Several open questions remain at the fleet level:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Heterogeneous hardware}: What happens when hosts mix H100,
  A100, and CPU-only nodes? The scheduler must handle different
  throughput ceilings and memory capacities, and the aggregate
  throughput calculation becomes a weighted sum rather than a simple
  multiple. Real-world measurements of mixed-device fleets are scarce;
  most published benchmarking uses homogeneous hardware, so the
  crossover point where heterogeneity hurts versus helps is not well
  quantified.
\item
  \textbf{Multi-model serving}: When multiple model sizes coexist (e.g.,
  70B for complex queries, 7B for quick ones), the scheduling problem
  gains a new dimension---placement must balance model size against
  query urgency and host memory. Mixed-precision serving (FP16, FP8,
  INT4) adds another layer: lower precision increases throughput but may
  degrade answer quality on subtle reasoning tasks, and the
  quality-throughput trade-off curve is model-specific.
\item
  \textbf{Economic auto-scaling}: The break-even point between owning
  hardware versus cloud-on-demand depends on utilization patterns that
  are workload-specific. A fleet that runs at 20\% utilization on
  average may be cheaper in the cloud; one at 80\% utilization favors
  owned hardware. The transition point is a function of capital
  expenditure amortization period, electricity cost, and the volatility
  of user demand. Published TCO analyses typically assume steady
  utilization, but bursty workloads with short-lived spikes can make
  ownership risky unless over-provisioned.
\item
  \textbf{Tail-latency arbitration}: When two queries compete for the
  same GPU HBM bandwidth, the 99th-percentile latency can spike
  disproportionately. We lack a general-purpose scheduler policy that
  guarantees a target tail latency under arbitrary arrival patterns;
  this remains an area of active research. Some systems use priority
  queues or token buckets to cap per-query bandwidth, but these
  mechanisms add scheduling complexity and can increase median latency.
\item
  \textbf{Cross-query caching effects}: In RAG workloads, the
  \textasciitilde9,200 in-tokens and \textasciitilde300 out-tokens per
  query overlap significantly across users asking related questions. The
  potential for caching intermediate embeddings or partial KV caches is
  substantial, but the cost of cache management (invalidation,
  coherence, metadata overhead) must be quantified against the compute
  savings. We lack large-scale measurements of caching efficiency across
  diverse query populations.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: Aggregate rps, Marginal Cost, and an
Infeasible
SLO}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-aggregate-rps-marginal-cost-and-an-infeasible-slo}

\textbf{Scenario}: A RAG Q\&A fleet serves 2,000 users with the
canonical parameters: 5\% concurrent, 10 rps average / 40 rps peak,
\textasciitilde9,200 in-tokens + \textasciitilde300 out-tokens per
query, 70B dense FP16 model, 8×H100 per host, 8 hosts total.

\textbf{Question}: What is the fleet's aggregate rps capacity under the
canonical KV/latency bound, and what is the marginal cost per rps when
scaling from 8 to 16 hosts? Is an end-to-end 99th-percentile latency
under 100 ms even feasible for this workload --- and if not, what can
the scheduler bound?

\textbf{Solution} (illustrative algebra --- the per-host figure is an
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} estimate for showing the fleet math; the real
per-host rps must come from a \hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14} deployment benchmark, bounded
by KV residency per \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Per-host request rate --- the KV/latency bound (Ch. 17
  basis).} A host holds 17 whole concurrent full-context requests (the
  KV-residency ceiling, \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}; byte-accurate 17.51, floored to 17
  --- no fractional admission) and each request holds KV for an assumed
  mean \textasciitilde8.6 s (1.08 s prefill + \textasciitilde7.5 s
  decode of 300 output tokens at \textasciitilde25 ms/token, \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8}
  --- Ch 17's conditional proxy for E{[}W\_KV-resident{]}). So C/W = 17
  / 8.6 = 1.977 ≈ \textbf{2.0 requests/s} \mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}} at steady
  state --- a \textbf{KV-slot-based analytical UPPER bound on admitted
  request completion rate, conditional on an assumed mean KV residency
  time}, the defensible capacity number, from concurrency × KV-holding
  time (Little's law), not a token throughput ÷ tokens per request (§3:
  \textasciitilde35,000 tok/s ÷ \textasciitilde9,500 needs
  \textasciitilde32 in flight --- 17 slots cannot hold).
\item
  \textbf{Fleet aggregate (8 hosts):} 8 × (17/8.6) = 15.8 ≈ \textbf{16
  req/s}, \textbf{below} the canonical 40-rps peak. The idealized
  minimum is ⌈344/17⌉ = \textbf{21 hosts}; the ρ ≈ 0.95 factor used in
  this chapter and Ch 17 --- a fractional-throughput efficiency derate
  on the rate, not a second slot floor and not a failure spare ---
  raises it to 344/(0.95 × 17) = 344/16.15 ≈ 21.3 → \textbf{22 hosts}
  (the ρ-derate of the KV screen; the \emph{independent} full-square
  case (ii) prefill compute screen also gives 22, ⌈21.81⌉ --- a
  different 22, from a FLOP-counting case rather than a ρ derate).
  (⌈86/17⌉ = \textbf{6} is not a second, tighter constraint: it is the
  \emph{same} identity λW/C = λ/(C/W) --- the KV-residency view of §3
  --- evaluated at the 10-rps average instead of the 40-rps peak; at the
  peak both views give ⌈344/17⌉ = \textbf{21} (→ \textbf{22} with the ρ
  ≈ 0.95 derate), so the peak requirement governs.)
\item
  \textbf{Marginal cost (real basis):} adding one host at
  \textasciitilde\$20/hr (cloud, \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}) buys
  \textasciitilde2.0 req/s, so marginal cost = \$20 ÷ (17/8.6) = \$20 ×
  8.6 / 17 = \$10.12 ≈ \textbf{\$10 per req/s per hour.} (The
  \textasciitilde\$0.012 figure from \hyperref[chap:4]{Chapter 4} is a per-\emph{request}
  managed-API price, not a per-req/s-per-hour rate --- \hyperref[chap:4]{Chapter 4}'s own
  basis note says the two cost bases are not interchangeable --- and
  with a real multi-second service time per request the per-host rate is
  \textasciitilde2.0, not a hypothetical 250.)
\item
  \textbf{Algebra practice (kept \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}):} the per-host 250
  rps / fleet 2,000 rps arithmetic is kept only to show the
  \emph{structure} of the fleet math (marginal host cost ÷ marginal
  capacity): \$20 ÷ 250 = \$0.08 per rps per hour. The 250 rps is
  hypothetical and unachievable for this 9.2K-in/300-out workload;
  substitute the measured per-host rate from a \hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14} deployment
  benchmark, or the \textasciitilde2.0 req/s bound derived here ---
  which gives the \$10 above.
\item
  \textbf{The 100-ms end-to-end p99 is infeasible under the stated
  workload, and that is the honest answer.} A request here takes
  \textasciitilde7.5 s of decode alone (300 output tokens ×
  \textasciitilde25 ms/token, \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8}), so no scheduler-overhead
  percentage can bring end-to-end p99 under 100 ms --- model execution
  already exceeds the budget by \textasciitilde86× (8,580 ms ÷ 100 ms)
  --- nearly two orders of magnitude. What the scheduler \emph{can}
  bound is the overhead it adds \emph{on top of} model execution. Here
  that overhead is a \textbf{target, not an achieved prediction}: the
  dispatcher's added p99 overhead is specified as
  \texttt{≤\ 5\%\ of\ the\ request\ time\ on\ the\ same\ request\ population},
  and the \textasciitilde0.5 ms dispatch figure is a median/nominal
  value that \textbf{cannot establish p99} without a measured scheduler
  distribution. Treat it as an SLO hypothesis --- \textbf{must be
  demonstrated in a load test} (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}) with the
  numerator/denominator defined as scheduler-added latency divided by
  that request's own model-execution time --- before any p99 claim is
  made.
\item
  \textbf{Planning-bound caveat (sizing tail).} §3's caveat governs:
  analytical upper bounds (the KV-slot bound on admitted rate for
  \textasciitilde2.0 req/s; the idealized Ch 8 roofline for
  \textasciitilde19,800 prefill tok/s), not implied deployed rates; no
  p95/p99 guarantee for the 21/22-host sizing (21 = KV screen under case
  (i); 22 = the ρ ≈ 0.95 derate and, separately, the full-square case
  (ii) prefill compute screen; 34 at 70\% × 95\% + N−1 under case (ii)
  versus 32 for KV-only) --- the host count is a
  KV-residency/service-time \textbf{capacity floor} (derived from the
  KV-slot upper bound on the per-host rate), and the deployable host
  count is \textbf{max(capacity requirement, tail-latency requirement
  N\_SLO)}, so measure p95/p99 and SLO-goodput (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}) before
  committing hardware. Little's Law stays a mean relation there:
  E\mbox{{[}L{]}} = λ·E\mbox{{[}W{]}} with E\mbox{{[}W{]}} the mean KV residency --- never a
  p95/p99 W.
\end{itemize}
