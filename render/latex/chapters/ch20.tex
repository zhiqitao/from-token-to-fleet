\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

As a RAG Q\&A workload scales from a single host to a fleet of serving
nodes, the architect must confront a new set of trade-offs: how does
aggregate throughput scale with added hardware, how does scheduling
latency affect user-perceived latency, and at what point does
autoscaling cease to be cost-effective? This chapter addresses these
questions through concrete arithmetic, not hand-waving.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


Fleet-level optimization treats the entire collection of serving hosts
as a single resource pool rather than independent islands. The central
insight is that aggregate system throughput is not merely the sum of
individual host throughputs; it is modulated by scheduling overhead,
network contention, and the cost of moving data between hosts and
storage. Two cost effects must be kept distinct. The \textbf{average}
cost per query declines only along the canonical 10 → 40 rps ramp, where
fixed overhead (model loading, infrastructure, staff) is spread across
4× the requests and across more SLO-compliant completions per
observation window (Ch 14's goodput definition). Cost per request may
decline only until additional capacity steps dominate; adding idle hosts
at fixed demand raises unit cost. The \textbf{marginal} cost of each
added host, however, is roughly constant in this model --- one host buys
\textasciitilde2.0 req/s (17/8.6 = 1.977, \mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}) at
\textasciitilde\$20/hr, so the marginal cost stays near
\textasciitilde\$10 per req/s per hour (Ch 17 §7 frames the Nth host as
diminishing marginal \emph{benefit}, not falling marginal cost). Unit
convention for this chapter: \emph{rps} denotes an arrival rate and
\emph{req/s} a capacity rate; both are requests per second. The economic
lever is amortizing the fixed term, not the marginal one.

\Needspace{7\baselineskip}

The key concepts are:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Aggregate throughput}: The total queries-per-second the fleet
  can sustain before latency SLO violations.
\item
  \textbf{Scheduling overhead}: The per-request cost of dispatching a
  query to an available host, including placement decisions and data
  movement.
\item
  \textbf{Autoscaling break-even}: The point at which growth in
  SLO-compliant completions per observation window covers the next
  host's fixed cost --- \emph{average} cost per query falls as fixed
  costs amortize, while marginal cost per query stays roughly constant.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


The mental model for fleet optimization replaces the single-host query
pipeline with a two-level hierarchy. At the lower level, each host
processes queries using its GPU/CPU pipeline. At the upper level, a
scheduler routes incoming requests across hosts, handling load
balancing, model parallelism where needed, and graceful degradation
under overload.

The scheduler's decisions have direct impact on the tail of the latency
distribution. Poor placement can cause some hosts to saturate while
others remain underutilized, inflating the 99th-percentile latency even
when aggregate throughput is ample. Conversely, intelligent scheduling
--- such as consistent hashing for session affinity or predictive
placement based on historical patterns --- tends to keep utilization
more uniform across the fleet and reduces tail latency.

A critical assumption in this model is that the workload follows a known
arrival process. For the canonical scenario (Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}):
\textasciitilde10 rps average and \textasciitilde40 rps peak. With
\textasciitilde9,200 in-tokens and \textasciitilde300 out-tokens per
query, a \textasciitilde8.6 s service time per request (1.08 s prefill +
\textasciitilde7.5 s decode), and a 70B dense FP16 model running on
8×H100 (640 GB total HBM3), the fleet must sustain
\textbf{\textasciitilde40 requests/s at peak}, which --- by Little's Law
--- corresponds to \textbf{λ·W ≈ 40 × 8.6 ≈ 344 requests in flight} (W
here is Ch 17's assumed mean KV-residency time E{[}W\_KV-resident{]} ≈
8.6 s, a conditional scenario proxy --- not the end-to-end W\_e2e, which
adds queue, tool and inter-turn time that, under the stated assumption,
holds no KV), not merely the \textasciitilde100 concurrent \emph{users}
or the nominal 40 rps. The distinction between ``concurrent users'' (a
population), the arrival rate (rps), and in-flight requests (λ·W) is
exactly what \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17} develops; it is the difference between sizing
for a population and sizing a fleet against a latency bound.

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


Take a fleet of N hosts, each with 8×H100 running a single 70B FP16
model instance. Two capacities must be reconciled ---
\textbf{throughput} (tokens/s) and \textbf{KV residency} (concurrent
requests) --- and neither can be reduced to a single ``rps'' constant,
because rps is workload-specific (per-request input/output tokens,
context length, batch).

\subsubsection{Latency floor first, then reconcile the two capacity
bounds}\label{latency-floor-first-then-reconcile-the-two-capacity-bounds}

A 300-output-token request at a \textasciitilde25 ms/token decode rate
(\hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8}) takes \textasciitilde7.5 s of generation alone, plus 1.08 s
prefill, so the service time is \textasciitilde8.6 s per request (before
tool calls). Two separate ceilings bound how many requests a host can
\emph{serve per second}, and they must be reconciled --- they are not
interchangeable.

\subsubsection{Throughput bound
(tokens/s)}\label{throughput-bound-tokenss}

A host sustains an illustrative \textasciitilde35,000 tokens/s of
\emph{total} (prefill+decode) serving throughput --- the conservative
low end of the \textasciitilde35,000--56,000 tokens/s achievable range
in \hyperref[chap:15]{Chapter 15} \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} --- distinct from the
\textasciitilde19,800 tokens/s \emph{prefill-only} ideal in \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8} (8
× 2,472 at 35\% MFU), which counts prefill compute alone. \textbf{These
two are not directly comparable as single numbers: \textasciitilde35,000
is a phase-mixed \emph{prefill+decode} total and \textasciitilde19,800
is a \emph{prefill-only} figure. To compare them one must first
phase-resolve both, then compare prefill with prefill (the presented
workload's prefill leg is 2.0 req/s × 9,200 = 18,400 input tokens/s) and
decode with decode (2.0 × 300 = 600 output tokens/s); aggregating the
two phases into one ``total'' rate requires an explicit
cost/phase-equivalence model.} Dividing the total by the
\textasciitilde9,500 tokens/request gives \textasciitilde3.7 requests/s
--- \textbf{but this is not achievable here}, because it would require
\textasciitilde3.7 × 8.6 ≈ 32 concurrent requests in flight, roughly
double the host's KV-residency ceiling (C ≈ 17.5, conservative integer
floor 17, \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}). The token-throughput figure is thus a non-binding
upper edge for this canonical workload.

\subsubsection{KV-residency / latency bound (Little's
Law)}\label{kv-residency-latency-bound-littles-law}

The real ceiling is set by concurrency and KV residency time together: a
host holds 17 whole concurrent full-context requests (the
\textasciitilde17.5 KV ceiling, \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}, floored to whole contexts
--- no fractional admission) each for an assumed mean KV residency
E{[}W\_KV-resident{]} ≈ 8.6 s, so C/W = 17 / 8.6 = 1.977 ≈ \textbf{2.0
requests/s} \mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}} at steady state --- a \textbf{KV-slot upper
bound on admitted rate} (defined below).

The binding per-host rate is the \emph{minimum} of the two --- here the
KV/latency bound under cases (i) and (iii); a third, phase-resolved
prefill-compute screen is evaluated below and binds under case (ii) (22
hosts raw, 23 with the ρ derate). This is exactly why a token-throughput
number must not be divided by tokens-per-request to manufacture an
``rps'' here: the units do cancel (tokens/s ÷ tokens/request =
requests/s), but the numerator is a phase-mixed prefill+decode total
with no cost/phase-equivalence model, and the \textasciitilde3.7 req/s
answer would need \textasciitilde32 concurrent requests against the
17-slot host capacity. The per-host request rate is a \emph{concurrency
× service-time} quantity --- what makes the division wrong here is what
its numerator mixes and its answer assumes, not its units.

\subsubsection{What the \textasciitilde2.0 req/s figure is --- and is
not}\label{what-the-2.0-reqs-figure-is-and-is-not}

The \textasciitilde2.0 req/s figure is a \textbf{KV-slot-based
analytical UPPER bound on admitted request completion rate, conditional
on an assumed mean KV residency time} --- a necessary capacity screen:
not a measured serving throughput, not a lower guaranteed rate, and not
an SLO goodput. The \emph{lower} bound on host count derives FROM this
upper bound on the per-host rate (⌈λ/(C/W)⌉ = ⌈λ·W/C⌉); it is never a
lower bound on the rate itself. It is achievable only if serving C
concurrent sequences does \emph{not} inflate W beyond the assumed
\textasciitilde8.6 s, and neither a compute, bandwidth, nor scheduler
constraint binds first. In real continuous batching, W is a function of
batch occupancy, scheduler policy, prefill interference, decode
batching, the arrival process, tensor-parallel communication, and the
context distribution --- so C and W are not always independent
constants. Treat \textasciitilde2.0 req/s as an upper-bound model, and
require benchmarked goodput (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}) under the target arrival
process for actual fleet sizing. \textbf{Planning-bound caveat:} like
the \textasciitilde19,800 prefill tokens/s/host figure (Ch. 8, idealized
prefill only), the \textasciitilde2.0 req/s/host figure is the
\emph{KV-slot upper bound on admitted rate} named above ---
model-derived, \textbf{not an implied deployed rate}. \textbf{No p95/p99
performance guarantee has been shown} for any of the host counts in this
chapter; the p95/p99 tail latency and achieved goodput must be measured
on the target deployment (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}) before the governing 23-host
sizing (21 = the KV/Little screen, case-independent; 22 = the ρ ≈ 0.95
derate of that screen --- and, independently, the raw full-square case
(ii) prefill compute screen, which also gives 22 and rises to 23 with
the same ρ derate, the governing screen; 34 at the 70\% × 95\% + N−1
policy against 32 for KV-only --- Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}, u and ρ independent)
is treated as validated.

\subsubsection{Simultaneous feasibility check (prefill + decode
coexistence)}\label{simultaneous-feasibility-check-prefill-decode-coexistence}

The \textasciitilde2.0 req/s/host bound assumes prefill and decode run
on the same host at the same time. Evaluated at the displayed
\textasciitilde2.0 req/s --- a deliberate stress screen, slightly above
the unrounded 17/8.6 = 1.977 --- the prefill demand is 2.0 × 9,200 =
\textbf{18,400 input tokens/s per host} (§3). The idealized prefill
ceiling is 8 × 0.989 PF/s × 35\% / (2 × 70e9) ≈ \textbf{19,780 tokens/s}
(\hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8}, 35\% MFU, prefill-only --- \textbf{case (i), linear-only
FLOP convention}). \emph{Cross-note on MFU:} Ch 20 uses the conservative
35\% planning MFU (Ch 8); Ch 22/24 use 40\% sustained (Ch 24 §3.3) ---
the choice moves the case-(i) screen from 19 to 17 hosts. The ratio is
18,400 ÷ 19,780 ≈ \textbf{93\%} under case (i); under case (ii)
(full-square, ×1.1723) the ceiling falls to ≈16,870 linear-equivalent
tokens/s and the ratio becomes 18,400 ÷ 16,870 ≈ \textbf{109\%} (the
same demand ÷ ceiling in PFLOP/s: 2 × 1.5099 ÷ 2.7692, with 2.7692
PFLOP/s the \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8} 35\%-MFU host peak), and under case (iii)
(causal-triangle, ×1.0861), ceiling ≈18,210 tokens/s, 18,400 ÷ 18,210
≈\textbf{101\%} --- above 100\% the prefill leg alone does not fit at 2
req/s --- so the \textasciitilde7\% headroom under case (i) is a
property of the linear-only convention, not of the hardware. Prefill
must run at \textasciitilde93\% of the idealized prefill ceiling under
case (i). This is \textbf{\mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}} (Axis-3 measurement status;
defined with the evidence taxonomy in the Sources, Method, and
Reproducibility chapter) --- a prefill-load check, not a measured rate.
The \textasciitilde93\% leaves very little headroom before decode
coexistence, interference, and the actual achieved MFU. Prefill and
decode share the same hardware. Batch scheduling and decode interleaving
compete for the same compute. The achieved MFU is below the 35\% ideal.
Therefore \textasciitilde2.0 req/s/host is an \textbf{analytical upper
bound}, \textbf{NOT a claim that a host serves 2 req/s at SLO}. Before
treating 2 req/s/host as deployable, measure the p95 TTFT, p95 TPOT, and
SLO-goodput on a representative host (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}).

\subsubsection{Fleet aggregate throughput (illustrative
form)}\label{fleet-aggregate-throughput-illustrative-form}

Keeping the illustrative per-host req/s = Q for the algebra, aggregate
throughput is

\[
\Lambda_\text{fleet} = N \times Q
\]

At the canonical peak of 40 rps, fleet size must satisfy \textbf{both}
the KV-residency and the throughput/latency bounds, whichever binds
first. The Little and the KV-residency bound are the same identity
written in two views: λW/C = λ/(C/W). They agree at the peak, exactly as
\hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17} notes. The additive form is a first-order capacity floor:
scheduling, network-contention and data-movement effects are real but
not captured here, and are measured in §4.

\textbf{Little/KV screen → 21 hosts.} With the per-host rate at
\textasciitilde2.0 req/s (17/8.6 = 1.977, the binding KV-slot upper
bound on admitted rate above; its \emph{reciprocal} gives the lower
bound on host count), a fleet for 40 rps starts at \textbf{21 hosts}:
⌈40 × 8.6 / 17⌉ = ⌈344/17⌉ = ⌈20.24⌉ = 21. By Little's Law the in-flight
count at 40 rps is λ·W = 40 × 8.6 ≈ 344, and ⌈344/17⌉ = \textbf{21
hosts} (17 whole contexts per host, no fractional admission).

\textbf{Token-throughput screen → 11 hosts (non-binding artifact).} The
token-throughput ceiling gives ⌈40 / 3.684⌉ = 11 hosts, but 3.684 req/s
is a phase-mixed prefill+decode rate divided by tokens-per-request:
without an explicit phase-equivalence model this is an artifact, not a
valid screen, and it stays non-binding here either way.

\textbf{ρ-derate → 22 hosts.} The ρ ≈ 0.95 fractional-throughput derate
raises the requirement to 344 / (0.95 × 17) = 344/16.15 ≈ 21.3 →
\textbf{22 hosts}.

Separately, the independent prefill compute screen (Ch. 8, 35\% MFU)
gives \textbf{19 hosts under case (i)} (⌈18.60⌉, linear-only),
\textbf{21 under case (iii)} (⌈20.21⌉, causal-triangle) and \textbf{22
under case (ii)} (⌈21.81⌉, full-square conservative) --- note that case
(ii)'s 22 is a \emph{different} 22 from the ρ-derate's 22 (one comes
from a FLOP-counting case, the other from a throughput-efficiency derate
of the KV screen); they coincide in value only by arithmetic accident.

The ``far fewer hosts (\textasciitilde6)'' figure applies only to the
10-rps average (⌈86/17⌉ = 6), not the 40-rps peak.

The correct statement is: the fleet must satisfy every named screen, and
the \emph{larger} requirement wins --- \textbf{19 hosts under case (i),
21 under case (iii), 22 under case (ii)}; with the standalone ρ = 0.95
derate applied to the prefill screen exactly as the policy arithmetic
applies it, case (ii) rises to ⌈21.81 / 0.95⌉ = \textbf{23 hosts} ---
the largest requirement before the full policy, and on that count the
fleet is \textbf{\textasciitilde95\% prefill-saturated}: each host
serves 40/23 = 1.74 req/s ⇒ 1.74 × 9,200 = 16,000 prefill tok/s against
the case-(ii) ceiling of ≈16,870 tok/s, i.e.~\textasciitilde5\% headroom
for decode coexistence and interference (ρ = 0.95 is a
throughput-efficiency derate: dividing the host requirement by ρ is the
same as dividing each host's effective rate by ρ, so it applies to any
throughput-bound screen --- KV or prefill --- and does not re-derive the
35\% MFU assumption itself); and at the 70\% × 95\% + N−1 policy
\textbf{34 under case (ii) versus 32 for KV-only} (⌈21.81/0.665⌉+1 = 34;
⌈344/11.305⌉+1 = 32 --- Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}, u = 0.70 and ρ = 0.95 applied
as two independent factors); \textbf{all of 19/21/22/23/32/34 are
illustrative planning screens under their stated FLOP/MFU assumptions,
not measured SLO-goodput.}

\textbf{Reading \hyperref[fig:20.1]{Fig 20.1}'s two gaps:} below saturation the
\textasciitilde5\% gap between C\_analytic(N) = N × 17/8.6 and C\_eff(N)
= 0.95 × C\_analytic is the assumed throughput-efficiency derate; above
saturation C\_eff − S, with S = min(40, C\_eff), is \emph{unused}
capacity from insufficient offered demand --- not scheduling overhead.
Queueing and tail-latency penalties are not determined by this two-line
calculation and must be shown separately if claimed.

\subsubsection{Scheduling overhead}\label{scheduling-overhead}

A lightweight round-robin scheduler adds \textasciitilde0.5 ms of
dispatch latency per request \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} --- negligible next to a
real 7.5 s decode. The governing target is absolute: \textbf{\textless{}
100 ms p99 dispatch}, \textasciitilde1.2\% of the canonical 8.6 s
request and, equivalently, ≤ 5\% of the 2 s TTFT SLO turn budget; the
fraction is derived from the absolute number, not a second constraint.

\subsubsection{Autoscaling economics}\label{autoscaling-economics}

Each 8×H100 host costs \textasciitilde\$20/hour (cloud, \$2.50/GPU-hr
canonical) or \textasciitilde\$8/hour amortized (owned) ---
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}. The marginal cost per additional req/s is \$20 ÷
(req/s gained per host); the economic lesson (declining average cost per
request as fixed costs amortize across more requests) is sound, but the
exact per-req/s figure is only meaningful after the real per-host
throughput is benchmarked. Quantifying the break-even, with a fixed
overhead of \textasciitilde\$40/hr (infrastructure and staff,
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}): at the 40-rps peak the autoscaled fleet runs the
governing \textbf{23 hosts} at (23 × \$20 + \$40) ÷ 144,000 requests/hr
≈ \textbf{\$3.47 per 1K requests}, and at the 10-rps average it runs 6
hosts at (6 × \$20 + \$40) ÷ 36,000 ≈ \textbf{\$4.44 per 1K} --- a
\textasciitilde22\% average-cost decline across the 4× demand ramp while
the marginal \$10.12 per req/s per hour is unchanged. Two basis notes:
the \$40/hr is autoscaling-only control-plane overhead --- a statically
provisioned fleet needs no autoscaler and avoids it --- and the 21-host
Little/KV screen (⌈344/17⌉) is a \emph{lower bound} on the host count;
the case-(ii) prefill screen with the ρ derate binds at 23 (§3). The
break-even against a statically provisioned, peak-sized 23-host fleet
(\$460/hr = 23 × \$20, no control plane) sits at 21 hosts,
i.e.~sustained demand ≤ \textasciitilde41.5 rps: above that, autoscaling
costs more than the peak-sized fleet it replaces.

Measurement methodology: use a distributed load generator (e.g., Locust
or a custom gRPC-driven pump) that sends queries at the target rps while
instrumenting each host's internal metrics --- GPU occupancy, kernel
launch time, scheduler dispatch timestamp, and queue depth. Aggregate
these via a time-series database (InfluxDB or Prometheus) and compute
the above metrics over sliding 1-minute windows.

A practical pitfall: measuring only aggregate req/s without tracking
per-host variance can mask saturation in individual nodes. Always report
both the fleet-wide and per-host metrics.

Placement also determines the communication and failure domain. A slow
or failed participant can delay an entire model-parallel group while
other replicas remain healthy. Group-level completion tails,
asynchronous failures and the affected requests therefore accompany
per-host utilization. A placement that improves average utilization but
increases cross-group contention can reduce SLO-goodput; replica
isolation and group recovery require measurement before they become
fleet-capacity assumptions.

The qualification gate in \hyperref[chap:18]{Chapter 18} applies before a configuration
enters any capacity pool. A fallback route consumes capacity too; its
quality, deadline and data-policy constraints remain part of the
placement contract.

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Treating hosts as independent}: Optimizing each host in
  isolation (e.g., tuning batch size per host) inflates the variance in
  per-host service time; the fleet's tail latency and the \hyperref[tab:20.1]{Table 20-1}
  utilization-variance target (\textless{} 0.2) are set by the slowest
  hosts, not by the average host.
\item
  \textbf{Overlooking data movement cost}: In RAG workloads, the
  retrieved-context text that becomes the \textasciitilde8,000-token
  portion of the \textasciitilde9,200 input tokens per query must be
  fetched from a vector database or cache over the network, while the
  \textasciitilde300 out-tokens are generated entirely on the host ---
  only the final response needs to reach the user. The quantity that
  distinguishes architectures is bytes fetched per query: if every host
  fetches independently, each query moves its full context bytes,
  repeated for every query and every host. A shared cache layer or a
  colocated store fetches each distinct context once and serves later
  hits locally, so the saving is proportional to the cross-query
  context-overlap rate --- not to a scaling exponent that is identical
  under both schemes.
\item
  \textbf{Autoscaling too aggressively}: Adding hosts at the first sign
  of latency increase can be suboptimal if the bottleneck is scheduler
  throughput or cache contention, not compute capacity. Measure the
  marginal cost of adding a host before triggering a scale-out event.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring cold-start cost}: When a host is freshly provisioned
  or re-warmed after idle, loading the full model weights into HBM takes
  \textasciitilde28--112 seconds \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} (a load-path
  assumption for a \textasciitilde140 GB model over a
  \textasciitilde10--40 Gb/s (≈1.25--5 GB/s) path --- 140 GB ÷ 28--112 s
  ≈ 5--1.25 GB/s, i.e.~≈40--10 Gb/s --- not a hardware benchmark;
  distinct from the much shorter KV-cache warm-up of \textasciitilde2 s
  discussed in \hyperref[chap:15]{Chapter 15}, which re-populates only the retained KV
  state). Autoscaling policies must account for this weight-load warm-up
  window when scaling out a fresh host; otherwise the fleet experiences
  a spike in error rate or timeout during scale-out events.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


Fleet-level optimization reshapes the serving architecture in three
ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Centralized scheduler}: A lightweight service (e.g., based on
  etcd or a custom placement engine) maintains real-time view of
  per-host utilization and makes dispatch decisions. This service must
  be highly available, and its dispatch overhead should stay a small
  fraction of the per-request SLO --- for the canonical
  \textasciitilde8.6 s service time, a sub-millisecond decision is a
  comfortable \emph{target} (the round-robin baseline adds
  \textasciitilde0.5 ms, §3), not a universal necessity: the binding
  constraint is that dispatch overhead not consume a meaningful share of
  the request/turn SLO, which depends on routing frequency and
  scheduling architecture.
\item
  \textbf{Data colocation}: Vector indexes and model weights are stored
  close to the hosts that consume them. This may mean running a local
  Qdrant or Milvus instance per host, or using a shared object store
  (e.g., MinIO) with read-replicas per availability zone. The goal is to
  minimize the distance --- measured in network hops --- between the
  scheduler's placement decision and the data fetch. But colocation is a
  trade, not a free win: a per-host index multiplies memory/storage,
  consistency, ingestion, and operational cost across the fleet, and
  model-weight locality is a different problem from vector-index
  locality. The right placement depends on the workload --- compare
  centralized retrieval, AZ-local retrieval, replicated indexes, and
  cached results, and identify the traffic/latency point at which
  colocation's access-latency saving outweighs its replication cost. Do
  not conflate the two locality questions.
\item
  \textbf{Graceful degradation}: The fleet should have a mode where,
  under extreme overload, it serves stale or truncated answers rather
  than failing entirely. This could mean returning the top-k results
  from a cached embedding rather than recomputing, or surfacing a ``try
  again'' message with an estimated wait time. The architectural
  contract must make this trade-off explicit so product teams can set
  appropriate SLOs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The useful completion is an accepted
output}\label{the-useful-completion-is-an-accepted-output}

For a RAG fleet the useful completion is an \emph{accepted} output --- a
response that passes the deadline, integrity, quality, and safety gates
in the observation window --- so hosts are sized against accepted
outputs per observation window, not raw work; the
rejection/OOM/cancellation counters and the cost-per-accepted-completion
basis follow that rule, and placement follows the measured-service-time
rule (place warm replicas where measured service time, not nominal load
time, sets the request path). The same accepted-completion goodput
accounting applies to any fixed-output-contract workload; a
video-generation fleet is the clearest parallel beyond RAG --- there the
accepted output is a clip passing the same four gates in the same
observation window, raw work rates (frames/s, clip-seconds/s) need a
stated denominator and do not normalize the nonlinear attention cost,
and warm replicas trade idle cost for avoiding checkpoint downloads,
initialization, compilation, and encoder loading on the request path.
For a RAG fleet architect the parallel is direct: both fleets size hosts
against accepted outputs in the observation window, not raw work, so the
same capacity screens and placement rules apply.

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


Several open questions remain at the fleet level:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Heterogeneous hardware}: What happens when hosts mix H100,
  A100, and CPU-only nodes? The scheduler must handle different
  throughput ceilings and memory capacities, and the aggregate
  throughput calculation becomes a weighted sum rather than a simple
  multiple. Real-world measurements of mixed-device fleets are scarce;
  most published benchmarking uses homogeneous hardware, so the
  crossover point where heterogeneity hurts versus helps is not well
  quantified.
\item
  \textbf{Multi-model serving}: Ch 18 covers routing, tiering, and
  quantization trade-offs for mixed model sizes; what this chapter
  leaves open is how to compute a fleet's aggregate capacity when
  per-host rates differ by model --- the N × Q identity in §3 assumes a
  single Q --- and how the scheduler should place a request whose model
  is not resident on the least-loaded host without breaking session
  affinity or the shared KV budget. Joint placement of heterogeneous
  model sizes under one KV budget (Ch 18 §3.3) remains unresolved.
\item
  \textbf{Economic auto-scaling}: The break-even point between owning
  hardware versus cloud-on-demand depends on utilization patterns that
  are workload-specific. A fleet that runs at 20\% utilization on
  average may be cheaper in the cloud; one at 80\% utilization favors
  owned hardware. The transition point is a function of capital
  expenditure amortization period, electricity cost, and the volatility
  of user demand. Published TCO analyses typically assume steady
  utilization, but bursty workloads with short-lived spikes can make
  ownership risky unless over-provisioned.
\item
  \textbf{Tail-latency arbitration}: When two queries compete for the
  same GPU HBM bandwidth, the 99th-percentile latency can spike
  disproportionately. We lack a general-purpose scheduler policy that
  guarantees a target tail latency under arbitrary arrival patterns;
  this remains an area of active research. Some systems use priority
  queues or token buckets to cap per-query bandwidth, but these
  mechanisms add scheduling complexity and can increase median latency.
\item
  \textbf{Cross-query caching effects}: RAG prompts share long leading
  prefixes across users asking related questions, and the open quantity
  is the \emph{prefix-overlap distribution} --- shared leading tokens as
  a fraction of a query's prefill FLOPs. That fraction, not the raw
  token count, bounds what prefix caching (Ch 15) can save. Charged
  against it is the cache-management cost: invalidation, cross-replica
  coherence, metadata, and the loss of batch-occupancy flexibility when
  requests pin shared prefixes. We lack large-scale measurements of that
  overlap distribution and of how much of the theoretical prefill saving
  survives the management cost. Reuse of earlier \emph{outputs} across
  askers is a separate question about answer quality and provenance, not
  a KV-reuse question.
\end{itemize}

the canonical KV/latency bound, and what is the marginal cost per req/s
