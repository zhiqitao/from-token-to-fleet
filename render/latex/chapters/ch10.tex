\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

After this chapter we should be able to answer the inevitable scaling
question: \emph{a single GPU cannot hold this model or keep up with this
workload --- how do we split the work across many GPUs, and which split
is right?} We will build the arithmetic for why a model outgrows one
GPU, then study the five parallelization strategies --- tensor,
pipeline, data, expert, and sequence parallelism --- plus a serving-only
sixth (P/D disaggregation, which has no training counterpart), and
develop a rule for choosing among them based on the model's
weight-residency, the workload's batch shape, and the interconnect
speed. After this chapter, ``we scaled it out'' becomes a precise claim
about \emph{what} was split and \emph{why}.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


The central idea: parallelism is \textbf{splitting one of the three
things a model occupies --- its weights, its layers, or its data ---
across multiple devices.} Before reading on, one framing note that
prevents a common source of confusion: \textbf{the parallelism toolkit
differs between training and inference, and ``data parallelism'' means
different things in each mode.} In \emph{training}, DP means replicated
weights plus gradient all-reduce every step (plus FSDP, which shards and
re-gathers weights/optimizer state); the communication load is at the
gradient-sync point. In \emph{inference}, DP simply means running
independent model replicas to raise throughput --- there is no gradient
sync at all, because each request is served by one replica. There is
also an inference-specific family --- context/pipeline partitioning,
expert parallelism, and \textbf{P/D disaggregation} (splitting the
prefill stage from the decode stage into separate pools) --- which has
no training analogue. The rest of this section defines the strategies,
and §10.3 applies them to inference (the book's canonical serving
context) unless a strategy is explicitly training-only.

The strategy names tell us what gets split:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Tensor parallelism (TP)} splits a single layer's weights
  across GPUs. Each GPU holds a slice of the weight matrix and exchanges
  partial results through the TP group's interconnect. These layer-level
  dependencies require sufficient measured bandwidth and low latency;
  NVLink is one implementation, not an algorithmic requirement. TP is
  \emph{intra-layer}. (Inference: shards weights; per-step exchange is
  activations, not weights --- see Ch. 9.)
\item
  \textbf{Pipeline parallelism (PP)} splits the model's layers (stages)
  across GPUs. GPU 1 runs layers 1--10, GPU 2 runs 11--20, and so on;
  activations flow forward, gradients flow backward. PP is
  \emph{inter-layer}, communication-light, but idle bubbles appear at
  stage boundaries. (Inference: can split decode stages; bubbles hurt
  latency-sensitive serving more than training.)
\item
  \textbf{Data parallelism (DP)} --- \emph{different in the two modes.}
  Training: replicates all weights on every GPU, feeds each a different
  batch, all-reduces gradients after each step; communication-heavy only
  at the gradient-sync point, but each GPU must hold the full model.
  Inference: independent replicas each serve a disjoint set of requests;
  no gradient sync; throughput scales linearly until KV or memory binds
  (Ch. 17).
\item
  \textbf{Expert parallelism (EP)} shards the experts of a
  Mixture-of-Experts model across GPUs and routes each token to the GPU
  holding its experts --- an all-to-all exchange per token. EP is
  MoE-specific. (Used in both training and inference for MoE models.)
\item
  \textbf{Sequence/context parallelism (CP)} splits the sequence
  dimension across GPUs, useful for very long contexts where the KV
  cache or attention activations exceed one GPU. (Inference-centric; the
  serving analogue of training sequence parallelism.)
\item
  \textbf{P/D disaggregation (inference)} --- separates the
  compute-bound prefill stage from the bandwidth-bound decode stage onto
  different GPU pools so each can be sized and scaled independently (Ch.
  11). This has no training counterpart and is a serving-only strategy.
\end{enumerate}

These are not alternatives; production systems \textbf{compose} them
(e.g.~DP across nodes × TP within a node × PP across a few nodes; or
TP+CP within a decode pool + a separate prefill pool). The architect's
job is deciding which dimension to split first, and in which mode
(training vs inference) the parallelism is being applied.

The strategies are not either/or. \hyperref[fig:10.1]{Fig 10.1} shows the three canonical
patterns side by side. \textbf{Tensor parallel} shards one layer's
weight matrix across GPUs --- activations are replicated, and each GPU's
partial sums are all-reduced at layer-level dependencies in the
illustrated layout. Those frequent exchanges make measured collective
latency and throughput margin important. \textbf{Pipeline parallel} cuts
the layer stack into stages on different GPUs --- hidden states cross
stage boundaries point-to-point, an idle bubble appears as the pipeline
fills and drains, and the communication can span nodes if the
stage-boundary transfer meets its budget. \textbf{Data parallel}
replicates the whole model and splits the batch --- in training every
replica all-reduces its gradient each step; in inference the replicas
are independent, each serving a disjoint slice of requests with no sync
at all. Expert and sequence parallelism (EP/CP) are additional axes that
reuse the same machinery. The three primary patterns \textbf{compose}
(e.g.~DP across nodes × TP within a node × PP across a few nodes) ---
which dimension binds determines which to split first.

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


Each strategy is best distinguished by \emph{what it trades bandwidth
for}. Tensor parallelism exchanges layer results to distribute weight
residency but adds synchronization dependencies. Pipeline parallelism
transfers stage-boundary activations and accepts bubbles as the price of
not holding all layers on one GPU. Training data parallelism trades
gradient bandwidth for replicated weights; inference replicas have no
gradient synchronization. In either mode, each replica's assigned
devices must fit its state.

The useful mental model is a \textbf{resource triangle}: we have weights
(memory), activations (work), and data (batch). Each strategy moves one
corner off a single GPU at the cost of communication. When a GPU runs
out of \emph{memory}, we reach for TP/PP (weight splitting). When a GPU
is \emph{idle} waiting, we reach for DP/CP (more work per GPU). When the
model is \emph{too big to replicate}, TP wins over DP. The binding
constraint chooses the strategy.

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


\subsection{Why the Canonical 70B Doesn't Fit One
H100}\label{why-the-canonical-70b-doesnt-fit-one-h100}

The canonical model is 70B FP16 dense = 140 GB of weights
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} (reality check: 70 ×10⁹ params × 2
bytes). A single H100 has 80 GB of HBM \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}FACT{]}}. The
arithmetic is immediate:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The weights alone don't fit on one H100:
\end{itemize}

\[
140 \text{ GB weights} > 80 \text{ GB HBM}
\]

We need at least \(140 / 80 \approx 1.75\) → \textbf{≥ 2 GPUs just to
hold the weights} (and that's before KV cache, which adds
\textasciitilde2.62 MB/token at FP16 (2.5 MiB) --- the 9.2K-token
canonical prompt needs \textasciitilde24.1 GB more (initial KV)).
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

The canonical 8×H100 host (640 GB total) fits the weights + KV
comfortably, which is why the single-host baseline in earlier chapters
works. But if the model grows (say to 400B) or owns more, the split
becomes mandatory:

\subsection{Tensor Parallelism: Splitting Weights Across
GPUs}\label{tensor-parallelism-splitting-weights-across-gpus}

With tensor parallelism, the 140 GB weight tensor is partitioned across
GPUs. Two H100 GPUs would hold 70 GB of weights each, before KV state,
activations and runtime overhead. A simplified tensor-parallel forward
pass exchanges partial activations rather than gathering the full
weights. For an eight-rank ring, one equal-sized all-reduce per layer
and no overlap, the bandwidth term is
\(T_\text{tp} \approx n_\text{layers} \times 2(N-1)/N \times M_\text{act}/B_\text{dir}\).
Here \(M_\text{act}\) is the full logical activation tensor in bytes
contributed by each rank to one collective; for a dense activation it is
token count times hidden dimension times bytes per element. The actual
number and shapes of collectives depend on the partitioning and kernels.
\(B_\text{dir}\) is directional effective bandwidth, not the sum of send
and receive peaks. H100 advertises 900 GB/s bidirectional per GPU,
corresponding to a 450 GB/s peak in each direction; achieved bandwidth
requires measurement. Step latency, contention and reduction work are
additional costs. \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

Tensor parallelism keeps weight shards resident and exchanges layer
results. In a row-parallel linear layer, ranks compute partial outputs
from their local weight shards; a reduction combines those partial
outputs. Other layouts can use reduce-scatter and all-gather
instead.\footnote{Row-parallel reduction and its collective mappings in
  NVIDIA Megatron-LM at pinned revision
  \texttt{b9d4e2b2c899ef7a77b482ff81d020b3d328d8ab} (retrieved
  2026-10-28 UTC; implementation observation outside the 2026-08-30
  snapshot, not an accelerator performance test):
  \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM/b9d4e2b2c899ef7a77b482ff81d020b3d328d8ab/megatron/core/tensor_parallel/mappings.py}
  and
  \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM/b9d4e2b2c899ef7a77b482ff81d020b3d328d8ab/megatron/core/tensor_parallel/layers.py}}
The buffer shape, communication precision, group size and number of
collective calls determine the byte budget. Ordinary TP decode does not
fetch the full model weights over the network again for every token.

Consider a small illustrative decode step with 16 active sequences, one
new token per sequence, hidden width 8,192, two-byte BF16 communication
and eight TP ranks. Assume two output all-reduces per transformer block
--- attention output and MLP --- 80 blocks, no sequence parallelism, and
a balanced point-to-point ring. (The bandwidth term above counts one
collective per layer; this budget's two-per-block assumption is why the
call count below is 160 rather than 80.) These are explicit teaching
inputs, not a measured model configuration.
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}

Each all-reduce has a logical input of

\[
M = 16 \times 8{,}192 \times 2 = 262{,}144\ \text{B} = 256\ \text{KiB}.
\]

The ring moves \(1.75M = 448\) KiB per rank sent and another 448 KiB
received --- the physical volume moved exceeds the logical payload
above.\footnote{Ring normalization 2(N-1)/N and the
  algorithmic/bus-bandwidth conventions: nccl-tests PERFORMANCE.md at
  pinned revision \texttt{afd59abab774a6a4b492eb344bd0c845a0b7bb4d}
  (retrieved 2026-10-27):
  \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/nccl-tests/afd59abab774a6a4b492eb344bd0c845a0b7bb4d/doc/PERFORMANCE.md}}
Across 160 calls (two per block × 80 blocks), each rank sends 70 MiB and
receives 70 MiB in that one decode step. The ideal byte term is about
0.163 ms at the 450 GB/s directional bandwidth above, or 2.936 ms at an
assumed 25 GB/s; neither is an inter-token-latency prediction. The
simple ring also contains 160 × 14 = 2,240 communication steps, plus
launch, compute dependencies and contention. At this message size,
latency and progress can matter more than the large-buffer bandwidth
score. This is the forward inference exchange, accumulated per layer ---
not a training backward pass, and not a whole-model weight transfer.
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}

The token population is the rows processed in this step, not the full
historical context length. Prefill may process many more rows. A
different sequence-parallel layout, fused kernel or offload path changes
the operation and its schedule. The budget therefore begins with the
deployed layer trace, not a fixed ``bytes per model'' rule. This
distinction also changes the economics: a faster nominal fabric may not
reduce the host count if launch overhead, compute or memory residency
remains binding.

\subsection{Pipeline Parallelism: Splitting Layers, Accepting
Bubbles}\label{pipeline-parallelism-splitting-layers-accepting-bubbles}

Pipeline parallelism puts stage \(P\) on GPU \(P\). The cost is the
\textbf{pipeline bubble}: while the first micro-batch fills the pipeline
and the last one drains, stages sit idle. With \(P\) stages and \(m\)
micro-batches, the steady-state \textbf{ideal speedup} over a single
serial pass is:

\[
\text{ideal speedup} = \frac{m \cdot P}{m + P - 1}
\]

The \(P{-}1\) term in the denominator is the bubble: the pipeline fills
and drains exactly once per \(m\)-batch pass, so \(P{-}1\) stage-slots
are wasted out of the \(m \cdot P\) slots each stage would otherwise
work. With \(P = 4\) stages and \(m = 8\) micro-batches, ideal
utilization is

\[
\frac{8 \cdot 4}{8 + 4 - 1} = \frac{32}{11} \approx 2.9\times \quad (\text{out of } 4\times)
\]

--- a meaningful bubble. Fewer stages or more micro-batches shrink it:
with \(m = 32\),

\[
\frac{32 \cdot 4}{32 + 4 - 1} = \frac{128}{35} \approx 3.66\times.
\]

The lesson: PP is communication-light (each stage only moves activation
boundaries across the fabric) but always pays a bubble that grows with
stage count. \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

\subsection{Data Parallelism: Replicating, Then Syncing
Gradients}\label{data-parallelism-replicating-then-syncing-gradients}

Data parallelism replicates the 140 GB model on every GPU. For the
gradient all-reduce: if the model has 140 GB of weights and we sync
gradients after each step across G GPUs, each GPU sends its gradient
tensor once per step. The gradient bytes are

\[
\text{grad bytes} = N_\text{params} \times \text{bytes/param} = 70 \times 10^9 \times 4 \text{ B} \approx 280 \text{ GB}
\]

per step (FP32 gradient; the all-reduce moves the gradient once, not
weights+gradients). As in \hyperref[chap:9]{Chapter 9}, a ring all-reduce moves
\(2\frac{N-1}{N}\) times the per-rank gradient through each rank, so for
\(N=8\) the per-rank traffic is \(1.75 \times 280 \approx 490\) GB and
the sync time is
\(T_\text{ring} = 2\frac{N-1}{N}\,\text{bytes} / B_\text{eff}\). At 25
GB/s (single 200Gb/s link) that is \texttt{490\ /\ 25\ ≈\ 19.6\ s} per
step --- far too slow if the step takes \textasciitilde1 s. At the H100
NVLink directional peak of 450 GB/s, the same ideal ring estimate is
\texttt{490/450\ ≈\ 1.09\ s}. The 900 GB/s bidirectional sum cannot
divide a send-only traffic count; this remains a communication model,
not proof of training-state fit. The sync cost, not compute, often caps
DP at small scale unless the interconnect is fast. \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

The 280 GB is the logical FP32 gradient for a full model replica, not a
feasible single-H100 allocation. In hybrid DP×TP training, a DP
communicator can join matching parameter shards from different replicas.
Its per-device buffer size and participating ranks replace the
full-replica logical inputs above; the TP group within each replica is a
different communicator
(\href{https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/user-guide/docs/usage/communicators.html}{NCCL
communicator definition}). \hyperref[chap:9]{Chapter 9}'s separate 140 GB example assumes
FP16 gradients, so the two examples must retain their different
precision bases.

Two more communication objects complete the picture. For expert
parallelism, the population is routed token copies rather than TP
partial outputs. With eight EP ranks, 256 local tokens per rank, top-2
routing, hidden width 8,192 and BF16, an illustrative uniform routing
model produces 8 MiB of route rows per rank. Seven eighths go remote:
dispatch sends and receives 7 MiB per rank. An equal-sized combine
doubles those totals to 14 MiB sent and 14 MiB received. This excludes
metadata, padding, compression, route deduplication and load skew. A hot
destination can dominate completion even when the average byte budget
fits. Specialized dispatch/combine implementations may exploit structure
that a fixed-count all-to-all does not expose.\footnote{Dispatch/combine
  structure in DeepSeek DeepEP at pinned revision
  \texttt{f6aa38fe06cd4a0489e7730365d4e0114970a2f3} (retrieved
  2026-10-27):
  \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/deepseek-ai/DeepEP/f6aa38fe06cd4a0489e7730365d4e0114970a2f3/README.md}}
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}

FSDP changes the object again. Its communication can gather a module's
parameter shards before computation and reduce-scatter that module's
gradients afterward. An illustrative 64 MiB unit split eight ways
requires 56 MiB sent and 56 MiB received per rank for a balanced ring
all-gather; the same full-size gradient gives the same per-rank
reduce-scatter volumes. This is a unit-sized calculation, not a claim
that every forward gathers the whole model or every optimizer
state.\footnote{FSDP \texttt{\_fully\_shard} implementation, PyTorch
  v2.9.0 tag (retrieved 2026-10-27):
  \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pytorch/pytorch/v2.9.0/torch/distributed/fsdp/_fully_shard/_fully_shard.py}}
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ANALYTICAL{]}}

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


Four habits anchor the architect:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Measure weight residency before choosing a split.}
  \texttt{bytes\ =\ params\ ×\ bytes\_per\_param} lands first; it
  decides whether TP/PP (memory) or DP (replication) is even possible.
\item
  \textbf{Measure the actual collective path.} For the H100 example, 900
  GB/s is the bidirectional peak sum, not a directional or sustained
  rate. Compare measured collective completion times at the deployed
  message shapes, rank placement and contention. A cluster's bandwidth
  depends on its links, rail count and topology; the fabric name alone
  does not determine the gap.
\item
  \textbf{Measure the bubble and the step time.} For PP, log the
  fraction of idle GPU time (bubble) vs compute. For DP, time the
  gradient all-reduce as a fraction of step time --- if it exceeds
  \textasciitilde20\%, it caps scaling.
\item
  \textbf{Measure communication overlap.} The best systems overlap
  all-reduce with the next forward/backward compute. Log the
  non-overlapped comm time --- that is the true cost, not the raw bytes.
\end{enumerate}

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Splitting with DP when the model doesn't fit.} DP replicates
  weights; if 140 GB \textgreater{} 80 GB, one DP replica doesn't even
  fit one GPU. Reach for TP/PP first.
\item
  \textbf{Using TP without budgeting its exchanges.} The same byte term
  is larger at 25 GB/s than at 450 GB/s, but whether it binds depends on
  message shape, call count and exposed latency. Tensor parallelism
  requires a communication path fast enough for its per-layer exchanges:
  NVLink is one implementation, not a requirement of the
  algorithm.\footnote{NVLink and NVSwitch are NVIDIA scale-up fabric
    examples, not requirements of tensor parallelism: the Gaudi 3 white
    paper documents a different integrated-network topology:
    \url{https://cdrdv2-public.intel.com/817486/gaudi-3-ai-accelerator-white-paper.pdf}
    ; PCIe-attached device backends appear in inspected engine sources
    --- llama.cpp SYCL backend at pinned revision
    \texttt{8ca4060b7e30e76baaa5670ee1ec1b760b7f8552}:
    \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/8ca4060b7e30e76baaa5670ee1ec1b760b7f8552/docs/backend/SYCL.md}
    ; vLLM XPU installation guide at pinned revision
    \texttt{5df63a7203cdb9328d824a27b682e77c7b9928be}:
    \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vllm-project/vllm/5df63a7203cdb9328d824a27b682e77c7b9928be/docs/getting_started/installation/gpu.xpu.inc.md}
    (retrieved 2026-10-28; outside the book's 2026-08-30 evidence
    cutoff).} PCIe or another fabric is viable only when measured
  collective cost leaves sufficient latency and throughput margin --- a
  measurement requirement, not a claim of universal performance for any
  fabric. The feasible split also depends on each rank's memory and
  supported kernels; a slower link can erase a capacity gain even when
  the weights fit.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring the pipeline bubble.} PP's idle stages waste real
  utilization; the bubble grows with stage count. It is not free.
\item
  \textbf{Quoting peak interconnect spec as sustained.} NVLink 900 GB/s
  is aggregate peak; real all-reduce achieves a fraction. Budget
  headroom.
\item
  \textbf{Treating parallelism as an either/or.} Real systems compose
  DP×TP×PP; the question is which dimension binds, not which one to use.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


The parallelism strategy is forced by the binding constraint, and that
constraint is usually \textbf{memory} for large models:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{A model that exceeds one GPU's memory} must split weights → TP
  (within a node, over the fabric that actually connects its GPUs ---
  the requirement is measured collective latency and throughput margin,
  not a vendor fabric name) or PP (across nodes, low comm). Which one
  depends on whether the model fits a node: fits one node → TP among its
  GPUs; spans nodes → PP across them, with TP inside each node.
\item
  \textbf{A workload with high throughput demand} (like the canonical
  \textasciitilde10 rps / 40 rps) → independent inference replicas after
  the state fits each replica's assigned devices. The canonical 70B fits
  across an 8×H100 node through intra-host model parallelism; additional
  replicas address fleet capacity. Gradient synchronization applies only
  to training or fine-tuning.
\item
  \textbf{MoE models} (if chosen per Ch3) push toward EP, because the
  full expert set must be resident (the active count sets compute per
  token, not residency) and EP shards the experts across GPUs.
\item
  \textbf{The canonical answer}: the book's \textasciitilde70B/8×H100
  baseline uses intra-host TP=8 to distribute the 140 GB weights and
  serving state. It requires model parallelism within the host, but not
  cross-host model parallelism. Eight ranks are the chosen baseline, not
  a minimum derived from weight bytes alone. TP, PP and independent
  replicas can then be composed according to measured capacity,
  communication and SLO constraints; their names do not establish a
  universal escalation order.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Optimal split for very large MoE or long-context models} is
  workload- and interconnect-dependent; the exact NVLink-vs-fabric
  crossover for a given model is unmeasured \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}} --- measure
  on the target cluster.
\item
  \textbf{2026 MoE scaling has moved the specialist/EP frontier.} Kimi
  K3 activates just 16 of 896 routed experts per token (2.8T total /
  104B active) via its Stable LatentMoE {[}1P: arXiv 2607.24653{]};
  Qwen3.8-Flash-Next routes 10+1 of 512 experts (125B total / 6B active)
  {[}1P: HF Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next{]}. These extreme sparsities (16/896
  ≈ 1.8\% expert activation) are why expert-parallel and fused
  cross-expert comm kernels (e.g.~MoE expert parallelism with fused
  compute/communication, as in DeepSeek's DeepEP/TileLang infra) are
  increasingly central to serving large MoE fleets {[}1P: arXiv
  2606.19348{]}. For the architect this changes the serving calculus: a
  frontier MoE's activated footprint (Kimi 104B, Qwen 6B) can be
  dramatically smaller than its total size, so the \emph{compute}
  decision is made against active parameters --- but the
  \emph{memory-bound} EP decision (how many experts must be resident) is
  made against \textbf{total} parameters, since the full expert set
  occupies HBM even when only a fraction is routed per token.
\item
  \textbf{Communication-overlap efficiency} in real kernels is hard to
  predict from spec; sustained all-reduce throughput is a
  \textbf{\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}} until measured on the target cluster.
\item
  \textbf{The right parallelism for mixed prefill/decode (P/D
  disaggregated) serving} --- where the prefill pool and decode pool may
  want different splits --- is an active research area and a
  \textbf{\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}} for this book.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: Diagnosing Why the Grown 400B MoE Is
Slow}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-diagnosing-why-the-grown-400b-moe-is-slow}

An architect inherits a deployment where the internal Q\&A model (from
Ch1's mini-case) has been fine-tuned and grown to a 400B-parameter MoE.
The team reports ``parallelism is killing us --- everything is slow.''
The architect applies this chapter's lens. First, weight residency: a
400B model needs \textasciitilde{}\texttt{400e9\ ×2\ bytes\ =\ 800\ GB}
even before KV --- that is \textasciitilde10×H100 for weights alone
(\textasciitilde11 once KV/workspace is included), far beyond one node.
\textbf{The model necessarily spans nodes; the architect must choose an
inter-node decomposition} --- often PP and/or EP for MoE --- while
avoiding expensive cross-node TP where possible (TP benefits from a
tightly coupled NVLink-style interconnect; across a slower network its
per-layer all-reduce cost grows fast). Within each node, the MoE active
weights are small, so the team uses EP to shard experts + TP inside the
node. The architect measures the interconnect: the cross-node PP
boundary runs over the cluster fabric (not NVLink), so they minimize the
number of PP stages to shrink the bubble, and they verify the DP
gradient-sync (if fine-tuning) stays under \textasciitilde20\% of step
time. The result: a composed PP × EP × TP configuration whose binding
constraints (memory across nodes + expert routing) are each addressed by
the strategy that trades the right bandwidth. The team's ``parallelism
is slow'' becomes a precise, diagnosed, and fixed split.
