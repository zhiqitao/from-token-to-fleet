\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

Before we can size a model, a context window, or a serving budget, we
have to know what the input actually is. After this chapter we should be
able to reason about an AI system's \textbf{smallest unit of cost and
meaning} --- the token --- and to translate between human units (a
sentence, a page, a word) and the machine unit every architecture
decision is priced against. This is the first rung of the token→fleet
spine: everything downstream (memory, compute, latency, cost) is
ultimately a statement about \emph{tokens}.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


A \textbf{token} is the atomic unit of \emph{text} that a language model
reads and writes. It is not necessarily a word, a character, or a byte
--- it is a vocabulary item, often a byte sequence, chosen by the
model's \emph{tokenizer}. A single token can be a whole word
(\texttt{handbook}), a common subword (\texttt{hand}), a single
character, a short punctuation+space pair, or, in edge cases, a single
byte (a fragment of a character). (This chapter scopes to
\emph{text-tokenized} autoregressive models. Modern multimodal systems
also use non-text token-like representations --- image patches, audio
frames --- that are not produced by a text tokenizer; for those, ``token
count'' is a different quantity from text-token count and does not map
one-to-one onto model compute the way it does here.)

The disconnection from human units is the first thing an architect has
to internalize: \textbf{cost and capacity in an LLM are measured in
tokens, not words or characters}, because the workload accounting is
token-based --- embedding lookup, attention arithmetic, KV cache memory,
and decode time are all \emph{counted} in tokens. That does not mean
every operation has a fixed per-token price: the cost per token varies
by phase (prefill vs decode), by operating point (batch size, context
length), and by hardware utilization, and attention work grows
nonlinearly with context. The token is the unit we count and budget in;
the per-token cost is what the later chapters derive. This chapter
exists to make that switch automatic.

\subsection{Tokenization}\label{tokenization}

Tokenization splits a string into an ordered list of integer IDs from
the model's \textbf{vocabulary} (typically tens of thousands, up to
\textasciitilde256k entries). The split is data-driven: learned from a
large corpus, byte-pair encoding (BPE) repeatedly merges the most
frequent adjacent byte/subword pairs until the vocabulary is built, so
the boundaries are wherever the corpus statistics happened to fall ---
not where a grammarian would put them. Two practical consequences
follow. First, text is generally \textbf{recoverable} from its token IDs
under the specified tokenizer (the detokenizer inverts the encoding),
but the \emph{segmentation} does not correspond cleanly to words or
semantics: the characters of \texttt{architecture} and
\texttt{architect} may or may not share a token depending on corpus
frequency. The encode/decode round-trip is usually lossless for the
byte-level mapping, but the \emph{token boundaries} are not semantically
meaningful. Second, token counts are \textbf{not proportional to
character counts in any fixed way}. \mbox{{[}2°{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}FACT{]}} A high-frequency
English word is often one token (verifiable on most vendor tokenizer
demos --- e.g.~common words like \emph{the}, \emph{we}, \emph{run}
render as single tokens); a rare technical term, a long digit string, or
dense code is frequently several. The ``one token ≈ 4 characters'' rule
of thumb is for rough estimation of general-English prose, never a law
--- we confirm against the model's own tokenizer before pricing
anything.

\subsection{Context window}\label{context-window}

The \textbf{context window} is the maximum sequence length the model can
condition on at a given generation step --- that is, the total of the
retained input/prefix and the tokens generated so far. It is a hard
\emph{service} constraint at the configured limit: exceed it and the
request fails or must be truncated. (This is the engine-configured
maximum, not a law of physics --- some models use sliding-window or
rolling attention that retains only a bounded recent context, and a few
support positional-scaling or provider-side context extension, so the
\emph{model's} positional capability and the \emph{serving stack's}
configured cap can differ; the distinction is developed in Ch. 7 and Ch.
22.) Current-gen model line-ups vary widely by vendor: older or
small-tier deployments may still ship windows of roughly 8K tokens,
while current frontier cards commonly advertise hundreds of thousands of
tokens up to a million-plus --- for example, the Qwen3.8-Flash-Next card
states a native 262K-token window with YaRN extension to 1M, and the
GLM-5.3-Flash and DeepSeek-V4 cards state 1M (\mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}FACT{]}} model
cards: HF Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next; HF zai-org/GLM-5.3-Flash; DeepSeek-V4
technical report, arXiv 2606.19348, and model card HF
deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro --- all accessed 2026-08-30, the book's
evidence cutoff; Appendix A §10); the 8K end is an older-generation and
small-tier deployment observation rather than a published figure for a
current frontier model --- this book's reading of the vendor line-ups
above, not a vendor statement \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}. The
advertised number is a fixed, checkable property of the model we choose
--- verify it on the card, never assume it from the family name. Note
there are three related but distinct limits: the \emph{model-advertised
maximum} (the card's window), the \emph{engine-configured maximum} (what
the serving stack is set to allow, which can be lower to bound memory),
and the \emph{request-level available input budget} (the advertised
window minus the reserved generation budget and any positional-scaling
or provider-API cap). The context window is one of the first numbers we
will be quoted, and one of the easiest to overcommit against. But it is
a \emph{configured maximum}, not a per-request charge: a 9.2K request in
a 128K window is computed, cached, and billed on the \textasciitilde9.2K
tokens actually used, not on the full 128K. What the window \emph{does}
bound is the worst case --- the memory a single request could reserve
and the admission capacity the engine must hold --- and some engines
reserve or pre-allocate against that maximum (a policy choice that
varies by engine), while others allocate only for the tokens actually
processed. So the window sets the ceiling on reservation and admission,
not the floor on per-request cost; the cost tracks the tokens actually
cached and billed (developed in Ch. 7 and Ch. 16).

\subsection{Embeddings}\label{embeddings}

Text enters the model as token IDs, but the model does arithmetic on
vectors. The first such vector is the \textbf{token embedding}: a
learned lookup table maps each discrete token id to a dense vector in a
high-dimensional space, and the transformer operates on those vectors.
Two distinct widths must not be conflated: the \textbf{token-embedding
width} (the model's hidden dimension, e.g.~4096 for LLaMA-7B, 8192 for
LLaMA-2/3 70B and for the canonical 70B reference, 12288 for a
GPT-3-class 175B model) and the \textbf{retrieval-embedding width} (the
dimension of a sentence/document embedding produced by a separate
encoder for RAG, typically 384--1536 for dedicated retrieval models, or
the full hidden dim if the LLM itself is used as the encoder). The ``128
to a few thousand'' range applies to \emph{retrieval} embeddings; the
\emph{token} embedding width is architecture-dependent and for modern
LLMs is \emph{illustratively} in the 4096--16384 range (not an
exhaustive bound across all models). Two other vector objects are
distinct from the token embedding and should not be merged with it:
\textbf{positional encodings} (which encode a token's position in the
sequence, added to or modulating the embedding) and \textbf{contextual
hidden states} (the per-layer, per-position activations the transformer
produces, which depend on the whole context, not just the token id). The
token embedding maps each discrete token id to a learned continuous
vector; relationships in that learned space emerge from training but
should not be interpreted as a simple human-semantic distance metric.

For the architect the practical facts are: embeddings live in a lookup
table sized \texttt{vocab\ ×\ dim}, they account for a real but small
slice of a model's parameter count (a 100k × 4096 table holds 100,000 ×
4,096 ≈ 409.6M ≈ 0.4B params --- a rounding error next to a 70B model,
but not zero), and they are the first thing a \emph{vocabulary mismatch}
or a \emph{re-tokenized prompt} can silently change.

\subsection{Attention}\label{attention}

Attention is the mechanism by which each token's representation is
recomputed as a weighted blend of the representations of all tokens
before it in the context. The weights come from a similarity (query·key)
between the \emph{current} token and every \emph{prior} token,
normalized, and used to mix the value vectors. It is the mechanism that
makes a model ``pay attention to'' relevant earlier tokens when
producing the next one.

The architect-relevant fact: attention's cost scales
\textbf{quadratically with context length} in the full-attention regime
--- the number of (query, key) position pairs within the sequence grows
as \texttt{L²}. That quadratic term is one of the two biggest reasons
context windows feel expensive (the other being KV-cache memory growth),
and it is the pivot for every optimization downstream: sparse/linear
attention and retrieval cut the quadratic prefill term, while KV caching
separately avoids recomputing the prefix's K/V during decode.

One precision matters before the mental model hardens: the \texttt{L²}
term is a \textbf{prefill} cost --- it describes computing attention
across all position pairs in one pass over the full input. During
\textbf{decode}, each generated token attends once to the growing
prefix, so the per-token step is a \emph{linear} cost in context length
--- an attention term that \emph{is} per-step compute --- and that term
holds a \emph{constant} arithmetic intensity, because its FLOPs and the
KV bytes it reads both grow linearly in L: \textasciitilde1 FLOP/byte
for full MHA at FP16 --- 4·n\_layers·L·d FLOPs (two matmuls at two FLOPs
each) against 4·n\_layers·L·d bytes (two FP16 tensors at two bytes each)
--- far below the H100 roofline ridge of \textasciitilde295 FLOP/byte
(Ch. 2, Ch. 8). The length also shows up in the \emph{growing KV cache
memory footprint} (per-token key/values). The practical read is
therefore \emph{``long context is quadratic in prefill compute, and
linear-but-memory-growing in decode''} --- a distinction as important as
the quadratic fact itself, and one we return to in \hyperref[chap:2]{Chapter 2} (prefill
and decode regimes), \hyperref[chap:7]{Chapter 7} (memory arithmetic) and \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8}
(roofline). Keeping prefill and decode separate is what prevents the
over-simple ``long context = quadratic inference'' once we optimize
context in later chapters.

One architectural consequence to hold now: \emph{which layers emit a
per-token cache is a property of the attention architecture}, not the
context length alone --- dense attention emits a key/value per token on
every layer, while hybrid/linear-attention layers (Mamba-style state,
linear-attention kernels) hold a \emph{constant-size} recurrent state
that does not grow with length. A third family --- \textbf{compressed
per-token KV}: MLA-style latent attention, grouped-query attention (GQA)
and multi-query attention (MQA) head sharing --- still stores a
per-token entry, so its cache \emph{does} grow with token count, but at
fewer bytes/token than full MHA. These are distinct mechanisms:
constant-state (no per-token growth) versus reduced-width per-token
(per-token growth, smaller footprint). The canonical three-row taxonomy,
used by name in Ch. 7 (mechanism inventory) and Appendix A rather than

\subsection{KV cache as a concept}\label{kv-cache-as-a-concept}

When the model generates one token at a time, it recomputes the same
prefix repeatedly unless it saves the intermediate results. The
\textbf{KV cache} (key--value cache) is the stored key and value tensors
for every token in the context, so that generating the next token only
computes the new token's query and attends against all cached K/V,
rather than re-deriving the whole prefix. It is the reason ``long
conversations are expensive in memory, not just in compute.''

KV cache size per token scales with

\[
KV_{\text{per-token}} = 2 \times n_\text{layers} \times n_{\text{KV-heads}} \times d_\text{head} \times \text{bytes-per-value}
\]

(one key and one value per layer per \textbf{KV head}). For \textbf{full
multi-head attention (MHA)} the KV-heads are all the query heads, so
\texttt{n\_KV-heads\ ×\ d\_head\ =\ d\_hidden} and the formula reduces
to the simpler introductory form
\texttt{2\ ×\ n\_layers\ ×\ d\_hidden\ ×\ bytes-per-value}. The general
form matters once we meet \textbf{grouped-query (GQA)} and
\textbf{multi-query (MQA)} attention, which share a small number of
KV-heads across many query heads and can make the cache several times
smaller than the full-MHA figure (Ch. 7) --- so \texttt{d\_hidden} alone
is \emph{not} the correct KV width for those architectures.
(\emph{Units:} MiB = 2\^{}20 bytes and MB = 10\^{}6 bytes; every MB
figure below is a decimal-megabyte conversion of the exact byte count,
consistent with the book's SI convention of K = 1,000 and M = 1,000,000
(Ch. 4).) It is on the order of \textbf{\textasciitilde256 KiB (0.26 MB)
per token at 8-bit for a 7B-class model (32 layers, d\_hidden 4096,
full-MHA) and \textasciitilde1.25 MiB (1.31 MB) per token at 8-bit
(\textasciitilde2.5 MiB, 2.62 MB at FP16) for a 70B-class model (80
layers, d\_hidden 8192, full-MHA)} --- two reference geometries chosen
for the arithmetic, not claims about a specific vendor model (Ch. 4,
\hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2} marks the canonical 70B geometry a constructed reference, not
a named vendor model). The arithmetic is the formula above with one byte
per value: 2 × 32 × 4096 = 262,144 B (= 256 KiB = 0.26 MB) and 2 × 80 ×
8192 = 1,310,720 B (= 1.25 MiB = 1.31 MB). That 8-bit figure is the
\emph{raw tensor byte-halving} of the FP16 value: 1.31 MB/token vs 2.62
MB/token, exactly 50\%, with any block-allocation/metadata overhead NOT
vendor-measured here and to be filled by a deployment benchmark.
\textbf{The canonical scenario's KV constant is the FP16 figure,
\textasciitilde2.62 MB/token (Ch. 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}; Ch. 7); the 8-bit number
is the FP8 variant, exactly half of it.} The vLLM FP8-KV blog post's
often-quoted `54\%' --- from its Llama-3.1-8B Table 3 (vLLM blog, post
dated 2026-04-22 --- inside the 2026-08-30 evidence cutoff; retrieved
2026-11-15, an access date only, since access dates are not evidence
dates, so no addendum revision applies) \mbox{{[}S6{]}} --- is, on this book's
reading of that table, a decode inter-token-latency (ITL) slope, not a
memory ratio or footprint coefficient \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}. That
reading is the book's own, not a vendor-stated interpretation; re-verify
it against the post. At 9.2K input tokens the canonical FP16 KV per

9,200 × 2,621,440 B = 24,117,248,000 B ≈ 24.12 GB --- and the 8-bit raw

Either way, a long context (say 32K tokens) alone can be tens of GB of
memory. A first non-obvious architectural lesson hides here: \emph{MoE
expert sparsity alone does not reduce per-token KV state.} A
Mixture-of-Experts model activates only a fraction of its neurons per
token, which saves compute, but the attention layers still process every
token densely and emit a key/value per token --- so MoE shrinks
compute-per-token, not memory-per-context. What actually determines KV
memory is the \emph{attention architecture and cache policy}, not the
expert count: GQA/MQA shrink the per-token K/V width,
hybrid/linear/compressed-latent attention change how much context is
retained, sliding-window caps some layers' retained context, and
quantization (FP8) reduces the bytes per element without stopping the
linear growth. This is a controlled comparison --- it holds the
attention geometry, context length, and cache dtype fixed; an MoE model
that also uses GQA or a different attention mechanism can have a much
smaller KV footprint than a dense full-MHA baseline. The distinction ---
compute-sparsity vs memory-sparsity --- is one an architect has to get
exactly right, and it is why ``the KV problem'' is an
attention-architecture question more than a model-size question
(developed fully in Ch. 3 and Ch. 7). We deliberately keep this to
concept + order of magnitude here; \hyperref[chap:7]{Chapter 7} (Memory) does the verified
arithmetic against the canonical scenario.

A token is best understood as a \textbf{metered unit of model I/O}, the
way electricity is metered by the kilowatt-hour --- important not
because a single kilowatt-hour is meaningful by itself, but because
\emph{every} downstream cost and capacity number is denominated in
tokens --- or, for non-autoregressive generation, in model evaluations
(§2.1.2; §3.1.1 and Appendix A §9A). (It is a computational input unit,
not a semantic unit: two tokenizers can render the same semantic content
in different token counts, and a reasoning model can spend very
different compute per visible token.)

We do not need to hold all of this as a practitioner. The durable mental
model is: \textbf{tokens are the interface between a human's request and
a machine's arithmetic}, and \emph{anything} we are asked to size or
price in an LLM system reduces, one way or another, to ``how many
tokens, in what context, with what precision.'' That holds for the
autoregressive text path this chapter scopes to; for non-AR generation
the same reasoning runs on model evaluations instead of output tokens;
§2.1.2 (the work unit changes with the generator) and §3.1.1
(diffusion/LLaDA-style) carry the extension.

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


The canonical scenario (Ch. 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2} --- the one set of numbers
every chapter cites) is an enterprise Q\&A system: \textasciitilde2,000
registered users, \textasciitilde10 requests/s average, prompt of
\textasciitilde1,200 tokens plus \textasciitilde8K retrieved context
(\textasciitilde9.2K input), \textasciitilde300-token output, on a
70B-class dense model. We reuse those numbers everywhere, so let us read
one concrete property off them here.

\emph{Worked example, not reference.} If average request is
\textasciitilde9.2K input + \textasciitilde300 output ≈ \textbf{9.5K

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Input tokens/s}: 9,200 tokens/req × 10 req/s = \textbf{92,000

  \textbf{\textasciitilde368,000 input tokens/s}).
\item
  \textbf{Output tokens/s}: 300 ×10 = \textbf{3,000 tokens/s} output
  (peaks at \textasciitilde40 req/s → \textbf{\textasciitilde12,000

\item
  Roughly \textbf{30× more input tokens than output tokens} in this

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


For this concept chapter, measurement is about \textbf{counting tokens
correctly}, because a miscount at the input silently misprices
everything downstream. Three things an architect can actually check:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Actual token count, not the rule of thumb.} Run the model's
  \emph{own tokenizer} on representative prompts. The ``4 chars ≈ 1
  token'' heuristic is for estimation only; real counts differ by
  language, formatting, code, and tokenizer version.
\item
  \textbf{Input vs output split.} Measure both legs of the request
  (prompt tokens and generated tokens), because they land on different
  bottlenecks --- input on prefill compute and KV-cache capacity, output
  on decode bandwidth --- and on different cost line items.
\item
  \textbf{Peak vs average context.} Log the distribution of context
  lengths, not just the mean. A workload that averages 9.2K input tokens
  but peaks far higher (illustrative variant, say 32K) has a very
  different KV cache and latency profile.
\end{enumerate}

This measurement habit is the token-layer answer to the book's recurring
question, ``what would I actually measure here?'' --- we measure token
counts and their distribution, at the edge, before any architecture
decision is made.

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Assuming one token ≈ one word.} A token is often a \emph{word
  fragment}, not a whole word: a high-frequency English word is
  frequently one token, but a longer or rarer word is usually split into
  several subword tokens, and code, numerics, and non-Latin scripts can
  run 2--5× the naive word-count estimate (rule of thumb; the multiplier
  is tokenizer- and language-specific) {[}ILLUSTRATIVE: industry rule of
  thumb, no named survey or benchmark{]}\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}. The mapping is
  tokenizer-dependent (it depends on the corpus the tokenizer was
  trained on), so the ``one token ≈ 4 characters'' rule of thumb is for
  rough estimation of general-English prose only --- confirm against the
  model's own tokenizer before pricing anything.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring the input/output asymmetry.} Treating a request as
  ``one unit'' hides that input-heavy workloads tend toward high prefill
  compute and KV-capacity pressure, while output-heavy ones are
  decode-dominant (run-time per generated token) --- opposite
  bottlenecks with opposite fixes. (Note: ``capacity pressure'' is a
  memory-\emph{capacity} constraint, not a memory-\emph{bandwidth}
  bound; see § 7.3, the concurrency ceiling, and § 8.2, the roofline
  diagnostic that separates memory-capacity-constrained from
  memory-bandwidth-bound.)
\item
  \textbf{Quoting context window as free capacity.} The window is an
  upper bound, not a recommendation; using it fully is expensive.
  Keeping a 9.2K average inside a 128K window still costs for the length
  we actually use.
\item
  \textbf{Trusting a vendor's tokenizer count without checking.}
  Tokenizer versions change and report differently; measure on
  \emph{our} traffic, not the marketing figure.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


Whatever we learned here, file it under \textbf{``can I size an input
before I buy capacity?''} Concretely, the decision this chapter enables
is: \emph{estimate tokens-per-second (input and output separately), and
therefore tokens-per-second capacity and cost, before choosing a model
or a serving configuration.} It is the load-bearing input to model
selection (Ch. 5), the memory budget (Ch. 7), the compute budget (Ch.
8), and the token economics (Ch. 16). No downstream chapter runs without
a token count to price against --- this one gives us the unit.

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


\emph{As of 2026-08:} tokenizer internals are rarely published in full,
so exact per-token behavior for a given model is often only empirically
observable, not specified (--- tokenizer construction details,
e.g.~exact BPE merge rules for commercial models, are not fully public).
Whether a fully learned, near-optimal tokenization for \emph{our} domain
can beat the generic vocabulary by a repeatable, quantified margin is
still an open, workload-dependent question. And the interaction between
tokenizer choice and downstream reasoning quality is not yet crisply
characterized --- we flag it as an open area rather than a settled fact.

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: The Internal Q\&A Tool, From First
Principles}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-the-internal-qa-tool-from-first-principles}

\emph{(Chains the continuous scenario --- this is its first appearance:
the canonical enterprise Q\&A over internal documents, sized at Ch. 4,

separate population input, not a projection from this one; it is worked

An architect is pulled into an early-stage design conversation about a
new internal Q\&A tool. There is no model, no server, no budget --- just
the raw ask: ``We have thousands of employees who want to ask questions
over our internal documents.'' Before any architecture can be defended,
we have to turn that vague desire into a \emph{machine-denominated}
statement.

From the token-level work alone (which we worked through in this
chapter), the architect can already establish: the unit is tokens; the
request shape will be prompt + retrieved context + output; the working
hypothesis is input-heavy (Ch. 4 quantifies it); and the first number to
lock down is tokens-per-request, because every downstream decision
(which model fits, how much memory, what latency is possible) is priced
against it. The specific sizing --- turning ``thousands of employees''
into \textasciitilde2,000 registered users, \textasciitilde10 req/s,
\textasciitilde9.2K input tokens --- is \hyperref[chap:4]{Chapter 4}'s job (workload
anatomy). Here the point is narrower and sharper: \emph{we can now speak
the system's currency}, and that is a prerequisite for every
architecture question that follows.
