\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

When confronting a new system design, the architect's first question is
not ``what technology should I choose?'' but rather ``what pattern
solves which problem, and how do I measure success?'' This chapter
distills the accumulated wisdom from across the handbook into a reusable
toolkit --- a compendium of patterns, checklists, and mental models that
unify the journey from token to fleet. Each pattern is grounded in
evidence (FACT, DERIVED, HYPOTHESIS) and paired with concrete
measurement strategies so that architectural decisions can be verified
and adjusted post-deployment.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


The Solution Architect's Toolkit answers three concerns --- scalability,
reliability, and observability --- and is organized as pattern templates
plus measurement rather than as three separate taxonomies. Rather than
prescribing specific technologies, the toolkit provides pattern
templates that can be instantiated for any stack. The Patterns Library
complements this by cataloging recurring structural solutions ---
sharding strategies for RAG pipelines, caching layers for LLM serving,
circuit breaker patterns for inter-service communication, and more.
Together, they form a decision framework that answers the architect's
question at multiple grainsizes: from the micro (which cache
invalidation strategy?) to the macro (how does sharding affect
fleet-wide latency?).

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


The central mental model is the \textbf{Pattern-Measurement-Feedback
Loop}. Every pattern in the library is paired with:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{FACT} --- a verified runtime quantity (e.g., request rate,
  cache hit ratio, query latency p99)
\item
  \textbf{DERIVED} --- a computed quantity from FACTs (e.g., throughput
  per shard, cost per query, fleet-wide rps)
\item
  \textbf{HYPOTHESIS} --- a predictive claim about what changing the
  pattern will affect (e.g., ``increasing replication factor from 3 to 5
  will reduce tail latency by X\%'')
\end{itemize}

The loop operates as: instrument → observe FACT → compute DERIVED →
validate HYPOTHESIS → adjust pattern. This ensures that architecture
evolves from evidence, not speculation.

\textbf{What a \mbox{{[}1P{]}} tag does and does not mean.} The statuses above
(FACT / DERIVED / HYPOTHESIS) describe what a number \emph{claims to
be}; a separate \emph{provenance} axis describes \textbf{where it came
from}. \textbf{\mbox{{[}1P{]}}} marks a first-party \textbf{source} --- the
original method paper authored by the method's creators, or a
hardware-vendor specification (technical report, model card, or official
documentation). It names \emph{whose} claim a number is; it does
\textbf{not} by itself assert a validated deployment benchmark. Keep the
four categories distinct: (a) an original method paper by the method's
creators, (b) a hardware-vendor specification, (c) a third-party
evaluation (the book's \mbox{{[}2°{]}} secondary provenance), and (d) an own
measured run (\mbox{{[}LAB{]}}/\mbox{{[}MEASURED{]}} --- Axis-1 provenance plus the
Axis-3 measurement status, both defined in the Sources, Method, and
Reproducibility chapter). Of these, \mbox{{[}1P{]}} covers (a) and (b) only ---
a first-party source is a vendor or paper author's figure, which must be
re-verified against the current source or re-measured on the architect's
own hardware before it is treated as a validated deployment result. Note
that the canonical 70B teaching model is \emph{not} a \mbox{{[}1P{]}} model:
being constructed (Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}) it carries
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}, reserving \mbox{{[}1P{]}} for named,
traceable first-party specifications.

\ntsection{Worked Example --- Capstone
Scenario}\label{worked-example-capstone-scenario}


Take the canonical deployment: \textasciitilde2,000 registered users
with \textasciitilde5\% concurrently active at peak (\textasciitilde100
concurrent \emph{users}, a population measure), RAG Q\&A over a 70B
dense FP16 model. The architect faces three interlocked questions: how
to scale inference, how to cache retrieved context, and how to keep the
model-service latency under its SLO --- here the model-service latency
SLO is defined as \textbf{time-to-first-token (TTFT)}, and for the
\emph{retrieved-context/short-prompt} leg of this scenario we adopt a
\emph{stricter} p99 ≤ 800 ms TTFT target (a scenario input, not the
canonical p95-TTFT ≤ 2 s of Ch 4: the capstone deliberately uses a
harder tail target to exercise the patterns). This is a \textbf{TTFT}
budget, not an end‑to‑end one --- the canonical request's total
end‑to‑end time is \textasciitilde8.6 s (1.08 s prefill +
\textasciitilde7.5 s decode of 300 output tokens), and the per‑host
ceiling is \textasciitilde2.0 req/s (Ch 17/20), both of which still hold
here. Do not read the sub‑second capstone figures as end‑to‑end request
latency. (All the other numbers below are the canonical values
established in Parts I--V --- Ch 4's workload, Ch 7/15's KV and memory
figures, Ch 17's fleet sizing --- recalled here rather than re-derived,
and this capstone shows how the toolkit's patterns compose them.)

\textbf{Pattern A --- Inference Sharding.} \mbox{{[}canonical
scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} --- model FLOPs/token = 2N = 1.4 × 10¹¹; KV
bytes/token = 2.62 MB (Ch 7, 22). The 70B model at FP16 requires
\textasciitilde140 GB of weights \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} --- 70
× 10⁹ parameters × 2 B/parameter = 140 GB, the footprint Ch 22 and §25.4
also derive. Following the canonical setup, one serving host is an
8×H100 80 GB node, which dedicates \textasciitilde140 GB to weights and
leaves the rest of the 640 GB pool for KV cache and runtime.
\textbf{Sharding here is \emph{baseline configuration}, not an
incremental optimization (R-04):} the 140 GB weights are already
tensor-parallel across the 8 H100s in the baseline, so the capstone
makes no change to the parallelism configuration between before and
after --- it does not claim sharding as a gain it did not actually
introduce. (An actual control intervention would name a change such as
TP2→TP8, a replica count, a parallelism placement, or a batch/operator
configuration, and would recompute latency, communication and memory for
both; the capstone does not do that, so sharding is configuration, not a
new lever.) With tensor parallelism across the 8 H100s, a single host
serves the model and can hold \textasciitilde17.5 concurrent 9,500-token
requests within the canonical \textasciitilde436 GB KV budget from
\hyperref[chap:15]{Chapter 15}. \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} --- with the 8-way
tensor parallelism across the node, decode latency is bandwidth-bound in
the low-batch decode regime of Ch. 8 (a first-order regime, not a
universal law: whether decode binds on bandwidth or compute depends on
batch size, context precision, kernel, and hardware), so per-token
throughput scales with the node's aggregate memory bandwidth rather than
8× the single-GPU rate. \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}} ---
adding a second host and load-balancing requests across two replicas
changes per-request latency by a small amount; this is an
\textbf{untested scenario hypothesis to be evaluated over an actual
offered-load interval}, not a basis for rejecting the second host (see
``Alternatives considered'').

\textbf{Pattern B --- Semantic Response Cache.}
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} --- 35\% of queries repeat within
5-min windows (a scenario input to be measured on real traffic). A cache
stores the completed answer (the retrieved context and the generated
response). \textbf{Cache key and isolation (R-03/R-13):} a raw question
\emph{hash} implements only an \textbf{exact-match response cache} ---
it returns a cached answer only when the query string matches
byte-for-byte, and it cannot serve semantic similarity (a true semantic
cache needs an embedding/nearest-neighbour lookup, which still incurs
encoding and lookup cost on hits). The capstone adopts exactly that
embedding/nearest-neighbour variant: its hit path pays query encoding +
cache lookup (the \textasciitilde50 ms term of §26.8 Step 2) ---
replacing corpus retrieval --- and skips the engine's prefill/decode ---
which is why the component earns the \emph{semantic} label in the
capstone. On top of the match method, the cache key must carry
\textbf{authorization and corpus-version isolation}, because the
canonical workload contains privacy-sensitive documents and
organizational access constraints (Ch 4): two callers asking the same
question may not be authorized to receive the same source passages or
answer. A safe example key is
\texttt{(tenant,\ authorization-scope\ digest,\ normalized\ query\ or\ semantic\ key,\ corpus/index\ snapshot,\ policy/model/prompt\ revision)},
with a recheck at serving time and explicit invalidation on document
permission change, deletion/withdrawal, index refresh, and source-update
events. Because the capstone describes that lookup mechanism and its hit
cost, the component earns the \emph{semantic} label there; a raw-hash
implementation without it must still be called an \textbf{exact-match
response cache}, not a semantic one (R-13). The five-minute TTL does
\textbf{not} repair an authorization mismatch: a cross-user hit could
leak another user's authorized response or return stale, now-disallowed
information, so secret-bearing responses must not be cached across
privilege boundaries (R-03). \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} --- a
cache hit ratio of 35\% reduces the number of requests that reach the
LLM engine by 35\%, lowering the generation work; the
embedding/retrieval step is also skipped for exact-match hits (the
raw-hash variant; the capstone's semantic variant instead pays encoding
+ cache lookup on hits --- replacing corpus retrieval --- and skips the
engine). \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}} --- expanding the
cache TTL from 5 to 15 minutes increases hit ratio to
\textasciitilde55\% with negligible staleness risk, because user
questions in a support session repeat within the same session (to be
validated, not assumed).

\textbf{Pattern C --- Circuit Breaker.}
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} --- model-service latency p99
degrades past the 800 ms SLO under concurrent load; downstream network
jitter p99 = 120 ms (scenario inputs to be measured). \mbox{{[}canonical
scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} --- the diagnosed symptom: when a host's
arrival rate exceeds the \textasciitilde2.0 req/s per-host ceiling (Ch
17/20) --- as the canonical \textasciitilde40 rps peak (Ch 4) would if
concentrated on one host --- the request queue grows and queueing delay
alone pushes TTFT p99 past the 800 ms SLO, the condition Pattern C
exists for; \hyperref[tab:26.2]{Table 26-2}'s \textasciitilde7\% fallback rate is the modeled
share of requests shed to the intent classifier during such excursions
(FACT target \textless{} 10\%). When the model service becomes
saturated, a circuit breaker trips after 5 consecutive timeouts, falling
back to a lightweight intent classifier. \textbf{Fallback is a degraded
outcome, not a successful RAG answer (R-10):} a fast answer from an
intent classifier is not equivalent to a correct full
retrieval-augmented answer, so the capstone does not count a
breaker-shed request as an SLO success --- the p99 and cost denominators
are \emph{completed useful requests}, and the \textasciitilde7\%
fallback share is labeled a separately-tracked degraded outcome (the
book's own Ch 6/14 establish that the right denominator is completed
useful requests, not any quick output).
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} --- circuit breaker activation
reduces tail latency for remaining requests by 35\% because the system
sheds load before the model queue fills. \mbox{{[}canonical
scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}} --- a grace-period of 2 seconds before
auto-recovery prevents thrashing when load spikes are transient.

\textbf{Capstone Execution.} The architect instruments all three
scenario quantities, observes the derived quantities in real time, and
validates each HYPOTHESIS against measured traffic. The result: one
8×H100 serving host + a 35\% semantic response cache + circuit breaker
yields an illustrative \textbf{p99 TTFT ≈ 250 ms} versus the
\textasciitilde450 ms uncached single-node baseline (one 8×H100 host,
already tensor-parallel across its 8 GPUs) --- the single capstone
configuration, derived step by step in §26.8 and recorded as \hyperref[tab:26.2]{Table 26-2}
(scenario outcome, not a measured benchmark). \textbf{Cost is stated on
its own basis (R-01 distinction):} the only defensible
\emph{marginal-generation-work} saving is the cache-hit share ---
\textbf{\textasciitilde35\% of requests skip generation entirely}, so
marginal generation work falls by roughly the cache-hit ratio
(illustrative, hypothetical). This does \textbf{not} reduce the all-in
self-hosted TCO of §16's fixed fleet, which is almost entirely
capex/opex and is unchanged by cache hits unless the fleet is also
re-sized. A typical re-sizing that lets the peak load concentrate on the
canonical fleet would have to be computed under §26.8's cost-basis rules
--- §26.8 states those rules but does not perform the re-sizing --- and
it is \emph{not} a 48\% figure; the old ``48\% (or 47\%)'' headline
mixed a marginal-work saving with a fixed-fleet all-in cost and is not
used. The Pattern-Measurement-Feedback Loop illustrates the hypothesis
that caching + load shedding is net positive on latency (to be validated
by the load test of §4), and the architect documents the configuration
as a reusable pattern instance.

\textbf{Alternatives considered.} Three obvious alternatives were
weighed against the canonical constraints, and each is rejected for a
stated, testable reason --- not merely because it ``changes the wrong
term.'' \emph{Prefix caching alone} reuses prefill compute behind an
exact-prefix match but still generates the answer, so the repeat
population keeps paying full decode --- it does not skip the generation
the semantic cache skips. \emph{A second serving host with load
balancing} is \textbf{not} rejected on the single phrase ``KV-bound'':
an independent replica adds KV slots and capacity, which is precisely
what relieves a queue caused by insufficient resident-request capacity.
Whether it helps is a load-curve/SLO-goodput question (a lightly loaded
host may see little gain, an offered-load-heavy host significant
queueing relief), so the capstone treats it as an \textbf{open sizing
question to be tested over an actual offered-load interval}, not as a
rejected alternative --- the explicit caveat is that one replica's fixed
\textless5\% per-request claim stays an untested scenario hypothesis,
not a basis for refusal. \emph{Deeper continuous batching on the single
host instead of sharding} raises throughput by lengthening the in-flight
queue, adding queueing delay exactly where the p99 TTFT SLO binds (Ch
8's decode economics; Ch 15's congestion check). The capstone therefore
does not credit the deeper-batching lever for a cost saving it is
simultaneously rejecting (see §26.8); sharding is treated as
\textbf{baseline configuration} (the weights are already
tensor-parallel), not as an incremental gain. Cache + circuit breaker
wins on the \emph{latency} story --- cache hits replace the retrieval
step with a cache lookup and skip the engine, and the breaker sheds tail
load --- while its \emph{cost} benefit is the marginal-generation-work
saving of the cache-hit share, stated on that basis.

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Mistake 1: Measuring only HYPOTHESIS outcomes.} Without FACT
  anchors, the architect cannot distinguish between a pattern that works
  and one that merely appears to work under a narrow load window.
\item
  \textbf{Mistake 2: Over-engineering before FACTs exist.} Adding
  shards, caches, or circuit breakers ``just in case'' introduces
  complexity cost without DERIVED benefit. Wait for the FACT to signal
  the need.
\item
  \textbf{Mistake 3: Ignoring the cost DERIVED.} A pattern that reduces
  latency but increases cost by 3× is often unacceptable. Measure cost
  per query as a first-class derived quantity --- computed from
  instrumented FACTs (throughput per shard, \$/host-hour), never left as
  opinion.
\item
  \textbf{Mistake 4: Circular HYPOTHESIS validation.} Self-confirming
  predictions (e.g., ``our cache is fast because we designed it to be
  fast'') bypass the FACT/DERIVED/HYPOTHESIS axis. Always validate
  against observed FACTs.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


The toolkit's patterns do not exist in isolation. Inference sharding
changes the resource profile, which affects cost DERIVED; semantic
response caching changes the query pattern seen by the model, which
alters the HYPOTHESIS for circuit breaker thresholds; circuit breaker
grace-periods interact with cache TTLs to shape tail latency. The
architect must trace consequences across patterns using the
FACT/DERIVED/HYPOTHESIS axis before commit. A change that looks
beneficial in isolation may degrade fleet-wide metrics when patterns
interact.

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  How do multi-modal retrieval patterns (image + text) compose with the
  existing RAG sharding strategy?
\item
  What is the optimal semantic-response cache size (and vector-index
  memory) for the canonical RAG workload when user query distributions
  shift seasonally?
\item
  Can circuit breaker thresholds be auto-tuned from FACT streams without
  manual intervention?
\item
  How does fleet-wide model versioning interact with per-shard caching,
  and what FACTs govern safe rollouts?
\end{itemize}

These open questions define the next research cycle. Each is framed as a
HYPOTHESIS waiting for FACT instrumentation.

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: The Capstone: Composing the
Patterns}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-the-capstone-composing-the-patterns}

\textbf{Scenario.} A growing AI startup moves from a single-node
prototype to a fleet serving \textasciitilde3,000 users. The initial
deployment is a 70B FP16 model on a single 8×H100 node (the canonical
host). Note the per‑host ceiling: with the canonical \textasciitilde8.6
s service time and C ≈ 17.5 KV‑resident requests, one host serves
\textasciitilde2.0 req/s (Ch 17/20) --- so the figures below are
expressed as \textbf{TTFT p99} on that host (the sub‑second latencies
are time‑to‑first‑token, not end‑to‑end), and the rps is the
\textasciitilde2 req/s the host can actually sustain, not a multiple of
it.

\textbf{Step 1 --- Instrument.} The capstone declares a \textbf{distinct
short-prompt workload} --- it is \emph{not} the canonical
9.2K-input/1.08-s-prefill case of Ch 4. It uses the same 70B/8×H100 host
and the same per-host ceiling (\textasciitilde2.0 req/s; C ≈ 17.5
KV-resident requests, Ch 17/20), but a much smaller prompt such that the
model's prefill contribution to client TTFT is well under the canonical
1.08 s. The canonical C ≈ 17.5 and \textasciitilde2.0 req/s ceiling are
held as \emph{conservative planning caps}: a shorter prompt only lowers
per-request KV (which would raise C) and shortens service time (which
would raise the ceiling), so the canonical values sit on the safe side
of this workload's real limits. This declared boundary is explicit so
the sub-second numbers are not read as a re-derivation of Ch 4's latency
budget. The \emph{baseline inputs} for this worked example are: request
rate \textasciitilde2 rps (within the \textasciitilde2.0 req/s per-host
analytical planning ceiling), SM utilization 45\%, per-query latency
breakdown for a \emph{baseline} (uncached) request: model compute 320
ms, I/O 80 ms, retrieval 50 ms --- the RAG retrieval step every request
pays. \textbf{These are assumed baseline inputs, not gathered FACTs
(R-07):} no author-run load test produced them, so they are labeled an
\emph{assumed baseline for a worked example} (an input to the math), not
a measured runtime quantity a FACT would be. They become FACTs only once
instrumented and recorded in a genuine load test. The baseline has
\textbf{no cache installed}, so there is no cache-\emph{miss} path in
it; the 50 ms is plain corpus retrieval, and Pattern B's hit path later
covers the same ground --- query encoding + cache lookup, of similar
cost --- without the engine. These components sum to a
\textbf{\textasciitilde450 ms baseline TTFT budget}, which the capstone
declares as the baseline \textbf{p99} scenario value (a scenario input,
not a derived quantile of a distributed sample; a real baseline p99 must
come from a measured latency distribution). The 450 ms is a
\emph{service-time sum with no queueing term}; at the offered
\textasciitilde2 rps against the \textasciitilde2.0 req/s analytical
ceiling (ρ near 1), a real queueing delay sits on top --- one more
reason this baseline is a scenario input to re-measure. Note the
component values are \emph{representative worst-case} estimates for a
baseline request, and the \textasciitilde450 ms is therefore the
baseline TTFT figure used throughout; the per-query values are not a p99
of a separate measured distribution. Because the 50 ms retrieval term is
part of the baseline, the headline \textbf{−44\%} is measured against
the full 450 ms path; excluding that term entirely (a 400 ms baseline)
would report (400 − 250) / 400 = \textbf{37.5\%} instead.

\textbf{Step 2 --- Pattern Apply.} The architect applies Pattern A
(inference sharding across an 8×H100 node) and Pattern B (semantic
response cache with 5-min TTL). \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}
--- sharding distributes the model across the node so decode lands in
the bandwidth-bound regime (the low-batch regime of Ch. 8; batch size,
context precision, kernel, and hardware decide whether it stays there);
cache hit ratio 35\% reduces the number of requests that reach the model
engine from \textasciitilde2.0 rps to \textasciitilde1.3 rps (2.0 × (1 −
0.35) = 1.3). The TTFT and cost figures below are \emph{scenario
estimates} for this illustrative configuration, not measured values.
\textbf{A weighted average of component latency values is a mean, not a
quantile, so the capstone separates the two.} Under the transparent
two-point model --- a cache hit returns at a fixed \textasciitilde50 ms
(query encoding + cache lookup, no corpus retrieval and no LLM prefill),
and a cache miss is served by the engine at \textasciitilde250 ms (a
scenario \emph{input}, not the 450 ms sum minus a computed term: the
baseline's 320 ms compute is a worst-case figure carrying prefill-queue
inflation at ρ near 1, and the miss value assumes that queue has drained
at the cached \textasciitilde1.3 rps --- both distributions are Step 3
load-test targets) --- the pooled latency has the CDF \(F(50)=0.35\),
\(F(250)=1.0\). Its \textbf{mean} is
\(0.35\times50 + 0.65\times250 = 180\) ms, but its \textbf{p99 is 250
ms} (because the 0.99 quantile falls in the miss population:
\(0.35 < 0.99 \le 1.0\)). So \textbf{mean TTFT ≈ 180 ms}. \textbf{p99
TTFT ≈ 250 ms} (the miss value, not a blend). \textbf{−44\% TTFT}
against the \textasciitilde450 ms uncached p99 baseline ---
\((450-250)/450 \approx 44\%\). \textbf{Cost is deliberately not
collapsed into a single percentage} (R-01/R-05): the only defensible
\emph{marginal-generation-work} saving is the cache-hit share ---
\textasciitilde35\% of requests skip generation entirely, so marginal
generation work falls by roughly the cache-hit ratio. This is
\textbf{not} an all-in TCO reduction: the §16 self-hosted fleet is a
fixed set of hosts whose capex/opex does not shrink with cache hits
unless the fleet is also re-sized; and sharding is \textbf{baseline
configuration} (the 140 GB weights are already tensor-parallel across
the 8 GPUs), so it is \emph{not} an incremental gain over the baseline.
The old ``−48\% generation cost'' figure additionally credited a
\textbf{deeper-continuous-batching} \textasciitilde20\% per-token
saving, but deeper batching is the exact lever \textbf{rejected} above
because it lengthens the queue and harms p99 TTFT --- a single lever
cannot be both rejected and credited, so that term is removed. A
defensible cost statement separates (A) marginal generation work
(cache-hit share), (B) variable metered API spend, and (C) all-in
self-hosted TCO, and never multiplies the fixed-fleet all-in \$/request
by a fraction of requests. The reproducible two-point model that yields
these numbers is \texttt{render/capstone\_calc.py}; a real deployment
must measure the actual cache-hit latency \emph{distribution} (not a
point mass), the prefill queue at 1.3 rps, and the per-query cost, and
the gains are \emph{not} independent --- they compound on the same
request path, so they must not be added as if separable.

\textbf{Step 3 --- Validate.} \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}
--- ``sharding + caching will keep TTFT p99 \textless{} 300 ms as the
fleet grows from the canonical \textasciitilde2,000 users to the
capstone's \textasciitilde3,000 users.'' A user \emph{population} is not
an offered-load law: growth from 2,000 to 3,000 users (1.5×) does not,
by itself, give the arrival rate, cache-hit ratio, peak-correlation, or
queueing distribution --- the capstone therefore states a concrete
scaling assumption (the same \textasciitilde2.0 rps sustained offered
rate, \textasciitilde5\% concurrently active at peak, and a 35\%
cache-hit ratio held constant for illustration) and validates capacity
and fallback quality against that assumption, rather than treating a
user count as an arrival model. Suppose a staged load test at
\textasciitilde3,000 users reports TTFT p99 = 285 ms and a
cache-hit-driven reduction in marginal generation work at the assumed
hit ratio --- in this scenario these are illustrative values to show how
a hypothesis is tested, not a real experiment the book ran. The
hypothesis is accepted only on a genuine load test's numbers.

\textbf{A note on utilization targets.} The ``SM util \textless{} 80\%''
row is an \emph{operational guardrail for this specific workload}, not a
universal performance criterion. A GPU has many utilization figures ---
SM utilization, tensor-core utilization, HBM bandwidth utilization,
NVLink utilization, memory-capacity occupancy --- and which one matters
is set by the workload's dominant constraint, not by the hardware (Ch.
8). Under an SLO-satisfying state, a GPU can sit at very different
utilizations depending on whether the workload is prefill-bound
(compute) or decode-bound (bandwidth/memory). The architect's question
is therefore not ``is utilization high enough?'' but \textbf{``which
resource is saturated when the SLO is satisfied, and does the pattern
keep the fleet the right side of that saturation?''} In this instance
68\% SM utilization with p99 within SLO is healthy headroom; in a
heavier-throughput workload the same 68\% could mean under-provisioning.
State the SLO-bound resource, not a bare percentage.
