\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

After this chapter we should be able to answer the question every
stakeholder actually asks --- \emph{what does this really cost?} ---
with a defensible number, not a guess. We will build a
total-cost-of-ownership model that separates capital, operating, and
opportunity costs, then price the canonical workload across three
delivery modes: self-hosted on our own cards, cloud GPU instances, and a
managed inference API. After this chapter, ``we save money by
self-hosting'' is either proven or refuted by arithmetic, and the
break-even point between modes is explicit.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) is the sum of everything required to
deliver a capability over its lifetime, not just the price tag of the
hardware or the per-token rate. It has three cost families:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Capital (capex)} --- the one-time cost of the cards, servers,
  networking, racks, and cooling that make up the system. For
  self-hosted inference this dominates up front: an 8×H100 server with
  networking and infrastructure carries a large price.
  \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}
\item
  \textbf{Operating (opex)} --- the recurring cost of running it:
  electricity and cooling, staff, software/licenses, cloud instance
  rental, and replacement/amortization of hardware. For cloud and
  managed API this is the whole cost; for self-hosted it compounds on
  capex.
\item
  \textbf{Opportunity} --- the cost of the \emph{alternative not taken}:
  the team time spent operating vs building, the delay to market, and
  the engineering hours that do not scale with either card or token
  counts. This is the most omitted and often the largest term.
\end{enumerate}

The architect's job is not to minimize capex, nor opex, nor provider
bill --- it is to minimize \textbf{the number that matters for the
organization's decision}, which usually means total cost per useful
request at the required quality and SLO.

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


TCO is \textbf{a utilization-weighted average over the whole system, not
a sticker price.} Three mental moves keep the arithmetic honest:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Amortize, don't stare at the price tag.} A \$400K server is an
  asset consumed over \textasciitilde5 years; spread it into
  dollars-per-month, then into dollars-per-request, before comparing to
  a per-token API rate. \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}
\item
  \textbf{Follow the token, not the GPU.} The thing being bought is
  completed, SLO-meeting requests. Two systems with the same card count
  can differ 10× in cost per request if one has 3× the goodput per card,
  better utilization, or no idle coasting.
\item
  \textbf{Include what does not scale.} Staff, power, networking
  engineering, and the latency of procurement are sunk or recurring
  whether utilization is 20\% or 80\%. A low-cost-per-card system that
  burns engineers can be the most expensive.
\end{itemize}

The decision shape is a \textbf{break-even graph}: at low volume,
managed/cloud-and-shut-down wins (no idle capex); at high volume,
self-hosted cards amortize below the per-token rate. The crossover point
is what we compute.

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


\subsection{Size the fleet first, then price
it}\label{size-the-fleet-first-then-price-it}

actually requires}. A common sizing mistake is to start from a single
host: a single 8×H100 host can only sustain \textasciitilde2.0 req/s
under the KV/latency analytical planning bound (\hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}/20), so it
cannot serve even the canonical 10 rps average, let alone the 40 rps
peak. We therefore size the fleet first (Ch 17) and price it second.

\textbf{Every TCO figure below is scaled to this fleet requirement;
pricing a single host would understate the cost by roughly 5--20×.}

\textbf{What kind of number is \textasciitilde2.0 req/s?} The per-host
rate \texttt{C\_KV\ /\ W\ ≈\ 17.5\ /\ 8.6\ ≈\ 2.0\ req/s} is an
\emph{upper-order planning estimate under a particular concurrency,
timing, and cache geometry} --- not an observed service rate. It is an
average-in-system relation, not a measured goodput. The three quantities
behind \texttt{N\_hosts\ =\ max(N\_throughput,\ N\_KV,\ N\_SLO)} are
\textbf{three different models that must be derived and measured
separately}, and the formula is only as strong as the weakest one:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{N\_KV (KV-memory occupancy ceiling)} --- how many requests
  \emph{reside} in KV cache given the host's usable HBM and the
  per-token KV footprint. This is an arithmetic allocation bound (the
  \textasciitilde17.5 figure).
\item
  \textbf{N\_throughput (phase capacity)} --- how many requests the host
  can \emph{complete} per second given the service time. The
  \textasciitilde8.6 s service time is an uncontended request-level
  estimate, and \texttt{C/W} treats it as fixed.
\item
  \textbf{N\_SLO (queueing / goodput)} --- the number of hosts needed to
  hold p95/p99 TTFT, TPOT, and success rate under \emph{concurrent}
  arrival. \textbf{Little's law relates average in-system work to
  elapsed time under stability; it does not independently prove a
  throughput ceiling or a p99 latency.} At concurrency
  \textasciitilde17, TTFT and TPOT can differ substantially from the
  isolated \textasciitilde8.6 s used to obtain \texttt{C/W}, because
  batch efficiency, per-request duration, and interference are all
  coupled to load.
\end{itemize}

So the 20-host figure is a \textbf{scenario-specific bound contingent on
the 8.6-second service time remaining valid under concurrent execution},
not a deployment-validated estimate. Before budgeting against it,
measure \texttt{λ\_goodput} per host at the target p95/p99 TTFT, TPOT,
and success rate on the actual workload, and re-derive N\_hosts. (See
the falsification protocol in Ch 17.)

\textbf{Treat both provisioning policies explicitly.} The headline \$
figures below use the full-utilization fleet (≈20 hosts, provisioned
exactly at the 40 rps peak with no headroom). The capacity-planning rule
(Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}) uses a 70\% max-operating-utilization target, which
gives \textbf{29 \emph{working} hosts} at the peak ⌈40×8.6/(17.5×0.7)⌉ =
⌈344/12.25⌉ = 29; because one host must be able to drain
(failover/maintenance) while the fleet still clears the peak, the N−1
policy requires \textbf{30 hosts total} (29 working + 1 spare --- 29
surviving × 2.0 × 0.70 ≈ 40.6 ≥ 40 rps). That spare is a \emph{reserve}
host, not serving capacity. Both outcomes belong in the decision:

\textbf{Mode 2 --- cloud GPU instances (rent, scale to load).} An 8×H100
on-demand instance \textasciitilde\$20/hr (canonical \$2.50/GPU-hr × 8).
Because the workload's \emph{peak} is 40 rps, the fleet must be able to
burst to \textasciitilde20 instances, but the \emph{average} load is
\textasciitilde5 hosts' worth --- so the honest cloud bill is the
instance-hours actually required. That requires a \textbf{load-duration
/ capacity-occupancy model}, not a single average. Parameterize the
monthly cloud host-hours as \(H_\text{cloud,month}\) (host-hours, not Ch
17's host-count \(N\)) --- the integral of required capacity over time,
plus the warm-pool and capacity-reservation terms:

\[
H_\text{cloud,month} = \int_0^T N_\text{required}(t)\,dt \;\; \text{plus a warm/startup pool and any capacity-reservation guarantee}
\]

where \(N_\text{required}(t)\) is the number of hosts that must be
running at time \(t\) (a step function that rises with the load curve at
that hour of the day/week). This depends on the actual temporal load
profile --- the \textbf{burst duration and frequency}, the
\textbf{scale-up latency} (how many seconds/minutes to attach and load a
fresh instance), the \textbf{model-loading/warmup time} (loading a 140
GB model into a new host is not instant), the \textbf{minimum warm pool}
to serve the base load without cold-starting, \textbf{provider
capacity/availability}, and \textbf{billing granularity}. Only if the
provider can attach instances faster than the load curve demands, with a
warm pool already holding the model, can the cloud bill approach the
pure average-load figure.

Under a simplified assumption of a continuously-arriving
\textasciitilde5-host average load that bursts briefly to
\textasciitilde20 at the peak, with a steady warm pool and fast
scale-up, that is ≈ 5 × \$20/hr × 730 hr ≈ \textasciitilde\$73K/month
(scaling the fleet with load; always-on-at-peak would be
\textasciitilde\$292K/month). \textbf{\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}}
--- this is \emph{explicitly} an assumed load-duration curve and fast
elastically-scalable capacity, not a measured or guaranteed cost. If the
burst is long-lived or scale-up is slow, the real cloud cost rises
toward the always-on-at-peak figure and the ``cloud is cheapest'' result
weakens accordingly.

\textbf{The cloud case is a family, not a single line.} \$73K and \$292K
are the two endpoints (ideal fast-scale vs always-on-at-peak). The
realistic midpoint is set by two \emph{parameters} --- the
\textbf{hours-at-peak} per day/week and the
\textbf{reserved-warm-instance} count held to avoid cold-start. For
example, a mid-case with \textasciitilde5 reserved warm hosts +
\textasciitilde15 burst hosts held for \textasciitilde4 peak hours/day,
at \$20/hr (burst) and the warm pool at a reserved rate, lands between
the two endpoints; the exact number depends on the provider's
reserved/discount and billing-granularity terms, which are scenario
inputs, not market facts. The durable lesson --- \emph{fleet size first,
price second} --- does not depend on this elasticity assumption, but the
specific \$2.8/1K number does and should be read as the \emph{fast-scale
ideal}, not the default decision line.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item

&\approx \$0.0208 \approx \$20.8 \text{ per 1,000 requests}

\textbf{Reading the result.} Once the fleet is sized correctly
(\textasciitilde20 hosts), the comparison flips entirely versus the
single-host framing. Self-hosted (\textasciitilde\$23.7/1K reqs) is now
\textbf{roughly twice the cloud-scaled rate} (\$11.7/1K) on a
fully-loaded basis (\$148K/mo vs \textasciitilde\$73K/mo), because the
canonical workload genuinely needs a multi-host fleet, and self-hosting
a 20-host fleet carries \textasciitilde\$116K/mo of capex amortization.
It is also \textbf{above the managed-API rate} (\$20.8/1K), so at this
active-hours volume self-hosting loses to both the API and scale-to-load
cloud. \textbf{This is an illustrative lower-bound for cloud, NOT an
apples-to-apples comparison} --- the \$73K cloud figure assumes ideal
scale-to-load elasticity and a continuously-arriving
\textasciitilde5-host average that bursts briefly to \textasciitilde20,
whereas the \$148K self-host figure is an always-provisioned 20-host
fleet. They do \textbf{not} share the same availability ceiling,
failover reserve, warm-capacity cost, peak-duration assumption, or p95
SLO. \textbf{Cloud-scaled being cheapest is therefore a lower-bound
result of an asymmetric scenario, not a demonstrated mode ranking.}
Without a measured traffic trace and equalized SLO/failover/availability
assumptions on both sides, the definitive economic winner is \textbf{not
determined}.

\textbf{Apples-to-apples caveat.} The \$73K cloud figure does not yet
include an N−1 failover reserve, capacity held for failures/maintenance,
warm-capacity cost during idle periods, model-startup time, or provider
availability variance; the self-host figure does not charge for the same
availability target's redundancy. So the two are \textbf{not compared on
equal reliability or load-serving assumptions}. Before treating cloud as
cheaper, add: the same served-load trace, p95 SLO, peak duration,
availability target, failover coverage, warm capacity, model startup
time, and price period to \emph{both} sides. Under a nonideal elastic
case (a reserved warm pool sized for N−1 plus slower scale-up), the
cloud cost rises toward the always-on-at-peak figure and the ``cloud is
cheapest'' result may not hold. As above: the \$2.8/1K figure is an
\textbf{optimistic lower bound} --- frame the decision as ``cloud is
cheaper \textbf{if} scale-up is fast and a warm pool can be held
cheaply,'' not as an unconditional ranking. The decisive caveat remains
the \textbf{staff/ops term and whether it is sunk}, and now also the
\textbf{fleet requirement} --- a single-host TCO understates the real
cost by 5--20×.

\textbf{Multi-region active-active availability is NOT priced in this
TCO.} The canonical workload's stated SLA (Ch 4) is multi-region
active-active at 99.9\% availability, but \textbf{every} \texttt{\$/1K}
figure in this chapter (and the host counts in Ch 17) prices a
\textbf{single-region} deployment with \textbf{N−1 single-host
failover}, \textbf{not} multi-region active-active. Multi-region
active-active is a \textbf{different provisioning problem} from N−1
single-host failover: it requires cross-region capacity duplication (no
single region's loss may take the service down), region-failure
tolerance, data locality and traffic routing (geo/health-based routing,
per-region caches and indices), and consistent state/data replication
(model weights + KV/index replication across regions). None of that is
priced or validated here, and none of it is implied by the N−1 reserve
host above. Accordingly the canonical cost figures \textbf{understate
the cost of the canonical workload's own stated 99.9\% availability
requirement} --- read the \texttt{\$/1K} and monthly totals as
\emph{single-region, N−1-only} numbers, and add a separate multi-region
capacity + replication cost line (on the order of ≥2× the single-region
fleet on the capacity-duplication term alone) before the availability
SLA can be considered met.

\textbf{Parametric sensitivity is the durable form.} All the fixed
inputs above --- GPU price, electricity, staffing, capacity, API price,
and the fleet size itself --- are \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} scenario values;
the \emph{structure} of the model is what generalizes. The architect
computes N\_hosts from the workload first (Ch 17/20), then re-runs this
TCO with the customer's real numbers (\hyperref[chap:23]{Chapter 23}). A runnable,
parameterised version ships with the book as
\texttt{render/tco\_calc.py}.

Crucially, these are \textbf{independent sensitivity dimensions}, and
each pushes the break-even in a different direction; do not collapse
them into one ``the assumptions vary'' disclaimer:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Fleet size} (N\_hosts) --- driven by the KV/throughput/SLO
  ceiling (Ch 17-20), not by TCO. A larger fleet raises the flat
  self-host line (more capex+opex) and shifts break-even right.
\item
  \textbf{Duty cycle} --- a burst-sized fleet does \emph{not} run every
  host at full paid duty all month. Self-hosted reserved cards bill flat
  regardless of utilization; cloud on-demand bills only what runs.
  Separating these two is what makes the self-host (high fixed, low
  variable) vs cloud (low fixed, high variable) comparison meaningful.
\item
  \textbf{Utilization} --- the \emph{provisioning} utilization target
  (70\% vs 100\%, Ch 17) sets how many hosts are needed, not how much
  they cost per unit; it is a separate line from the price-per-host.
\item
  \textbf{Reserved / on-demand mix} --- whether capacity is committed
  (cheaper, flat) or burst (pricier, on-demand) is a distinct lever; a
  mixed fleet has both a flat and a variable term.
\item
  \textbf{Failover reserve} --- N-1/headroom hosts (Ch 17) add cost but
  are not production-serving capacity; they must be counted as a
  separate reserve line, not folded into the served-load hosts.
\item
  \textbf{Staffing} --- the dominant human term for self-hosting; it is
  frequently the decider at low volume (Ch16 §16.8 mini-case) and should
  be its own sensitivity dimension, not absorbed into opex.
\end{itemize}

Because these dimensions are independent, the break-even is a
\textbf{region}, not a point: the figure marks the \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}
price band, and the honest output is a range (here
\textasciitilde5.9--9.9M req/mo) rather than a single ``the break-even
is 7.1M'' claim. The headline range varies the \textbf{API price}
against the full-utilization self-host cost (\$148K/mo): the low end
(\textasciitilde5.9M) is 148,000 ÷ 0.025 (the \$25/1K top of the band)
and the high end (\textasciitilde9.9M) is 148,000 ÷ 0.015 (the \$15/1K
bottom of the band) --- the same \textasciitilde5.9--9.9M range reported
with \hyperref[fig:16.1]{Fig 16.1} and in §16.6. The \textbf{70\%-utilization provisioning
case} is a separate dimension and is named separately: the \$217K/mo
self-host cost against the canonical \$20.8/1K API rate gives 217,000 ÷
0.0208 ≈ 10.4M req/mo. Report the range and name which dimension is
being varied.

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


Four metrics keep the TCO model honest:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Cost per useful (SLO-meeting) request} --- the decision
  number; divide total cost by goodput-meeting requests (Ch6), not raw
  tokens.
\item
  \textbf{Effective utilization} --- actual goodput ÷ theoretical
  capacity across the billing period; low utilization is the hidden tax
  in both capex and rented instances.
\item
  \textbf{Break-even volume} --- the requests/month where self-host
  cost-per-request crosses managed API; below it, don't buy cards.
\item
  \textbf{Staff/duty-cycle adjusters} --- the engineering-hours and
  duty-cycle assumptions that dominate the comparisons; measure them,
  don't assume.
\end{enumerate}

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Comparing sticker price to per-token rate.} A \$/1K-token API
  quote already includes amortization; a card price tag does not.
  Amortize both.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring the staff/ops term.} Self-host ``saves money'' on
  paper and loses the moment two engineers are consumed full-time.
\item
  \textbf{Assuming 100\% utilization.} Idle cards are still burning
  electricity and depreciation; bake duty cycle in.
\item
  \textbf{Forgetting opportunity cost.} A 6-month procurement cycle
  versus a same-week API integration is a real cost measured in
  time-to-value.
\item
  \textbf{Using peak capacity for cost-per-request.} Peak sizing (40
  rps) overstates cost if average (10 rps) utilization is what governs.
  (The headline \$5.7/1K above does divide the peak-sized 20-host fleet
  by average volume --- deliberately: this workload must clear the 40
  rps peak, so peak capacity is real cost here; the mistake applies when
  peak sizing is avoidable, e.g.~batch or queueable traffic.)
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


TCO is the final gate in the design loop (Ch12 candidates → Ch14
benchmark → Ch16 cost). It settles which candidate survives: the 70B
8×H100 self-host \textbf{passes the capability screen and is
analytically screened for feasibility --- it \emph{would} need a
representative hardware benchmark to establish SLO-goodput} (the
\texttt{C/W} and roofline estimates of Chs. 7--8 are planning bounds,
not a measured p95 TTFT/TPOT pass at the target offered load), and on
TCO it \textbf{beats the managed API at canonical volume but loses to
scale-to-load cloud} --- so the surviving recommendation is
\emph{conditional} (self-host wins only if the staff/ops term is sunk
and sustained near-peak utilization holds and a benchmark confirms the
SLO; otherwise scale-to-load cloud at \textasciitilde\$2.8/1K is
cheapest). This completes the chain from vague requirement to a
defensible, costed architecture, while being honest that the TCO gate is
not an unconditional win and that the SLO pass is \emph{provisional
until benchmarked}. It also feeds fleet decisions (Ch17-18): the
break-even curve is what justifies buying a second host versus renting
peaks, and the staff term is what pushes toward consolidation (Ch18).

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: TCO Discipline and the Staff
Term}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-tco-discipline-and-the-staff-term}

A startup's CTO shows the architect a warrant to buy two 8×H100 servers
for the new internal RAG assistant, citing ``we'll save on token fees.''
Applying TCO discipline, the architect does not dispute the raw token
math. Instead they price it: at this startup's \emph{actual} early
volume --- \textasciitilde2 rps average, \textasciitilde5M
requests/month, and only 0.5 engineers fully available for GPU ops ---
the break-even against a managed API is this chapter's own division:
\$40,000 ÷ \$0.0208 ≈ \textbf{1.9M requests/month}, so the current
volume is already \textasciitilde2.6× past the break-even. The honest
number: self-hosting two servers \textasciitilde\$40K/month all-in vs
\textasciitilde\$104K/month API at current volume (5M × \$0.0208) --- a
\textbf{\textasciitilde\$64K/month saving in favor of self-hosting, with
the staff term already inside the \$40K} (capex \textasciitilde\$11.6K +
power \textasciitilde\$2.2K for two hosts leaves ≤ \textasciitilde\$26K
for staff --- upkeep is the power line already counted, so
\textasciitilde\$0.2K of the \$26.2K remainder is unallocated --- too
small to dwarf a \$64K saving). The staff term is on a different basis
than Mode 1: the mini-case prices a startup's 0.5-FTE operator
(\textasciitilde\$26K/mo --- ≈\$624K/yr fully loaded for one engineer, a
scenario value rather than a market rate), not Mode 1's mature-operator
alloc (\textasciitilde\$10K/mo for 20 hosts) --- re-price on one
staffing basis before comparing. The token arithmetic therefore does not
send this startup to the API; it points to self-hosting. Only one term
can flip the decision: the \emph{opportunity} cost of the GPU-ops time
the two servers consume --- the better part of the only engineer the
startup can spare. To justify paying the API premium, that term must
exceed \textbf{\textasciitilde\$64K/month}, about \$770K/year of product
work the startup is not doing. The architect's verdict: \textbf{start on
the managed API now, and revisit self-host the moment the consumed
engineer's opportunity cost falls below \textasciitilde\$64K/month ---
for example when a second engineer frees up} --- then re-run the same
model. Request volume is not the lever: more volume only widens the
\textasciitilde\$64K gap. The CTO did not buy the servers. The
break-even graph did not make the answer self-evident; it narrowed the
whole decision to one labeled assumption --- this startup values its
half-engineer's product time above \$64K/month. A team that cannot say
that, or that has a second engineer to spare, runs the same numbers and
buys the servers.
