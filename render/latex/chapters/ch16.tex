\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

After this chapter we should be able to answer the question every
stakeholder actually asks --- \emph{what does this really cost?} ---
with a defensible number, not a guess. We will build a
total-cost-of-ownership model that separates capital, operating, and
opportunity costs, then price the canonical workload across three
delivery modes: self-hosted on our own cards, cloud GPU instances, and a
managed inference API. After this chapter, ``we save money by
self-hosting'' is either proven or refuted by arithmetic, and the
break-even point between modes is explicit.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) is the sum of everything required to
deliver a capability over its lifetime, not just the price tag of the
hardware or the per-token rate. It has three cost families:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Capital (capex)} --- the one-time cost of the cards, servers,
  networking, racks, and cooling that make up the system. For
  self-hosted inference this dominates up front: an 8×H100 server with
  networking and infrastructure carries a large price.
  \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}
\item
  \textbf{Operating (opex)} --- the recurring cost of running it:
  electricity and cooling, staff, software/licenses, cloud instance
  rental, and replacement/amortization of hardware. For cloud and
  managed API this is the whole cost; for self-hosted it compounds on
  capex.
\item
  \textbf{Opportunity} --- the cost of the \emph{alternative not taken}:
  the team time spent operating vs building, the delay to market, and
  the engineering hours that do not scale with either card or token
  counts. This is the most omitted and often the largest term.
\end{enumerate}

The architect's job is not to minimize capex, nor opex, nor provider
bill --- it is to minimize \textbf{the number that matters for the
organization's decision}, which usually means total cost per useful
request at the required quality and SLO.

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


TCO is \textbf{a utilization-weighted average over the whole system, not
a sticker price.} Three mental moves keep the arithmetic honest:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Amortize, don't stare at the price tag.} A \$400K server is an
  asset consumed over \textasciitilde5 years; spread it into
  dollars-per-month, then into dollars-per-request, before comparing to
  a per-token API rate. \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}
\item
  \textbf{Follow the token, not the GPU.} The thing being bought is
  completed, SLO-meeting requests. Two systems with the same card count
  can differ 10× in cost per request if one has 3× the goodput per card,
  better utilization, or no idle coasting.
\item
  \textbf{Include what does not scale.} Staff, power, networking
  engineering, and the latency of procurement are sunk or recurring
  whether utilization is 20\% or 80\%. A low-cost-per-card system that
  burns engineers can be the most expensive.
\end{itemize}

The decision shape is a \textbf{break-even graph}: at low volume,
managed/cloud-and-shut-down wins (no idle capex); at high volume,
self-hosted cards amortize below the per-token rate. The crossover point
is what we compute.

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


\subsection{Size the fleet first, then price
it}\label{size-the-fleet-first-then-price-it}

actually requires}. A common sizing mistake is to start from a single
host: a single 8×H100 host can only sustain \textasciitilde2.0 req/s
under the KV/latency analytical planning bound (\hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}/20), so it
cannot serve even the canonical 10 rps average, let alone the 40 rps
peak. We therefore size the fleet first (Ch 17) and price it second.

So the canonical workload's peak KV screen is \textbf{21 hosts}
(⌈344/17⌉, zero spare --- the headline policy provisions exactly at the
40 rps peak with no headroom), and the average needs \textbf{6} --- a
fleet, not one box. Ch 17's whole-slot convention floors C to 17 first
and gives 21; equivalently the floored whole-slot rule gives 21 --- this
TCO prices that 21-host scenario, and a one-host difference changes no
mode comparison. FP8 changes only the KV arithmetic: the raw FP8 byte
factor 0.5 gives a hard floor of 35 slots BEFORE efficiency and the host
ceil, so the FP8 KV screen is ⌈344/35.0⌉ = 10 at peak (11 at the 95\%
scheduling factor) --- KV-residency arithmetic on a screen, \textbf{not
an assertion that real deployments halve in size}; measured allocation,
compute and goodput are absent, and the independent prefill and
unmeasured SLO scopes do not shrink with it. \textbf{Every TCO figure
below is scaled to this 21-host KV-screen fleet; pricing a single host
would understate the cost by roughly 6--21×.}

\textbf{What kind of number is \textasciitilde2.0 req/s?} The per-host
rate \texttt{C\_KV\ /\ W\ ≈\ 17\ /\ 8.6\ ≈\ 2.0\ req/s} is an
\emph{upper-order planning estimate under a particular concurrency,
timing, and cache geometry} --- not an observed service rate. It is an
average-in-system relation, not a measured goodput, and it is derived
\emph{from} the KV bound, so it does not add an independent constraint.
The scopes behind fleet sizing must be derived and measured separately,
and the formula is only as strong as the weakest one:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{N\_KV (KV-memory occupancy ceiling)} --- how many requests
  \emph{reside} in KV cache given the host's usable HBM and the
  per-token KV footprint. This is an arithmetic allocation bound (the
  floor-17 figure).
\item
  \textbf{C/W (service-time surrogate inside the KV screen)} --- the
  \textasciitilde8.6 s W\_plan is an uncontended request-level estimate;
  C/W inherits both the KV bound and the service-time assumption, so it
  is not an independent compute bound and not an independently measured
  throughput.
\item
  \textbf{Independent prefill screen} --- a separate compute bound (5
  avg / 19 peak from ⌈368,000 ÷ 19,770⌉), legitimately independent of KV
  size.
\item
  \textbf{N\_SLO (queueing / goodput)} --- the number of hosts needed to
  hold p95/p99 TTFT, TPOT, and success rate under \emph{concurrent}
  arrival. \textbf{This scope is unmeasured here. Little's law relates
  average in-system work to elapsed time under stability; it does not
  independently prove a throughput ceiling or a p99 latency.} At
  concurrency \textasciitilde17, TTFT and TPOT can differ substantially
  from the isolated \textasciitilde8.6 s used to obtain \texttt{C/W},
  because batch efficiency, per-request duration, and interference are
  all coupled to load.
\end{itemize}

So the 21-host figure is a \textbf{scenario-specific KV/service-time
screen contingent on the 8.6-second service time remaining valid under
concurrent execution}, not a deployment-validated estimate. Before
budgeting against it, measure \texttt{λ\_goodput} per host at the target
p95/p99 TTFT, TPOT, and success rate on the actual workload, and
re-derive N\_hosts. (See the falsification protocol in Ch 17.)

\textbf{Mode 2 --- cloud GPU instances (rent, occupancy-input priced).}
An 8×H100 on-demand instance \textasciitilde\$20/hr (canonical
\$2.50/GPU-hr × 8). Because the workload's \emph{peak} is 40 rps, the
fleet must be able to burst to 21 instances, but what the bill actually
depends on is \textbf{paid host-hours} --- an INPUT this chapter does
not derive:

\[
H_\text{cloud,month} = \text{paid host-hours (an occupancy INPUT, supplied or assumed)}
\]

The honest cloud bill would be the integral of required capacity over
time, plus the warm-pool and capacity-reservation terms:

\[
H_\text{cloud,month} = \int_0^T N_\text{required}(t)\,dt \; \; \text{plus a warm/startup pool and any capacity-reservation guarantee}
\]

where \(N_\text{required}(t)\) is the number of hosts that must be
running at time \(t\) (a step function that rises with the load curve at
that hour of the day/week). This depends on the actual temporal load
profile --- the \textbf{burst duration and frequency}, the
\textbf{scale-up latency} (how many seconds/minutes to attach and load a
fresh instance), the \textbf{model-loading/warmup time} (loading a 140
GB model into a new host is not instant), the \textbf{minimum warm pool}
to serve the base load without cold-starting, \textbf{provider
capacity/availability}, and \textbf{billing granularity}. Only if the
provider can attach instances faster than the load curve demands, with a
warm pool already holding the model, can the cloud bill approach a pure
average-load figure.

\textbf{Default paid-occupancy ASSUMPTION.} The canonical default bills
\textbf{6 × 730 = 4,380 host-hours → \$87,600/month → \$14.04 per 1,000
requests} (14.038461538461538 exact). This is an explicit illustrative
occupancy INPUT: it is \textbf{not a measured duty cycle, not a
load-duration integral, not a guaranteed lower bound, not a complete
warm-plus-burst forecast, and not a proven feasible serving schedule}
--- and it must never be inferred from the 6/21 host-count ratio (a
ratio of selected host counts, not an occupancy). The always-on-at-peak
alternative bills 21 × 730 = 15,330 host-hours → \textbf{\$306,600/month
→ \$49.13 per 1,000} (49.13461538461539 exact).

\$306.6K are the two named endpoints (default occupancy input vs

\textbf{Reading the result.} Once the fleet is sized correctly (21
hosts), the comparison changes entirely versus the single-host framing.
On the included-cost lines above: self-hosted is \textbf{\$24.97/1K}
(\$155,816.93/mo), the cloud default-occupancy input is
\textbf{\$14.04/1K} (\$87,600/mo), and the managed API is
\textbf{\$20.8/1K} (\$129,792/mo). The self-host-to-default-cloud ratio
is \textbf{\textasciitilde1.8} (155,816.93 ÷ 87,600 = 1.78) --- a
\textasciitilde1.8 ratio, not a 2× gap --- and self-host sits
\textbf{above} the API at the canonical 6.24M volume, with the API
break-even at \textasciitilde7.5M. \textbf{None of these three numbers
is an unconditional ranking.} The \$87.6K cloud line is a paid-occupancy
INPUT assumption (not a measured duty cycle or a proven feasible serving
schedule), the \$155.8K self-host line is an always-provisioned 21-host
fleet carrying a fixed staff alloc, and the scopes are not equalized:
the three modes do not share the same availability ceiling, failover
reserve, warm-capacity cost, peak-duration assumption, occupancy
evidence, or p95 SLO. \textbf{Any ``cheaper'' reading is conditional on
those named inputs, not demonstrated.} Without a measured traffic trace
and equalized SLO/failover/availability/occupancy assumptions on both
sides, the definitive economic winner is \textbf{not determined}.

\textbf{Apples-to-apples caveat.} The \$87.6K cloud figure is an
occupancy input; it does not include an N−1 failover reserve, capacity
held for failures/maintenance, warm-capacity cost during idle periods,
model-startup time, or provider availability variance, and the self-host
figure does not charge for the same availability target's redundancy. So
the two are \textbf{not compared on equal reliability or load-serving
assumptions}. Before treating any mode as cheaper, add: the same
served-load trace, p95 SLO, peak duration, availability target, failover
coverage, warm capacity, model startup time, occupancy evidence, and
price period to \emph{both} sides. Under a nonideal elastic case (a
reserved warm pool sized for N−1 plus slower scale-up), the cloud cost
rises toward the always-on-at-peak figure and any ``cloud is cheapest''
result may not hold. As above: the \$14.04/1K figure is an
\textbf{assumption-bounded input} --- frame the decision as ``cloud is
cheaper \textbf{if} the occupancy input holds and a warm pool can be
held cheaply,'' not as an unconditional ranking. The decisive caveat
remains the \textbf{staff/ops term and whether it is sunk}, and now also
the \textbf{fleet requirement} --- a single-host TCO understates the
real cost by roughly 6--21×.

\textbf{Included-cost scope and exclusions (from the canonical YAML,
riding every CLI output).} No separate rack / external network / site
price line exists in source; none is invented here. Fully-built-host
capex covers only its stated scope; unpriced external items are
exclusions/unknowns. Staffing, startup, reservation and availability
costs are NOT equalized across delivery modes. This model totals
included illustrative lines only; it is not procurement-complete.

\textbf{Multi-region active-active availability is NOT priced in this
TCO.} The canonical workload's stated SLA (Ch 4) is multi-region
active-active at 99.9\% availability, but \textbf{every} \texttt{\$/1K}
figure in this chapter (and the host counts in Ch 17) prices a
\textbf{single-region} deployment with \textbf{N−1 single-host
failover}, \textbf{not} multi-region active-active. Multi-region
active-active is a \textbf{different provisioning problem} from N−1
single-host failover: it requires cross-region capacity duplication (no
single region's loss may take the service down), region-failure
tolerance, data locality and traffic routing (geo/health-based routing,
per-region caches and indices), and consistent state/data replication
(model weights + KV/index replication across regions). None of that is
priced or validated here, and none of it is implied by the N−1 reserve
host above. Accordingly the canonical cost figures \textbf{understate
the cost of the canonical workload's own stated 99.9\% availability
requirement} --- read the \texttt{\$/1K} and monthly totals as
\emph{single-region, N−1-only} numbers, and add a separate multi-region
capacity + replication cost line (on the order of ≥2× the single-region
fleet on the capacity-duplication term alone) before the availability
SLA can be considered met.

\textbf{Parametric sensitivity is the durable form.} All the fixed
inputs above --- GPU price, electricity, staffing, capacity, API price,
occupancy, and the fleet size itself --- are \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} scenario
values; the \emph{structure} of the model is what generalizes. The
architect computes N\_hosts from the workload first (Ch 17/20), then
re-runs this TCO with the customer's real numbers (\hyperref[chap:23]{Chapter 23}). A
runnable, parameterised version ships with the book as
\texttt{render/tco\_calc.py} (its pure model is
\texttt{render/tco\_model.py}, reading
\texttt{design/canonical-workload.yaml}).

Crucially, these are \textbf{independent sensitivity dimensions}, and
each pushes the break-even in a different direction; do not collapse
them into one ``the assumptions vary'' disclaimer:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Fleet size} (N\_hosts) --- driven by the KV/prefill/SLO scopes
  (Ch 17-20), not by TCO. A larger fleet raises the flat self-host line
  (more capex+opex) and shifts break-even right.
\item
  \textbf{Paid occupancy} --- the cloud bill is an occupancy INPUT
  (host-hours), while self-hosted reserved cards bill flat regardless of
  utilization. Separating these two is what makes the self-host (high
  fixed, low variable) vs cloud (low fixed, high variable) comparison
  meaningful --- and it is exactly why the two scopes are not comparable
  until the occupancy is evidenced.
\item
  \textbf{Utilization} --- the \emph{provisioning} utilization target
  (70\% vs 100\%, Ch 17) sets how many hosts are needed, not how much
  they cost per unit; it is a separate line from the price-per-host.
\item
  \textbf{Reserved / on-demand mix} --- whether capacity is committed
  (cheaper, flat) or burst (pricier, on-demand) is a distinct lever; a
  mixed fleet has both a flat and a variable term.
\item
  \textbf{Failover reserve} --- N-1/headroom hosts (Ch 17) add cost but
  are not production-serving capacity; they must be counted as a
  separate reserve line, not folded into the served-load hosts.
\item
  \textbf{Staffing} --- the dominant human term for self-hosting; it is
  frequently the decider at low volume (Ch 16 §16.8 mini-case) and
  should be its own sensitivity dimension, not absorbed into opex.
\end{itemize}

Because these dimensions are independent, the break-even is a
\textbf{region}, not a point: the figure marks the \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}
price band, and the honest output is a range (here
\textasciitilde6.2--10.4M req/mo) rather than a single ``the break-even
is 7.5M'' claim. The headline range varies the \textbf{API price}
against the 21-host self-host cost (\$155,816.93/mo): the low end is
6,232,677.2 = 155,816.93 ÷ 0.025 (the \$25/1K top of the band) and the
high end is 10,387,795.33 = 155,816.93 ÷ 0.015 (the \$15/1K bottom of
the band) --- the same \textasciitilde6.2--10.4M range reported with \hyperref[fig:16.1]{Fig 16.1} and in §16.6; the mid-point at the canonical \$20.8/1K is
7,491,198.56 ≈ \textbf{7.5M}. The \textbf{70\%-utilization provisioning
case} is a separate dimension and is named separately: the
\$218,309.90/mo 30-host cost against the canonical \$20.8/1K API rate
gives 218,309.90 ÷ 0.0208 ≈ \textbf{10.5M} req/mo. Report the range and
name which dimension is being varied. Note also that these are
\textbf{parameterized fixed-resource cost curves, not capacity
forecasts}: the self-host line is flat because the 21 hosts are paid for
regardless of volume, so nothing here predicts what volume the fleet can
\emph{serve} as requests grow.

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


Four metrics keep the TCO model honest:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Cost per useful (SLO-meeting) request} --- the decision
  number; divide total cost by goodput-meeting requests (Ch 6), not raw
  tokens.
\item
  \textbf{Effective utilization} --- actual goodput ÷ theoretical
  capacity across the billing period; low utilization is the hidden tax
  in both capex and rented instances.
\item
  \textbf{Break-even volume} --- the requests/month where self-host
  cost-per-request crosses managed API; below it, don't buy cards.
\item
  \textbf{Staff/occupancy adjusters} --- the engineering-hours and
  paid-occupancy assumptions that dominate the comparisons; measure
  them, don't assume.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Support lifecycle belongs in service
cost}\label{support-lifecycle-belongs-in-service-cost}

A lower accelerator price does not establish a lower service cost.
Migration work, pinned-runtime maintenance, security updates, failed
attempts, spare capacity, and fallback execution belong in the
numerator. The denominator remains quality-qualified, SLO-compliant
work. If a candidate needs more replicas because of worse tail latency
or lower usable KV capacity, that requirement can reverse an apparent
device-price advantage. This extends the cost model; it does not supply
a backend ranking. \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Comparing sticker price to per-token rate.} A \$/1K-token API
  quote already includes amortization; a card price tag does not.
  Amortize both.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring the staff/ops term.} Self-host ``saves money'' on
  paper and loses the moment two engineers are consumed full-time.
\item
  \textbf{Assuming 100\% utilization.} Idle cards are still burning
  electricity and depreciation; bake duty cycle in.
\item
  \textbf{Forgetting opportunity cost.} A 6-month procurement cycle
  versus a same-week API integration is a real cost measured in
  time-to-value.
\item
  \textbf{Using peak capacity for cost-per-request.} Peak sizing (40
  rps) overstates cost if average (10 rps) utilization is what governs.
  (The headline \$24.97/1K above does divide the peak-sized 21-host
  fleet by average volume --- deliberately: this workload must clear the
  40 rps peak, so peak capacity is real cost here; the mistake applies
  when peak sizing is avoidable, e.g.~batch or queueable traffic.)
\item
  \textbf{Treating an occupancy input as a measurement.} The cloud
  default (4,380 paid host-hours) is an assumption, not a trace; never
  promote it to a measured duty cycle or a guaranteed lower bound.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


TCO is the final gate in the design loop (Ch 12 candidates → Ch 14
benchmark → Ch 16 cost). It settles which candidate survives: the 70B
8×H100 self-host \textbf{passes the capability screen and is
analytically screened for feasibility --- it \emph{would} need a
representative hardware benchmark to establish SLO-goodput} (the
\texttt{C/W} and roofline estimates of Chs. 7--8 are planning bounds,
not a measured p95 TTFT/TPOT pass at the target offered load), and on
TCO it \textbf{sits above the managed API at the canonical 6.24M volume}
(\$155,816.93/mo vs \$129,792/mo --- the \textasciitilde7.5M API
break-even sits above the canonical volume) and above the cloud
default-occupancy input (\$87,600/mo) \textbf{on that input's
assumptions} --- so the surviving recommendation is \emph{conditional}
(self-host wins only once volume passes the \textasciitilde7.5M
break-even --- lower if the staff/ops term is sunk --- with sustained
near-peak utilization and a benchmark confirming the SLO; the cloud
alternative is lower-cost \textbf{only if} its paid-occupancy input
holds and the scopes are equalized; neither is an unconditional
ranking).

This completes the chain from vague requirement to a defensible, costed
architecture, while being honest that the TCO gate is not an
unconditional win and that the SLO pass is \emph{provisional until
benchmarked}.

It also feeds fleet decisions (Ch 17-18): the break-even curve is what
justifies buying a second host versus renting peaks, and the staff term
is what pushes toward consolidation (Ch 18).

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: TCO Discipline and the Staff
Term}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-tco-discipline-and-the-staff-term}

A startup's CTO shows the architect a warrant to buy two 8×H100 servers
for the new internal RAG assistant, citing ``we'll save on token fees.''
Applying TCO discipline, the architect does not dispute the raw token
math. Instead they price it: at this startup's \emph{actual} early
volume --- \textasciitilde2 rps average, \textasciitilde5M
requests/month, and only 0.5 engineers fully available for GPU ops ---
the break-even against a managed API is this chapter's own division:
\$40,000 ÷ \$0.0208 ≈ \textbf{1.9M requests/month}, so the current
volume is already \textasciitilde2.6× past the break-even.

The honest number: self-hosting two servers \textasciitilde\$40K/month
all-in vs \textasciitilde\$104K/month API at current volume (5M ×
\$0.0208) --- a \textbf{\textasciitilde\$64K/month saving in favor of
self-hosting, with the staff term already inside the \$40K} (capex
\textasciitilde\$11.6K + power \textasciitilde\$2.2K for two hosts
leaves ≤ \textasciitilde\$26K for staff --- upkeep is the power line
already counted, so \textasciitilde\$0.2K of the \$26.2K remainder is
unallocated --- too small to dwarf a \$64K saving). The staff term is on
a different basis than Mode 1: the mini-case prices a startup's 0.5-FTE
operator (\textasciitilde\$26K/mo --- ≈\$624K/yr fully loaded for one
engineer, a scenario value rather than a market rate), not Mode 1's
mature-operator alloc (\textasciitilde\$10K/mo for 21 hosts) ---
re-price on one staffing basis before comparing.

The token arithmetic therefore does not send this startup to the API; it
points to self-hosting. Only one term can flip the decision: the
\emph{opportunity} cost of the GPU-ops time the two servers consume ---
the better part of the only engineer the startup can spare. To justify
paying the API premium, that term must exceed
\textbf{\textasciitilde\$64K/month}, about \$770K/year of product work
the startup is not doing. The architect's verdict: \textbf{start on the
managed API now, and revisit self-host the moment the consumed
engineer's opportunity cost falls below \textasciitilde\$64K/month ---
for example when a second engineer frees up} --- then re-run the same
model. Request volume is not the lever: more volume only widens the
\textasciitilde\$64K gap. The CTO did not buy the servers. The
break-even graph did not make the answer self-evident; it narrowed the
whole decision to one labeled assumption --- this startup values its
half-engineer's product time above \$64K/month. A team that cannot say
that, or that has a second engineer to spare, runs the same numbers and
buys the servers.
