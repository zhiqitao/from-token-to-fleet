\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

After this chapter we should be able to take a vague deployment ask ---
``thousands of employees asking questions over internal documents'' ---
and turn it into a \emph{measurable workload} across six concrete
dimensions. We will trace the arithmetic from user concurrency through
traffic, token profile, latency, economics, and operational constraints,
ending with a workload characterization that directly enables system
architecture decisions. After this chapter, the question ``which model
should we choose?'' can be answered with numbers, not intuition.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


\subsection{The Six-Dimension Workload-Characterization
Framework}\label{the-six-dimension-workload-characterization-framework}

A workload is defined by six dimensions (\hyperref[tab:4.1]{Table 4-1} applies the framework
to the canonical workload). Together they form a complete specification
that --- jointly, not taken alone --- constrains model selection, system
architecture, and economic feasibility. No single dimension is
sufficient; the architect must characterize all six before committing to
a design.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Quality} --- the fidelity, accuracy, and capability the
  workload demands of the model. Includes reasoning depth, tool-use
  requirements, modality (text‑only, multimodal, etc.), and any accuracy
  thresholds (e.g.~\textgreater85\% factual accuracy on internal Q\&A).
  Quality is the \emph{goal}; the other dimensions are the
  \emph{constraints} within which quality must be delivered.
\item
  \textbf{Traffic} --- the request rate the system must sustain,
  expressed in requests per second (rps) or queries per second, plus its
  burst shape (sustained level, peak-to-average ratio, burst duration).
  \textbf{Concurrency is not a traffic input:} it is a serving-side
  quantity derived from the arrival rate and the time in system
  (Little's law), computed separately in this chapter as execution
  occupancy (§4.3). Traffic, combined with the token profile and the
  latency SLO, determines the compute and memory bandwidth required to
  keep the serving pipeline saturated without excessive queuing.
\item
  \textbf{Token profile} --- the distribution of input length, output
  length, and context length per request. This is the workload's
  ``shape'' in token space: how many tokens arrive in the prompt, how
  many the model generates, and whether the prompt is dense (long
  context) or sparse. The token profile is the primary driver of memory
  (KV cache) and prefill cost.
\item
  \textbf{Latency} --- the time budget for different stages of request
  processing. Typically split into \emph{time-to-first-token} (TTFT),
  which encompasses retrieval and prefill, and
  \emph{time-per-output-token} (TPOT), which governs decode speed (the
  rate of token generation, not the number of tokens generated). Latency
  budgets feed directly into SLOs and serve as the key differentiator
  between serving configurations (e.g.~continuous batching vs.~discrete
  batching).
\item
  \textbf{Economic constraints} --- the cost budget denominated in
  tokens per dollar, dollars per million tokens, or cost per request.
  This dimension translates traffic and token profile into a recurring
  cost line, and it determines whether a given infrastructure choice
  (single node vs.~multi‑node, FP16 vs.~FP8) is affordable at scale.
\item
  \textbf{Operational constraints} --- availability, privacy, locality,
  and update frequency. Multi‑region deployment for fault tolerance.
  Data‑privacy requirements that force on‑prem or VPC‑local embedding
  models. Update frequency that dictates how often embeddings or model
  weights must be refreshed. These constraints often interact with
  traffic and economics to force architectural compromises.
\end{enumerate}

The framework is deliberately dimensional: a workload that is
``high‑quality, high‑traffic, long‑context, low‑latency, cost‑sensitive,
private'' is fundamentally different from ``low‑quality, low‑traffic,
short‑context, high‑latency, cost‑abundant, public,'' even if the raw
text looks the same. The framework prevents the architect from
optimizing one dimension at the expense of others --- a common failure
mode.

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


The six dimensions are best read as the axes of a six‑dimensional space
in which every AI workload resides. A workload's position in this space
is its \emph{fingerprint}. When an architect says ``we need a model for
enterprise Q\&A,'' that is only a descriptor of the quality dimension.
To make the statement actionable, we must project the workload onto all
six axes: \emph{what quality level, how much traffic, what token
profile, what latency SLO, what economic ceiling, what operational
constraints?} The intersection of these projections is the workload
point that drives every downstream decision --- model selection,
infrastructure sizing, serving configuration, and TCO.

The mental model is not a checklist; it is a \emph{lens}. Looking
through it, the architect sees which dimensions are tight (binding) and
which are loose (permissive). Tight dimensions become the design
drivers; loose dimensions offer optimization freedom. The goal is to
identify the binding constraints so that resources are allocated where
they matter most.

\subsubsection{Arithmetic Walk‑Through: From Users to Declared Traffic
and Derived
Occupancy}\label{arithmetic-walkthrough-from-users-to-declared-traffic-and-derived-occupancy}

The step from ``\textasciitilde2,000 registered users'' to
``\textasciitilde10 rps average'' is the key connective tissue that
makes the continuous scenario concrete. Here is the
declaration-plus-occupancy walk-through, traceable line by line:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Concurrency}: a fraction \(f\) of the \(N\) registered users
  are active at any moment. With \(f = 0.05\) and \(N = 2{,}000\) (the
  active-user population is written \(U\); \(C\) is reserved throughout
  the book for a host's KV slot capacity, \(C ≈ 17.5\) --- §4.6, Ch 17):
\end{enumerate}

\[
U = f \cdot N = 0.05 \times 2{,}000 = 100 \text{ concurrent users}
\]

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{1}
\item
  \textbf{Request completion time} (service time \(W\)): the average
  end‑to‑end time from request submission to answer delivery. We
  deliberately keep TWO named values, and the distinction matters: a
  \textbf{planning round number of \textasciitilde10 s} (retrieval
  \textasciitilde120 ms + prefill budget cap \textasciitilde1.08 s +
  decode \textasciitilde7.5 s ≈ 8.7 s, plus \textasciitilde15\% planning
  headroom to \textasciitilde10 s --- an author-chosen planning figure,
  not a measurement) versus the \textbf{derived model-execution service
  time of \textasciitilde8.6 s} (prefill 1.08 s + decode 7.5 s, without
  the retrieval/plumbing term) that the later fleet chapters use in
  Little's-law sizing. The two differ by the retrieval/pipeline room and
  by rounding; they are not a numerical contradiction. Whichever we use,
  we must use it consistently --- the canonical ledger pins the
  fleet-sizing service time at \textasciitilde8.6 s (Chapters 16--17 use
  it for Little's-law occupancy; \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8} gives the prefill + decode
  decomposition), and the \textasciitilde10 s figure is the coarser
  planning-round headline. \textbf{The 1.08 s prefill term inside
  \(W_{\mathrm{exec}}\) is the two-term budget cap, not a modelled
  runtime} (\hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}: TTFT 1.2 s − retrieval 120 ms, with zero
  allowance for admission/queue and tokenization/transfer). The modelled
  host prefill is \textasciitilde0.16 s at peak and \textasciitilde0.47
  s at 35\% MFU (§4.3; Ch 2, Ch 8). So \(W_{\mathrm{exec}}\) ≈ 8.6 s is
  an \emph{upper} bound on the execution window, and the Little's-law
  occupancies (\textasciitilde86 / \textasciitilde344) and every host
  count derived from them (Chapters 7, 16--17) inherit that upper-bound
  status --- conservative, not measured.
\item
  \textbf{Arrival rate --- a declared traffic assumption, NOT a
  Little's-law derivation from the active-user population}: Little's
  Law, \(L = \lambda W\), relates the average number of \emph{requests
  in the defined system} \(L\), the arrival rate \(\lambda\), and the
  mean time in system \(W\). It cannot be applied to a count of
  \emph{active people} --- 100 recently-active users is not the same as
  100 requests in the serving system. So the \textasciitilde10 rps
  average is \textbf{declared as a scenario input} (≈ 2,000 registered
  users × one request \textasciitilde every 200 s → \textasciitilde10
  rps), not solved for from the 100-user population.
\item
  \textbf{Peak arrival rate}: \textasciitilde40 rps is the same behavior
  scaled to a burst/traffic spike --- again a declared scenario input
  (see step 3).
\end{enumerate}

The walk-through makes the \emph{distinctions} explicit: the
\textbf{\textasciitilde100 active users} are a population/activity
measure, not a request-occupancy count; the \textbf{\textasciitilde10
rps average / \textasciitilde40 rps peak} are declared traffic
assumptions (scenario inputs), not derived from the user count; the
\textbf{\textasciitilde86 / \textasciitilde344 execution occupancy} is
derived from those arrival rates via Little's Law (L = λ ×
\(W_{\mathrm{exec}}\), \(W_{\mathrm{exec}}\) ≈ 8.6 s). The point of the
exercise is to keep population, arrival rate, and occupancy separate,
not to claim that a registered-user count empirically determines an
arrival rate. Change any one assumption and the others shift
accordingly; the architect recomputes them when the requirement changes.
If the architect starts from a measured 10 rps instead, the relation
runs the other way (it yields \textasciitilde86 in-flight at W ≈ 8.6 s,
not a user count).

Before moving on, it is worth naming the four distinct quantities so
they are not collapsed: \textbf{registered users} are the population;
\textbf{concurrent users} are the activity state (active users in the
modeled window, not in-flight requests); \textbf{arrival rate} (λ) is
requests per unit time; and \textbf{service time} (W) is the end-to-end
duration of a request. Little's Law, L = λW, relates only the
request-level quantities --- L (in-flight requests), λ, and W --- not
the registered- or active-user population; the architect must
\emph{estimate or measure} request concurrency, arrival rate, and
service time --- none is a fixed property of the workload. Two further
separations matter for KV planning and are easy to collapse, so we hold
them apart here too:

\textbf{``Concurrent users'' here means \emph{active users}, not
simultaneous in-flight inference requests.} The 5\% figure gives
\textasciitilde100 active users in the modeled activity window ---
people who are using the tool over the window --- not 100 requests that
are all mid-execution at the same instant. The in-flight \emph{request}
concurrency is a different quantity, computed from Little's Law with the
derived service time W ≈ 8.6 s. The two operating points are distinct
rows, not one figure reconciled by an unstated multiplier:

The reason these columns differ in kind is that \textbf{active-user
population} and \textbf{request occupancy} are different state
variables. Active users is a \emph{population} measure --- how many
people engaged the tool over the modeled window. Request occupancy is an
\emph{instantaneous} Little's-law quantity --- how many requests are
resident in the serving system at a moment. They are related only
through the arrival rate and service time, not through a per-user
request multiplicity. So at the average operating point the population
(\textasciitilde100) and the occupancy (\textasciitilde86) happen to be
close, while at the peak they differ (400 vs 344); neither gap needs a
``one user issues several requests'' reconciliation, and we do not claim
one. Whenever the book says ``concurrent users,'' read ``active users in
the modeled activity window''; whenever it needs the request-level
occupancy that a host's KV budget must absorb, use the Little's-law λ·W
figure for that specific operating point.

\textbf{The \textasciitilde40 rps peak is assumed sustained over the
provisioning burst interval, and the interval now has a stated duration:
up to \textasciitilde15 minutes} \emph{\mbox{{[}canonical
scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} declared burst-length input, not a measured
traffic trace} --- long enough for Little's Law and service-time
occupancy to reach the modeled steady state (at least the service-time
scale, \(W_{\mathrm{exec}}\) ≈ 8.6 s --- not a one-second or few-second
spike), and not a one-request 40-rps spike. The \textasciitilde15-minute
bound is the time basis the peak-fleet sizing inherits (Ch 17): a longer
peak keeps the same provisioned count but eats the 95\% SLO headroom and
the N−1 margin, while a much shorter burst can be absorbed by a burst
buffer instead of a provisioned peak fleet --- which is why the
average-fleet and peak-fleet figures (6 vs 21 hosts) are only comparable
once this duration is stated. A one-second spike would not populate the
queue to λ·W; the fleet sizing deliberately treats 40 rps as the
sustained burst the system must absorb. A load generator reproducing
this workload should emit the same sustained arrival process, not merely
target the same average rps.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Concurrent \emph{users} ≠ concurrent \emph{requests}.} ``100
  concurrent users'' is a statement about the \emph{population's
  activity}, not about how many requests are in flight at the scheduler
  at any instant. The two are different state variables, and they need
  not be close --- at the average operating point they happen to be
  (\textasciitilde100 vs \textasciitilde86), at the peak they differ
  (\textasciitilde400 vs \textasciitilde344).
\item
  \textbf{Requests \emph{in flight} ≠ KV-resident sequences.} A request
  in the queue, or one whose prefill is not yet admitted, is ``in
  flight'' from the user's perspective but does not yet hold a KV slot.
  In-flight concurrency upper-bounds the KV-concurrency the system must
  serve, but the KV budget is sized by the number of \emph{resident
  active sequences} --- which is what the concurrency ceilings in Ch 17
  count. Little's Law gives the in-flight number; the KV budget is a
  separate, residency-side quantity obtained by multiplying the resident
  sequence count by per-token KV. Keeping these three apart (in flight
  vs resident vs per-token KV) is what prevents the ``100 users → 100 KV
  slots'' leap.
\end{itemize}

The ``5\% active'' and the ``\textasciitilde10 s planning headline''
(the planning service-time assumption --- not the \(W_{\mathrm{exec}}\)
≈ 8.6 s used in occupancy arithmetic) are deliberately simple scenario
assumptions (\mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}), not externally
verified facts; in a real engagement the architect replaces them with
observed concurrency, measured latency, and a burst profile from
telemetry. Getting this separation right is what turns traffic modeling
from a guess into a reproducible arithmetic.

\subsubsection{Token Profile in Detail}\label{token-profile-in-detail}

The canonical workload's token profile is input‑heavy, which has direct
consequences for system design:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Per‑request input}: 1,200 tokens (enterprise query, possibly
  reformulated) + 8,000 tokens (retrieved context from vector DB) ≈
  9,200 tokens. The \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} provenance
  traces to the canonical scenario (Ch 4, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}).
\item
  \textbf{Per‑request output}: \textasciitilde300 tokens (the generated
  answer, possibly with citations).
\item
  \textbf{Input‑to‑output ratio}: 9,200 ÷ 300 ≈ 30× more input tokens
  than output tokens. This ratio is \mbox{{[}canonical
  scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} from the canonical numbers and sets the
  relative prefill‑versus‑decode \emph{work} split; the 9,200‑token
  absolute context length is what sets KV residency per request and
  therefore the concurrency ceiling. The canonical workload's FLOP share
  is \textbf{prefill-heavy} and its capacity pressure is
  \textbf{KV-heavy}, but its per-request wall-clock is
  \textbf{decode-heavy} --- because the 300-token output at
  \textasciitilde25 ms/TPOT costs \textasciitilde7.5 s of the
  \textasciitilde8.6 s execution window, not because of the input/output
  ratio. These are three different senses of load (share of FLOPs,
  capacity pressure, share of wall-clock), and none contradicts the
  others (KV residency also scales with context length × concurrency ×
  bytes/token, and decode throughput must still be checked at the target
  batch size and SLO).
\item
  \textbf{At 10 rps}: input tokens/s = 9,200 ×10 = 92,000 tokens/s;
  output tokens/s = 300 ×10 = 3,000 tokens/s. At peak 40 rps, input
  spikes to \textasciitilde368,000 tokens/s and output to
  \textasciitilde12,000 tokens/s. These \mbox{{[}canonical
  scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} numbers are the Canonical Workload box's
  derived rates (above).
\end{itemize}

The input-heavy profile means the system's dominant resource demands are
\textbf{prefill compute and KV-memory capacity}; under the canonical
timing assumptions prefill is \textbf{not} the dominant wall-clock
component of an individual request. The 1.08 s prefill figure is the
\emph{two-term budget cap} (TTFT 1.2 s − retrieval 120 ms; \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}),
not a modeled prefill runtime: the modeled host prefill is
\textasciitilde0.16 s at peak and \textasciitilde0.47 s at 35\% MFU (Ch
2), and only the budget-cap term enters the execution window used here.
On that budget-cap basis the window is \textasciitilde8.58 s and
generating the 300-token answer takes about 7.5 s (300 × 25 ms/TPOT) ---
so decode is the dominant per-request wall-clock component
(\textasciitilde87\% of the \textasciitilde8.58 s window: 7.5 ÷ 8.58 =
87.4\%; at the modeled \textasciitilde0.47 s prefill the share is
higher, 7.5 ÷ 7.97 ≈ 94\%; the end-to-end user-visible total
additionally carries retrieval \textasciitilde120 ms and
admission/queue, so the end-to-end decode share is marginally lower). A
model that processes the 9,200-token input inside that 1.08 s prefill
budget is important to keep the prefill leg within the 1.2 s TTFT
budget, but it does not make prefill the latency bottleneck; that
distinction is a resource-regime statement (in the canonical
long-prompt, low-batch regime, prefill is compute-bound and decode is
bandwidth-bound), not a wall-clock latency ranking. Architecture choices
must be based on phase-specific demand, measured utilization, and
SLO-goodput, rather than the input/output token ratio alone. This is why
the token profile is the primary architectural driver for this workload.

\subsubsection{Latency from SLO}\label{latency-from-slo}

The latency dimension is specified through Service Level Objectives,
which translate user‑experience goals into concrete time budgets:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{TTFT (time-to-first-token)}: budget of 1.2 s, comprising
  retrieval (\textasciitilde120 ms for vector search and reranking on a
  local GPU) + prefill (budget cap 1.08 s for 9,200 tokens across the
  8×H100 canonical host --- a \emph{budget}, not a modeled runtime. The
  cap implies ≈8,500 tokens/s (9,200 ÷ 1.08 = 8,519): that is only the
  budget-implied single-request rate for a request that holds the whole
  host, not the modeled or measured prefill rate. The modeled host
  prefill is \textasciitilde0.16 s at peak and \textasciitilde0.47 s at
  35\% MFU, and the host's capacity screen is the \textasciitilde19,800
  tok/s sustained-planning rate at 35\% MFU --- a planning rate, not a
  ceiling. The minimum GPU count for this single-request prefill
  deadline under the stated 35\% MFU \emph{planning assumption}
  \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} is four, not eight --- an
  analytical \textbf{lower bound conditional on that assumption}, not a
  determined count: the 9.2K prefill is ≈1.29 PFLOP of work at 140
  GFLOP/token, implying ≈1.19 PFLOPS of required compute rate over 1.08
  s (9,200 × 140 GFLOP ÷ 1.08 s); one H100 sustains \textasciitilde2,472
  tok/s at 35\% MFU, so a single GPU needs \textasciitilde3.7 s for it
  (9,200 ÷ 2,472 = 3.72 s) and fails the cap, while ⌈1.19 PFLOPS ÷
  (0.989 × 0.35)⌉ ≈ 4 GPUs meet it --- the 8-GPU host needs
  \textasciitilde0.47 s (9,200 ÷ 19,780 = 0.465 s). That four is a
  per-request prefill-feasibility \emph{floor} under the 35\% MFU
  planning assumption \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}: real MFU
  below 35\% raises the count, so verify it by benchmark before sizing
  on it (Ch 14). It is not a determined count, not a deployable GPU
  count, and not a deployment recommendation. The canonical 8-GPU choice
  is driven by concurrency, KV capacity and headroom, and the deployable
  host count is Ch 17's (\hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}), not this cap's. Per Ch 2 and Ch
  8). The \textbf{p95 TTFT ceiling (≤ 2 s)} must hold --- a tail ceiling
  above the 1.2 s design budget, allowing occasional cache misses or
  network jitter to land between the 1.2 s design budget and the 2 s
  ceiling. The two thresholds are not interchangeable: 1.2 s is the
  design budget, ≤ 2 s is the p95 ceiling the budget relaxes to, and any
  pass/fail judgement on a measured distribution is made against the p95
  ceiling, not the budget. \textbf{TTFT\_e2e convention (used
  throughout):}
  \(\text{TTFT}_{\text{e2e}} = \text{retrieval} + \text{admission/queue} + \text{tokenization/transfer} + \text{prefill}\).
  Here the 1.2 s budget names the \emph{two dominant modeled terms} ---
  retrieval (\textasciitilde120 ms) + prefill (\textasciitilde1.08 s)
  --- and the 1.08 s is therefore a \textbf{two-term-only upper reach,
  NOT a feasible deployed prefill target}: it leaves zero allowance for
  admission/queue and tokenization/transfer, which are positive. Write
  it as \texttt{budget\_prefill\ ≤\ 1.08\ s\ −\ B\_other} until
  measurement supplies B\_other; every ``meets 1.2 s'' statement needs a
  named tested workload and end-to-end timing (see the ``Prefill compute
  budget --- two-term-only upper cap, NOT a feasible deployed prefill
  target'' row in \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}). The 1.2 s is an \textbf{aspirational SLO
  budget} (a sub-2-second user-experience target), not a demonstrated
  p95 TTFT from a benchmark; the ≤2 s p95 is the budgeted ceiling, and
  the demonstrated value under load requires an actual measurement.
\item
  \textbf{TPOT (time-per-output-token)}: budget of \textasciitilde25
  ms/token. A 300‑token answer therefore takes \textasciitilde7.5 s of
  total decode time. p95 TPOT ≤ 35 ms/token provides headroom for
  batching variability.
\end{itemize}

These budgets are \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} from typical
enterprise Q\&A user expectations (sub‑2‑second feel) and from the
hardware's published prefill/decode rates. They are the latency
constraints that every subsequent architectural decision must respect.

\subsubsection{Economic Constraints}\label{economic-constraints}

The economic dimension translates the token profile and traffic into a
cost structure:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Infrastructure}: A single host with 8×H100 GPUs (640 GB total
  GPU memory, 140 GB model FP16 weights fit with room for KV cache).
  On-demand rental (operating) cost ≈ \$20/hour (\$2.50/GPU-hr), or
  \textasciitilde\$2,500/month reserved (illustrative committed-use
  discount; a \textasciitilde83\% discount on H100 on-demand is
  unusually deep and differs from the on-demand basis the canonical box
  uses) --- the canonical box uses the on-demand basis.
\item
  \textbf{Token pricing}: \textasciitilde\$1.20 per million input tokens
  and \textasciitilde\$2.00 per million output tokens as a mid-tier
  managed-API list price (2026 snapshot) --- a provider's price for a
  hosted service, not a rate for the canonical H100 instance, which is
  priced separately at \textasciitilde\$20/hr on-demand (see the
  Infrastructure bullet). The frontier-class API used in the Ch 16 TCO
  comparison is priced higher (\textasciitilde\$2/\$8 per 1M) --- a
  different provider tier, not the same line item.
\item
  \textbf{Throughput per dollar}: At 10 rps average, the system
  processes \textasciitilde92,000 input tokens/s + \textasciitilde3,000
  output tokens/s. \textbf{The dollar denominator must be the whole
  fleet that serves this rate, not one host.} At the
  \textasciitilde6-host average the whole-fleet spend is
  \textasciitilde\$120/hour (≈ \$0.0333 per second), so
  \textasciitilde92,000 / 0.0333 ≈ \textbf{\textasciitilde2.76 million
  input tokens per \$1 of fleet spend} and \textasciitilde3,000 / 0.0333
  ≈ \textbf{\textasciitilde90,000 output tokens per \$1} --- the
  \textbf{active-hours basis} (\textasciitilde\$120/hour ×
  \textasciitilde173.33 active h/mo ≈ \$20,800/mo, the hour count
  matching the 6.24M active-hours request volume); on the
  \textbf{wall-clock paid-occupancy basis} (\$87,600/mo --- the same
  fleet paid for all 730 h/mo --- ÷ the same month's
  \textbf{\textasciitilde57.4B input tokens, an active-hours (173.33 h)
  volume}) the same volume gives \textbf{\textasciitilde655K input
  tokens per \$1} --- wall-clock spend over active-hours tokens, an
  hour-basis mismatch and not a 730-hour token rate. The 2.76M and 655K
  figures are \textbf{not comparable}: they differ only in which hour
  count divides the spend (173.33 vs 730), not in the workload or the
  fleet. These are \emph{modeled, whole-fleet} figures (before
  SLO/headroom/failover). A separate quantity is a single host's
  \emph{sustained-planning} prefill rate (\textasciitilde19,800 tok/s at
  35\% MFU, Ch 8 --- a planning rate, not a ceiling: the peak-derived
  ceiling is \textasciitilde56.5K tok/s): \textasciitilde19,800 × 3,600
  / \$20 ≈ \textbf{\textasciitilde3.56M prefill tokens per host-dollar}
  --- a different denominator and a different rate, and \textbf{not} a
  claim that one host serves the offered load. Never present a
  tokens-per-dollar figure with the offered workload rate divided by a
  single-host price. \textbf{Decision rule:} quote the
  \textasciitilde2.76M fleet basis for procurement comparisons --- what
  the whole build delivers per cloud dollar --- and the
  \textasciitilde3.56M host basis only for per-host utilization
  questions --- how hard one purchased host runs at its planning prefill
  rate. (\hyperref[chap:5]{Chapter 5} expresses the same economics on a GPU list-price
  basis as \textbf{tokens per dollar}; see its throughput-basis
  reconciliation note before cross-chapter comparison --- \hyperref[chap:5]{Chapter 5}'s
  \textbf{9,000 tokens per dollar} is single-stream (50 tok/s), while
  this chapter's \textbf{\textasciitilde2.76M input tokens per \$1 of
  fleet spend} (\textasciitilde2.85M combined) is whole-fleet
  aggregate.)
\item
  \textbf{Cost per request}: At 10 rps, each request carries
  \textasciitilde9,500 tokens (9,200 input + 300 output). At the
  per‑million rates, cost per request ≈ (9,200 × \$1.20/1M) + (300 ×
  \$2.00/1M) = \$0.01104 + \$0.0006 = \$0.01164 ≈ \textbf{\$0.012 per
  request} per inference cycle (M = 1,000,000 per the book's SI
  convention, so the 9.2 K input is 9,200 tokens, not 9.2). The
  per-request cost stays \textasciitilde\$0.012; what scales linearly
  with request rate is the \emph{aggregate} spend --- per second or per
  month --- not the per-request figure. \textbf{Basis note --- two
  different cost bases, not interchangeable.} The \$0.012 figure is
  derived from \emph{per-token market/API list prices} (\$1.20/M in,
  \$2.00/M out) for the \emph{mid-tier} managed API; it is a
  cloud-provider price a customer would pay per request on that hosted
  API. (The \emph{frontier-class} API used in the Ch 16 TCO comparison
  is priced higher --- \textasciitilde\$2/\$8 per 1M,
  \textasciitilde\$0.0208 per request; see the token-pricing bullet
  above for the tier distinction.) It is \emph{not} the fleet owner's
  marginal infrastructure cost. The owned-fleet cost is nearly all fixed
  capex/opex and barely scales per request, but it must be computed on
  the \textbf{whole fleet}, not one host: the \$20/hr is the price of a
  \emph{single} 8×H100 host, while the canonical 10 rps average / 40 rps

  \textbf{\textasciitilde\$0.0033 per request} at average, and (21 ×

  peak (6/21-host full-util basis; the 70\% × 95\% + N−1 policy fleet

  \textasciitilde\$0.0056 / \textasciitilde\$0.0044) --- average and

  the full-util formula gives, because both divide an hour-matched spend
  by an hour-matched volume. On the \textbf{wall-clock paid-occupancy
  basis} (the sidebar's \$87,600/mo --- the fleet paid for all 730 h/mo,

  1K, i.e.~\textbf{\textasciitilde\$0.014 per request}. The 4.2× gap
  between \$0.0033 and \$0.014 is the duty cycle (730 ÷ 173.33 = 4.21),
  not a different fleet. (Dividing one host's \$20/hr by the whole
  workload's 10 rps would give \textasciitilde\$0.00056, but that is
  dimensionally wrong: one host cannot serve 10 rps of this workload.)
  Keep the two bases separate: use the per-token price for a
  \emph{hosted/API} cost model, and the fleet \$/hr ÷ fleet rps for an
  \emph{owned fleet}. The two are juxtaposed in the cost-basis table
  below (§4.3) only to show the hosted-API pricing basis, not as an
  owned-fleet marginal cost.
\end{itemize}

The economic constraint is what makes the workload real: a 70B FP16
model on one host \emph{is analytically feasible against the per‑request
latency SLO} (TTFT/TPOT) under the first-order Ch 2 model --- and only
in that model's two-term (retrieval + prefill) form, with B\_other
unmeasured (\hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}) --- end-to-end timing must be confirmed by
benchmark (per the TTFT bullet in §4.3) --- but the \emph{capacity}
question, how many hosts absorb the \textasciitilde10 rps average /
\textasciitilde40 rps peak arrival, is a separate computation. As
Chapters 8 and 16--17 show, a single 8×H100 host holds
\textasciitilde17.5 KV‑resident requests (436 GB KV budget ÷ 24.9 GB per
9.5K-token resident request ≈ 17.51, §4.6). \textbf{Whole-slot
convention (canonical statement in § 7.3, \hyperref[fig:7.7]{Fig 7.7}):} the canonical
concurrency ceiling is that resident-basis quotient floored to a whole
slot --- \textbf{17}, not 18. The input-only quotient (436 ÷ 24.1 ≈
18.1) is shown for comparison only and is never used for fleet sizing;
the two differ by one host at every traffic level in Ch 17 (⌈344/17⌉ =
21 hosts against ⌈344/18⌉ = 20). So one host cannot carry the
\textasciitilde40 rps peak (\textasciitilde344 in flight); the canonical
workload needs on the order of \textasciitilde6 hosts at the average and
\textasciitilde21 hosts at the peak. Scaling to higher traffic or longer
contexts pushes the host count further, and the cost line must be
re‑evaluated.

without labelling the basis confuses readers into treating basis B as if
it were basis A.

\textbf{Cost-basis reconciliation for the canonical workload --- six
figures, four bases, never cross-compared.} The chapter carries six
per-request figures for this one workload. Read each on its own basis:
\textbf{(a) \textasciitilde\$0.0116 per request} --- mid-tier
managed-API \emph{list price}, 9,200 × \$1.20/1M + 300 × \$2.00/1M =
\$0.01104 + \$0.0006; \textbf{(b) \textasciitilde\$0.0208 per request}
--- the \emph{frontier-class} API list price (\textasciitilde\$2/\$8 per
1M), a different provider tier on the same API basis; \textbf{(c)
\textasciitilde\$0.0033 per request} (≈\$3.33 per 1K) --- \emph{owned
fleet}, 6-host average at full utilization on the hour-matched
active-hours basis (\$120/hr × 173.33 h ≈ \$20,800/mo ÷ 6.24M requests);
\textbf{(d) \textasciitilde\$0.0140 per request} (≈\$14.04 per 1K) ---
the \emph{same} owned 6-host fleet paid for all 730 h/mo and divided by
the same active-hours volume, i.e.~the hour-basis artifact (730 ÷ 173.33
= 4.21), not a costlier design. \textbf{(e) \textasciitilde\$0.0029 per
request} --- the \emph{same rate form as (c)} at the peak traffic level
(21-host full-utilization fleet ÷ 40 rps), a traffic-level variant, not
a new basis; \textbf{(f) \textasciitilde\$0.0056 /
\textasciitilde\$0.0044 per request} --- the \emph{policy-fleet variant}
of the same rate form (10 provisioned hosts at average, 32 at peak, ÷ 10
rps and ÷ 40 rps), a fleet-size sensitivity, not a costlier design.
\textbf{The one owned-fleet figure to quote: (c) \textasciitilde\$0.0033
per request (≈\$3.33 per 1K) --- owned fleet, hour-matched active-hours
basis, 6-host average at the \textasciitilde10 rps average.} Every other
owned-fleet number --- (d), (e), (f) --- is a basis, traffic-level or
fleet-size variant of it, and none replaces it. \textbf{The comparison
rule:} API basis against API basis only (a vs b --- the
\textasciitilde1.8× tier gap), owned-fleet basis against owned-fleet
basis only (c vs d --- a duty-cycle sensitivity), never across. The
canonical workload's \textbf{economic constraint} is written against the
whole-fleet monthly spend on the \textbf{730-hour paid-occupancy basis}
--- provisioned cost, not utilized cost:
\textasciitilde\$87.6K--\$306.6K/mo at full utilization (6 hosts ×
\textasciitilde\$14.6K; 21 hosts × \textasciitilde\$14.6K ≈ \$306.6K),
\textasciitilde\$146K--\$467K/mo on the 70\% × 95\% + N−1 policy fleet
(⌈6 ÷ 0.665⌉ = 10 provisioned hosts ≈ \$146K; ⌈21 ÷ 0.665⌉ = 32
provisioned hosts ≈ \$467K), single-region N−1 only. The same fleets on
the hour-matched active-hours basis spend
\textasciitilde\$20.8K--\$72.8K/mo (6 × 173.33 h × \$20 ≈ \$20,800; 21 ×
173.33 h × \$20 ≈ \$72,800) --- the two ranges are not one cost: the
730-hour figures pay for idle hours, the active-hours figures do not.
The \textasciitilde\$3.33/1K (6-host average) and
\textasciitilde\$49.13/1K (21-host peak) lines are quoted only after
naming the fleet \emph{and} the hour basis; (a) is the figure to quote
when the alternative under evaluation is a managed API, and it is the
number the \textasciitilde\$0.0030 post-2K-input figure in §4.6 belongs
to.

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


For this chapter, measurement is about \textbf{quantifying the six
dimensions} so the workload can be communicated definitively and used to
drive architecture decisions. The token-counting habits of § 1.4 apply
unchanged (run the model's own tokenizer, split input from output, log
the distribution not just the mean) --- rather than restate them, the
three things the \emph{six-dimension} view adds are:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Quantify each dimension with a number, not a label.} For the
  six dimensions (quality, traffic, token profile, latency, economic
  constraints, operational constraints), attach a concrete measured or
  derived figure. ``RAG'' is not a load; ``1.2K prompt + 8K retrieved,
  300 out, 40 rps peak, p95 TTFT ≤ 2 s'' is.
\item
  \textbf{Tie the token split to the bottleneck it drives.} An
  input-heavy profile (like RAG's 30× ratio) means prefill compute and
  KV-capacity pressure dominate; an output-heavy profile would dominate
  decode. State which side dominates, because it decides where the money
  and the architecture effort go (Ch 8).
\item
  \textbf{Record the peak and the tail, not just the average.} A
  workload that averages 9,200 input tokens but has a long tail
  (e.g.~32K peak contexts) sizes the fleet very differently. Log
  percentiles (p90, p99) alongside the average and size against the
  peak.
\end{enumerate}

These habits answer the book's recurring question, ``what would I
actually measure here?'' --- and they let the six dimensions be stated
as numbers an architecture decision can be defended on.

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Treating ``5\% concurrency'' as the throughput}. Concurrency
  is a snapshot of simultaneous users; throughput depends on how long
  each request takes. 100 concurrent in-flight requests with a 200 s
  service time yield 0.5 rps, not 10 rps. The binding connection is
  through request duration (Little's Law). Catch this by always asking
  ``how long is each request in system?'' before quoting a
  concurrency-derived throughput.
\item
  \textbf{Using the \textasciitilde10 s planning round as Little's-law
  W.} The planning round (\textasciitilde10 s) is not the execution
  service time (W\_exec ≈ 8.6 s); plugging \textasciitilde10 s into L =
  λW gives occupancies of 100/400 --- exactly the active-user population
  --- and conflates people with requests. Use W\_exec for occupancy (the
  tokenizer-side traps --- token ≈ word, context window as free capacity
  --- are § 1.5's; measure there, not re-derived here).
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring the input--output asymmetry}. An input-heavy workload
  like RAG tends to increase \emph{prefill compute} and
  \emph{KV-capacity} pressure --- it does not by itself make the system
  ``memory-bandwidth-bound.'' In the canonical model, prefill is
  \textbf{compute-bound} under the analytical roofline, while long input
  raises \textbf{KV residency}, which constrains sustainable concurrency
  (a capacity constraint, distinct from bandwidth). An output-heavy
  workload is \textbf{decode-dominant} (bandwidth/runtime per generated
  token). Raising \emph{aggregate} decode throughput (output tokens/s
  across the fleet, e.g.~via more batching) on an input-heavy workload
  moves the needle negligibly --- the binding constraints are KV
  residency and prefill, not output rate; but per-request decode rate
  and TPOT are reciprocals, so reducing TPOT \emph{does} shorten service
  time and host count --- the same lever read per request, not a
  different one. The real win is prefill reduction, prefix/KV reuse, or
  increasing effective carrying capacity.
\item
  \textbf{Sizing KV and the fleet from the mean context and the average
  fleet.} The 9,200-token mean hides the tail (a 32 K context costs
  \textasciitilde84 GB of KV per request, §4.7), and the
  \textasciitilde6-host average fleet is not the \textasciitilde21-host
  peak fleet --- size against the tail context and the peak rate, not
  the mean.
\item
  \textbf{Overlooking operational constraints}. Multi--region deployment
  adds replication cost; privacy requirements may force a more expensive
  on-prem embedding model. These are often the true binding constraints,
  not the per-token price.
\end{itemize}

\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Model selection}: A 70B FP16 dense model (140 GB weights) fits
  on a single host with 8×H100 (640 GB GPU memory). The model is large
  enough to follow instructions and synthesize retrieved context without
  task‑specific fine‑tuning (factual accuracy still depends on retrieval
  quality, context, and evaluation), but the 140 GB footprint means KV
  cache for a 9,200-token context adds \textasciitilde24.1 GB of GPU
  memory per request (24.9 GB on the resident slot-counting basis),
  leaving headroom but not abundance.
\item
  \textbf{Serving configuration}: One host is the per-request latency
  baseline, not the capacity answer: at 10 rps the offered prefill load
  is \textasciitilde92,000 tok/s against one host's
  \textasciitilde19,800 tok/s sustained-planning rate, so prefill alone
  screens at ≥5 hosts at average (92,000 ÷ 19,800 = 4.65, ceiled --- an
  \emph{analytical bound on a planning rate, not a capacity result});
  the KV-residency screen (86 in-flight ÷ 17 slots = 5.06) is the
  binding bound and raises this to \textasciitilde6 hosts (§4.3). On
  each host, continuous batching (e.g.~vLLM) is nearly mandatory under
  this load to hold the 1.2 s TTFT budget: it reduces queueing and
  improves utilization under concurrency (it does not shorten a single
  request's 9,200-token prefill); without admission scheduling, queued
  prefills can push p95 TTFT past the \textbf{p95 TTFT ceiling (≤ 2 s)}
  --- the tail ceiling, not the 1.2 s design budget --- chunked prefill
  and priority scheduling are the levers that address prefill/decode
  interference directly. The input‑heavy token profile (30× more input
  than output) makes continuous batching especially effective: with long
  prefills and short outputs, decode slots would otherwise sit idle
  between admissions, and continuous batching keeps them filled. The
  benefit of many requests sharing the same retrieved-context prefix
  belongs to prefix caching --- a distinct mechanism (§ 2.1, developed
  in Ch 11) --- not to continuous batching.
\item
  \textbf{Memory planning}: KV cache for a 9,200-token context on the
  canonical model --- a 70B \textbf{full-MHA reference model} (every
  query head has its own K,V, so K/V width = hidden\_dim = 8192) --- is
  2 × layers × hidden × bytes = 2,621,440 B ≈ 2.62 MB/token (\hyperref[chap:7]{Chapter 7}),
  giving ≈9,200 ×2.62 MB ≈ 24.1 GB of initial KV per request; a
  \emph{resident} request also holds its 300 output tokens, so the
  slot-counting basis is 9,500 ×2.62 MB ≈ 24.9 GB. This is the MHA
  upper-bound baseline; a GQA model (8 KV-heads, d\_head=128) would
  carry only \textasciitilde0.33 MB/token, \textasciitilde8× less (Ch
  7). At 10 concurrent full-context requests the MHA figure is
  \textasciitilde241 GB of KV on top of the 140 GB weights --- 381 GB of
  the 640 GB pool --- and the pool presses well before concurrency
  reaches 100 (\textasciitilde17 full-context requests once the
  \textasciitilde64 GB runtime is reserved: 436 GB ÷ 24.9 GB ≈ 17.5,
  floored to 17 --- the input-only 24.1 GB figure above would give 436 ÷
  24.1 ≈ 18 before the output tokens are counted). That 17-slot ceiling
  is a \textbf{per-host} figure, and its binding limit is
  \textbf{per-rank}: an 80 GB rank runs 99.97\% full at 17.5 slots (Ch
  7, Technical note --- per-rank fit: 17.5 GB weights + 54.48 GB KV + 8
  GB runtime/NCCL = 79.98 GB of 80 GB), so the per-rank test --- not
  this pool-level arithmetic --- decides residency. Read the 381
  GB-of-640 GB sentence as a pool-level illustration, not as the
  headroom test. The architecture must therefore limit concurrency,
  quantize KV (FP8 → \textasciitilde1.31 MB/token, \textasciitilde12.06
  GB/request at the same 9,200-token context), or use a GQA
  architecture. This is the same KV-residency discipline developed fully
  in Chapters 7 and 17.
\item
  \textbf{Economic feasibility}: Two cost bases meet here and are not
  interchangeable: \textasciitilde\$20/hour per 8×H100 host (owned-fleet
  basis --- a cloud rental price at \$2.50/GPU-hr, not a capex/energy
  self-host TCO) and \textasciitilde\$0.012 per request at mid-tier API
  list prices (from \$1.20/M input × 9,200 + \$2.00/M output × 300 ---
  an API-basis figure, not a self-host TCO). On the \textbf{API basis}
  the request cost is driven by the 9,200 input tokens: if the input
  token count could be reduced to 2 K (e.g.~tighter retrieval/reranking
  to fewer chunks, context compression), or lower effective prefill cost
  for the same tokens (prefix caching, if priced favorably), the
  per-request \emph{API list} cost falls from \$0.01164 to ≈\$0.0030 ---
  the input leg goes from 9,200 × \$1.20/1M = \$0.01104 to 2,000 ×
  \$1.20/1M = \$0.00240 while the output leg stays 300 × \$2.00/1M =
  \$0.0006, so the total moves \$0.01164 → ≈\$0.0030, a
  \textasciitilde3.9× reduction (\$0.01164 ÷ \$0.0030 = 3.88). That is a
  hosted-API figure and says nothing directly about fleet capacity. On
  the \textbf{owned-fleet basis} the same input reduction lowers prefill
  work and the KV footprint per request, which changes the host-count
  screen: a 2,000-input + 300-output slot costs 2,300 × 2.62 MB ≈ 6.03
  GB, so one host's 436 GB KV budget holds 436 ÷ 6.03 ≈ 72 slots instead
  of 17, and the KV screen falls to ⌈86 ÷ 72⌉ = 2 hosts at average and
  ⌈344 ÷ 72⌉ = 5 at peak; prefill screens at the same counts (10 × 2,000
  ÷ 19,800 = 1.01 → 2, 40 × 2,000 ÷ 19,800 = 4.04 → 5). Those are
  \emph{analytical screens on the planning basis, not capacity results}
  --- Ch 17 recomputes them. Compute the cost on the fleet \$/hr ÷ fleet
  rps basis: (5 × \$20/hr) ÷ (40 rps × 3,600 s/hr) ≈ \$0.0007 per
  request at peak, against (21 × \$20/hr) ÷ (40 rps × 3,600 s/hr) ≈
  \$0.0029 at 9.2K input --- not from this \$/request API number. This
  is the lever the architect pulls when TCO is the binding constraint.
\item
  \textbf{Operational topology}: Multi‑region deployment (active‑active)
  provides region-failure tolerance at an illustrative \textasciitilde2×
  infrastructure cost --- a figure that is \textbf{UNPRICED / NOT
  VALIDATED} in this book's TCO/fleet arithmetic, not a priced line (see
  caveat below); 99.9\% availability alone does not require it, since a
  single-region multi-AZ design can meet 99.9\% depending on the failure
  domains. If the SLA requires region-failure tolerance, the
  architecture must absorb a separately modeled active-active cost ---
  not necessarily 2×. If it is negotiable, a single-region with
  graceful-degrading fallback may suffice. The operational constraint
  thus directly sets the economic floor. \textbf{Caveat --- this
  multi-region active-active topology and the \texttt{2×\ cost} here are
  UNPRICED / NOT VALIDATED in this book's TCO/fleet arithmetic (Ch
  16--17), which prices a single-region N−1 deployment instead;
  multi-region active-active is a different provisioning problem
  (cross-region capacity duplication, region-failure tolerance, data
  locality and traffic routing, state/data replication) and must be
  costed separately before the 99.9\% SLA is met --- see the
  canonical-box availability-topology note.}
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Tokenizer exactness for technical domains}: While the ``one
  token ≈ 4 characters'' rule of thumb is convenient, enterprise Q\&A
  prompts may contain code, JSON, or domain‑specific terminology that
  tokenizes differently. The exact per‑token count for a given prompt
  distribution is \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}} and would require running the model's
  tokenizer on a representative sample of real user queries.
\item
  \textbf{KV cache scaling with longer contexts}: The canonical scenario
  uses \textasciitilde9,200 input tokens. If retrieval quality degrades
  and context lengths grow to 32 K or 128 K, the KV cache memory per
  request grows linearly. \textbf{Be precise about what breaks.} A
  single 32 K canonical request costs \textasciitilde84 GB of KV, which
  --- with the \textasciitilde140 GB weights and \textasciitilde64 GB
  runtime/workspace \mbox{{[}canonical scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} (the
  runtime/NCCL/activation reserve assumed in Ch 7, which subtracts it to
  reach the \textasciitilde436 GB KV budget) --- still fits the nominal
  640 GB aggregate host \emph{as a single request} (\textasciitilde288
  GB total, well under 640 GB). What fails is not one-request fit but a
  particular \textbf{concurrency/SLO requirement}: at the canonical
  \textasciitilde40 rps peak and \textasciitilde8.6 s service time, the
  in-flight occupancy (\textasciitilde344 requests) demands far more
  than one host's aggregate KV budget once per-request KV grows to 32 K
  or 128 K (at 128 K a single request alone needs \textasciitilde335 GB
  of KV, leaving little for any concurrency). So state the exact
  breakpoint in terms of the \textbf{maximum concurrent requests and the
  per-rank/aggregate feasibility}, not as ``a 32 K or 128 K context no
  longer fits.'' The specific breakpoint is workload‑ and SLO‑dependent
  and must be checked per‑rank (Chapters 17, 20), not asserted as a
  universal constant.
\item
  \textbf{Impact of reranking on latency}: The TTFT budget assumes
  retrieval (\textasciitilde120 ms) plus prefill. If a late‑stage
  reranker is added to the pipeline, the retrieval component's latency
  may increase, eating into the TTFT budget and potentially requiring a
  wider SLO margin or a faster retriever. The magnitude of this effect
  is \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}} and should be measured before production
  deployment.
\item
  \textbf{Economic durability at scale}: The tokens‑per‑dollar numbers
  are derived from 2026 on‑demand H100 pricing. Spot instances, reserved
  contracts, or custom cloud agreements could shift the economics
  significantly. The durability of the economic model across provider
  changes is \mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}}.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: A Six-Dimension Workload
Characterization}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-a-six-dimension-workload-characterization}

An architect is brought into the early design of an internal Q\&A
platform. The stakeholder says: ``We have thousands of employees who
want to ask questions over our internal documents. We need it to be
accurate and fast, but we don't know how many thousands or how fast is
fast enough.'' Before any architecture can be defended, the architect
does the workload characterization that this chapter walks through.

\textbf{Label note for this mini-case.} This 5,000-employee growth
variant is non-canonical. Its own inputs (5,000 employees, 10\% peak
activity, 50 rps) carry \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}. Every host
count derived from them (26 / 38 / 39 / 40) carries
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}. The \textasciitilde8.6 s engine
response duration is not a variant input: the variant inherits it
unchanged from the canonical scenario (\(W_{\mathrm{exec}}\) = prefill
1.08 s + decode 7.5 s ≈ 8.58 s, \hyperref[tab:4.2]{Table 4-2}, \mbox{{[}canonical
scenario{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}), and it keeps that label here. The
canonical-scenario tags in §4.1--§4.7 do not transfer to the rest of the
variant.

Using the canonical scenario as a starting point ---
\textasciitilde2,000 registered users, \textasciitilde5\% concurrency,
\textasciitilde10 rps, \textasciitilde9,200 input + 300 output tokens
--- the architect projects the enterprise's actual headcount. If the
company has 5,000 employees and expects 10\% concurrent activity during
peak Q\&A periods (after a policy rollout), that gives \textbf{500
active people} --- a \emph{population} measure, \textbf{not} 500
requests currently occupying the inference service. An arrival rate
requires its own traffic assumption or observation. Suppose user
research and demand modeling independently set the busy-period peak to
\textbf{50 requests/s} (a declared scenario input at this growth scale,
not a figure derived from the 500-user count --- Little's Law cannot
convert an active-user population into an arrival rate without knowing
in-flight request occupancy). With the illustrative \textasciitilde8.6 s
engine response duration, Little's Law implies approximately 50 × 8.6 ≈
\textbf{430 requests in the system} across the fleet at that rate ---
distinct from the 500 active people. It is the \emph{arrival rate}, not
the user count, that sizes the fleet. Using the canonical per-host bound
from § 7.3 (the whole-slot floor of 17 KV-resident requests) and Ch 20
(17 slots ÷ 8.6 s = 1.977 rps --- a single 8×H100 host serves
\textasciitilde2.0 rps at full modeled utilization --- C ≈ 17.5 (= 436
GB ÷ 24.9 GB, the 9.5K-token resident basis of §4.6) is the analytical
quotient, floored to 17 deployable whole-context slots per the book's
whole-slot convention; applied in Ch 17), the fleet rounds up by the
same whole-slot ceiling the canonical 40 rps case uses (⌈344/17⌉ = 21):

\[
n_\text{hosts} = \left\lceil \frac{50 \text{ rps} \times 8.6 \text{ s}}{17 \text{ slots}} \right\rceil = \lceil 430/17 \rceil = \lceil 25.29 \rceil = 26 \text{ hosts} \quad (\text{full utilization})
\]

and on the u-only screen at a 70\% target utilization the
\textbf{normative host-count form} of Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1} gives ⌈26 ÷ 0.70⌉
= ⌈37.14⌉ = \textbf{38 hosts} (the occupancy cross-check ⌈430/(17 ×
0.7)⌉ = ⌈430/11.9⌉ = ⌈36.13⌉ = 37 sits one host below it) --- then
\textbf{39 hosts} before the spare, \textbf{40 under the book's
provisioning policy}, which multiplies the independent ρ = 0.95 as well
and adds the N−1 reserve (⌈430/(17 × 0.70 × 0.95)⌉ = ⌈430/11.305⌉ =
⌈38.04⌉ = 39; Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1} adds +1 for the N−1 spare → 40).
\textbf{The same total by the host-count form:} with N full-utilization
hosts, total (provisioned) = ⌈N ÷ (u × ρ)⌉ = ⌈26 ÷ 0.665⌉ and working =
total − 1 --- here N = ⌈430/17⌉ = 26, so ⌈26 ÷ 0.665⌉ = ⌈39.10⌉ = 40
provisioned, 39 working. \textbf{Ceiling placement and the normative
form.} The single normative rule is the host-count form (stated once in
Ch 17 \hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}): ceil the load to whole full-utilization hosts first
(N = ⌈load ÷ C⌉), then total provisioned = ⌈N ÷ (u × ρ)⌉ and working =
total − 1. The occupancy form --- ⌈load ÷ (C × u × ρ)⌉ --- is a
\textbf{cross-check}, not the normative rule, and it may differ by one
host because it divides the un-ceiled load before the ceiling. Where the
screens disagree, the fleet is the \textbf{maximum of all screens} ---
KV-residency/service-time, prefill-throughput, and policy fleet: the
binding constraint wins --- and the text states which form produced the
quoted number. Here the cross-check differs from the normative form by
one host at every stage --- 37 against 38 at the u-only screen, and 39
against the 40 provisioned total at policy (39 is the working count; the
normative form adds the N−1 spare). The normative form is the one
quoted, 40 provisioned / 39 working; the cross-check is a bound, not a
reproduction of the provisioned total. The one-host delta is the
expected rounding artifact of dividing the un-ceiled load before the
ceiling, not a disagreement about policy. A prefill-throughput
cross-check from the \hyperref[chap:8]{Chapter 8} model bounds it from below: 50 rps ×
9,200 input = 460,000 prefill tokens/s ÷ \textasciitilde19,800 tokens/s
per host (the host's \emph{sustained-planning} prefill rate, 8 ×
\textasciitilde2,472 tok/s at 35\% MFU --- 8 × 0.989 PFLOPS × 0.35 ÷ 140
GFLOP/token = 19,780 tok/s per host, i.e.~2,472.5 per GPU, which Ch 8
\hyperref[tab:8.1]{Table 8-1} prints as \textasciitilde2,472 --- a planning rate, not a
ceiling: the peak-derived ceiling is \textasciitilde56.5K tok/s; not a
measured goodput) = \textbf{24 hosts} (460,000 ÷ 19,800 = 23.23, ceiled
to a whole host). (Do not divide by the \textasciitilde8.5K tok/s
single-request prefill rate, 9,200 ÷ 1.08 s --- that is the rate for one
request monopolizing a host, not a per-host aggregate, and using it here
would be a unit error.) So prefill-throughput is a \emph{lower} bound on
host count; the binding figure is the KV-residency/service-time
\textasciitilde26 (full util) / \textasciitilde38 (u-only 70\%,
normative host-count form) / \textasciitilde39 before the spare, 40
under the 70\% × 95\% provisioning policy (\hyperref[tab:17.1]{Table 17-1}, +1 N−1 spare)
result. The two do not ``agree'' --- the KV-bound dominates, and the
prefill model shows why adding hosts for prefill alone is the wrong
lever at this scale; both are \emph{analytical} bounds that must be
confirmed by benchmark goodput (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}). Note this mini-case is a
5,000-employee growth scenario at 50 rps, distinct from the canonical 40
rps peak (which sizes to \textasciitilde21 hosts full-util /
\textasciitilde31 working at the 70\% × 95\% policy (32 with N−1),
Chapters 8/16/17/20/25); the two are separate cases, not a
contradiction.

The specific sizing --- turning ``thousands of employees'' into a
characterized workload across all six dimensions --- is the payoff of
Ch. 4's framework. Each dimension carries an arithmetic (quality →
accuracy target and its validation; traffic → offered load and
occupancy; token profile → KV and prefill demand; latency → service time
and SLO; economics → \$/token and \$/request; operational → availability
topology, data locality, refresh cadence) that the later chapters plug
specific numbers into. Until a workload has \emph{numbers} on all six
axes, any architecture chosen against it is being chosen against a
category, not a workload.
