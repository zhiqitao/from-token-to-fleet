\section{The Signal in One Paragraph}\label{the-signal-in-one-paragraph}

A striking number of 2026 frontier models --- four open models we
surveyed --- converge on a \textbf{second-order architectural pattern},
which we read as an interpretation rather than an established historical
fact. We should be precise about \emph{what kind} of gain each lever
delivers. The common error is to read architecture innovation as raw
capability. In our interpretation, it is mostly \textbf{efficiency}
(more context, less cost per token) that comes out of the attention and
MoE changes below, not intelligence. The capability story --- reasoning,
post-training, test-time compute --- lives elsewhere (A.8), and carries
at least as much of the frontier's real progress. We separate the two
explicitly.

\emph{Definition of ``active'' (what counts in the activated-parameter
numerator, and what it does \textbf{not} mean).} Here ``active''
(equivalently ``activated'') means the parameters that participate in
the forward pass for a given token: the routed experts dispatched for
that token, the shared/always-on experts, and the attention and
embedding/head parameters that run for every token. It is \textbf{not}
the same as any of these: (a) \textbf{resident} parameters --- the full
expert + attention + embedding fabric that must be held in memory (or
sharded/offloaded), which is the total-parameter count, not the active
count; (b) per-token \textbf{FLOPs}, since activation count is not a
proxy for compute cost under softmax-routing and layered dispatch; (c)
KV or attention-state \textbf{sparsity}, which is a different quantity
(see the GQA/MLA discussion in Ch 7); and (d) \textbf{achievable serving
speed} --- a low active fraction does \textbf{not} translate
proportionally into a faster served rate, because total-weight
residency, expert routing/traffic, and attention cost still dominate.
The four families use different expert counts, dispatch schemes, and
embedding splits (Qwen's N-gram embedding table is counted in total but
is offloadable), so the active fractions below are comparable only
\emph{after} this definition is fixed; the numbers are not a drop-in
cross-model ranking of serving cost. (Here ``active'' contrasts total vs
activated parameters --- see the model/MoE discussion in Ch 3 and the
GQA/MHA KV discussion in Ch 7.) \mbox{{[}1P{]}}

\emph{Frontier MoE sparsity in 2026. Kimi K3 is the extreme case: 2.8 T
total / 104 B active (\textasciitilde3.7\% active); DeepSeek-V4-Flash
284 B/13 B (\textasciitilde4.6\%); GLM-5.3-Flash 320 B/18 B
(\textasciitilde5.6\%); Qwen3.8-Flash-Next 125 B/6 B
(\textasciitilde4.8\% --- \emph{backbone-only ratio}: the denominator is
the 125 B backbone; the separate 51 B n-gram embedding and 4 B MTP
modules are excluded from both numerator and denominator, so this is a
\emph{backbone} fraction, not a fraction of the complete deployed
parameter inventory). Active-parameter fraction in each of these four
frontier MoE families is in the single digits --- the anchor for the
Ch10 expert-parallelism serving calculus. \mbox{{[}1P{]}} arXiv 2606.19348;
arXiv 2607.24653; HF Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next; HF
zai-org/GLM-5.3-Flash{]}}

Three \textbf{efficiency levers} every 2026 frontier family pulled:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Hybrid attention as the new KV lever --- an efficiency lever,
  not (by itself) a capability lever.} Every frontier model
  re-engineered attention to shrink long-context cost at the mechanism
  level --- compressed/sparse hybrids (DeepSeek CSA+HCA), delta+sparse
  (Qwen Gated DeltaNet + QSA), delta/residual (Kimi KDA + AttnRes),
  sparse+linear (GLM). KV cache is no longer a fixed per-token constant
  we only quantize; it is a design surface the model vendor already
  optimized. The intended benefit is to preserve useful model capability
  while reducing long-context inference cost and/or extending practical
  context length --- indispensable, but it does not by itself make the
  model smarter. Capability gains come from the post-training/reasoning
  stack in A.8.
\item
  \textbf{MoE sparsity went extreme --- the capacity-per-FLOP lever.}
  Active-parameter fractions fell to single digits (Kimi 104B of 2.8T;
  Qwen 6B of 125B). In our reading, one could argue MoE is a
  \emph{larger} architectural innovation than attention: it lets a lab
  grow knowledge/capacity without proportionally growing FLOPs per
  token. That is why the 2026 open landscape is heavily MoE-oriented.
  Expert parallelism and fused cross-expert communication moved from a
  footnote to a serving-critical capability.
\item
  \textbf{Efficiency is a first-class result, not an afterthought.}
  Training-stable optimizers (Muon) and post-training RL (GRPO, async
  RL) are now reported as core architecture, not tooling.
\end{enumerate}

These three form the \emph{cost/context} ledger of the frontier. The
\emph{capability} ledger --- reasoning, post-training, test-time
compute, agent harness --- is where most of the qualitative improvement
actually lives (A.8). Distinguishing efficiency levers from capability
levers is the architect's first move when reading any new model card.

\section{How to read this appendix: Observed, Interpretation,
Hypothesis}\label{how-to-read-this-appendix-observed-interpretation-hypothesis}

This appendix surveys vendor-reported material, so we are careful to
separate three layers of statement that are easy to blur --- especially
in a fast-moving field full of first/``most-efficient''/3T-class claims:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Observed} --- what the vendor's technical report or model card
  actually states (e.g., ``DeepSeek-V4 reports a reduced KV
  footprint''). This is \mbox{{[}1P{]}} \emph{fact about the claim}, not the
  same as the third layer.
\item
  \textbf{Interpretation} --- our read of what architectural mechanism
  the report demonstrates (e.g., ``the KV reduction suggests the model
  changes how attention stores state''). This is analysis, ours, and
  could be wrong.
\item
  \textbf{Hypothesis} --- a prediction about consequence we draw for
  serving architecture (e.g., ``we expect long-context serving to become
  more memory-comfortable at equal context''). This is a testable (a
  hypothesis), not a fact.
\end{itemize}

When we write ``a number of frontier models converge'' or ``one could
argue MoE is a larger architectural innovation,'' read those as
\textbf{Interpretation / Hypothesis} --- defensible reads of the
frontier signal, not independent measurements. Vendor numbers stay
tagged \mbox{{[}1P{]}} and flagged verify-on-own-hardware; our reads sit one
register lower and should be read with the same skepticism we ask of any
vendor claim. The practical rule: \emph{if it's a percentage from a
report, it's Observed \mbox{{[}1P{]}}; if it's a claim about what the industry
trend means, it's ours --- treat it as analysis, tune it against the
reader's real workload.}

\textbf{Mapping onto the book's evidence taxonomy.} These three
reader-friendly layers are an on-ramp to, not a replacement for, the
three-axis taxonomy used throughout the book (provenance × epistemic
status × measurement status): Axis 1 provenance
\mbox{{[}1P{]}}/\mbox{{[}2°{]}}/\mbox{{[}LAB{]}}/\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} × Axis 2 status
\mbox{{[}FACT{]}}/\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}/\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}}/\mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}/\mbox{{[}UNRESOLVED{]}},
with Axis 3 riding along.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Observed} ↔ \mbox{{[}1P{]}} · \mbox{{[}FACT{]}} --- the vendor's stated
  claim, a fact \emph{about the claim}.
\item
  \textbf{Interpretation} ↔ \mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}} --- the book's own analysis, a
  derivation from the evidence. (Where the book's taxonomy would label
  it \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}, note that; the appendix uses ``interpretation''
  only as plain prose for a derived read, never a separate sanctioned
  status.)
\item
  \textbf{Hypothesis} ↔ \mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}} --- a testable prediction,
  exactly the taxonomy's \mbox{{[}HYPOTHESIS{]}}.
\item
  \textbf{Measurement status} ↔ Axis 3 --- ANALYTICAL / ILLUSTRATIVE /
  MODELED / MEASURED (or \mbox{{[}UNMEASURED{]}} where the stage is not yet
  determined): how far the number has climbed the measurement-status
  hierarchy, defined in the Sources, Method, and Reproducibility
  chapter.
\end{itemize}

The appendix's labels are the human-friendly framing; the three-axis
labels are the precise book-wide convention. They should never conflict.

\textbf{This is a dated field guide, not an evergreen ranking.} The

separately dated September and October observations outside the

\section{DeepSeek-V4 --- Making Long Context Cheap by Re-Architecting

\textbf{Decisive fact \mbox{{[}1P{]}} --- arXiv 2606.19348; HF
deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro:} V4-Pro is 1.6 T total / 49 B activated,
V4-Flash 284 B / 13 B activated, both 1M-token context, MIT-licensed.
Its hybrid attention combines Compressed Sparse Attention (CSA) and
Heavily Compressed Attention (HCA). At 1M context, DeepSeek reports
\textbf{\textasciitilde27\% (Pro) / \textasciitilde10\% (Flash) of
V3.2's single-token inference FLOPs and \textasciitilde10\% /
\textasciitilde7\% of its KV cache}.

\textbf{What this means for the architect (ties to Ch7 Memory, Ch8

confirmation that \emph{KV-per-token is a moving target}. \hyperref[chap:7]{Chapter 7}'s

DeepSeek shows the \emph{constant factor itself} can be cut
\textasciitilde10× without changing the model's parameter count. The
architectural decision shifts from ``how much KV memory does this
context need?'' to ``which KV scheme does this model use, and is its
vendor-published saving reproducible on my serving stack?''

\section{Kimi K3 --- Vendor-Reported First Open 3T-Class Model, and the
MoE-Sparsity
Extreme}\label{kimi-k3-vendor-reported-first-open-3t-class-model-and-the-moe-sparsity-extreme}

\textbf{Decisive fact \mbox{{[}1P{]}} --- arXiv 2607.24653; HF
moonshotai/Kimi-K3:} 2.8 T total / 104 B activated, native vision, 1M
context. Built on \textbf{Kimi Delta Attention (KDA)} +
\textbf{Attention Residuals (AttnRes)}, with \textbf{Stable LatentMoE}
activating just \textbf{16 of 896 routed experts} per token. Claims
\textasciitilde{}\textbf{2.5× scaling efficiency} over Kimi K2. Weights
are QAT-trained from SFT onward with \textbf{MXFP4 weights / MXFP8
activations}. Full weights are released --- per the vendor, the first
open 3T-class model.

\textbf{What this means for the architect (ties to Ch3 Model, Ch10
Parallelism).} A model whose \emph{total} size is 2.8 T but whose
\emph{activated} footprint is 104 B is a clear illustration of Ch3's
total-vs-activated distinction and Ch10's expert-parallelism doctrine.
Serving Kimi K3 requires accommodating the \textbf{2.8 T total parameter
fabric} across the serving system (the full expert set must reside
somewhere unless an explicit offload/load-on-demand architecture is
used) while only \textasciitilde104 B parameters participate in the
computation of a given token. The architectural problem is therefore
simultaneously one of \textbf{total weight residency/distribution} and
\textbf{active-weight communication/computation} --- sharding and
scheduling the expert fabric, plus the KV for our context. The decision
surface is expert-parallel communication and fused cross-expert kernels,
not raw capacity.

\section{Where 2026 Capability Actually Comes From (beyond
Architecture)}\label{where-2026-capability-actually-comes-from-beyond-architecture}

A recurring error --- easy to fall into after reading four new model
cards --- is to attribute frontier gains to the attention mechanism
splashed across the front page. The industry is converging not just on
\emph{architectures} (MoE + efficient attention + very long context) but
on a \emph{multi-layered recipe} in which architecture is one

\subsubsection*{Three independent
dimensions}\label{three-independent-dimensions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{Three independent dimensions}

A model description now needs at least three independent dimensions:
parameter allocation, history-state representation, and execution depth.
Dense feed-forward layers can coexist with recurrent token mixing.
Sparse experts can coexist with global attention. Shared weights can
execute repeatedly within one token prediction. None of these properties
determines the others.

\texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B} is a useful counterexample to the idea that a
dense model must use conventional full attention throughout. Its card
describes dense feed-forward layers and native image/video
understanding. Its language configuration contains 48 Gated DeltaNet
layers and 16 full-attention layers. DeltaNet updates a recurrent
history state; the full-attention layers retain a separate
history-dependent KV component. Dense parameter allocation therefore
does not imply a full-MHA cache formula.\footnote{Qwen, Qwen3.8-27B
  model card, ``Model Overview,'' ``Benchmark Results,'' and
  long-context guidance:
  \url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B/raw/main/README.md} . Released
  configuration:
  \url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B/raw/main/config.json} . Public
  implementation:
  \url{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/huggingface/transformers/main/src/transformers/models/qwen3_5/modeling_qwen3_5.py}
  .}

A different form of recurrence repeats a shared core over the current
representation. Huginn and Ouro provide public research examples. Their
shared weights reduce the unique parameter inventory relative to an
equally deep untied network. The repeated executions still consume
compute. Attention states can also differ between iterations, even when
the weights are identical. These research artifacts are not evidence
that adaptive-depth production serving is already
routine.\footnote{Geiping et al., ``Scaling up Test-Time Compute with
  Latent Reasoning: A Recurrent Depth Approach,'' arXiv:2502.05171, v2,
  17 February 2025: \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171} . Huginn checkpoint
  card, ``KV-cache Details'' and ``Per-Token Adaptive Compute'':
  \url{https://huggingface.co/tomg-group-umd/huginn-0125/raw/main/README.md} .}\textsuperscript{,}\footnote{Zhu
  et al., ``Scaling Latent Reasoning via Looped Language Models,''
  arXiv:2510.25741v6, revised 11 October 2026, especially §5.4.2 and
  Table 14: \url{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25741v6} . Revision history:

\subsubsection*{Phase and storage tier
matter}\label{phase-and-storage-tier-matter}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{Phase and storage tier matter}

\texttt{deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash} changes more than one constant.
Its vendor report describes a Causal Encoder-Decoder with 552B backbone
parameters and a separate 196B Engram component. It reports 8B activated
parameters per token during prefill and 16B during decode. A single
active-parameter figure would obscure this phase difference. These
counts describe the vendor's computation paths; they do not establish an
elapsed-time ratio.\footnote{DeepSeek-AI, ``DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash: Pushing
  the Limits of KV Cache Compression,'' Abstract and §§2.1--2.4, 3.2:
  \url{https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash/resolve/main/DeepSeek_V41_Tech_Report.pdf}
  . Model card:
  \url{https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash/raw/main/README.md}
  . The numerical memory and activation claims are vendor-reported, not
  independently reproduced.}

The report also separates memory objects. Cross-layer reuse and FP4
main-KV storage produce a reported global HBM KV footprint of 890 bytes
per token. This is not total request memory. Bounded sliding-window
state, weights, workspaces, and allocation overhead remain separate. The
reported persistent-cache reduction to approximately one-eighth of
DeepSeek-V4-Flash concerns host or SSD storage under SWA Bounded Replay.
It is not the same metric as global HBM KV.

The transferable rule is to account for each independently stored state,
its update rule, and its location. Sparse attention that reads selected
history entries need not store only those entries. Cross-layer consumers
can share one stored object. A local window can bound live state while
prefix restoration still incurs storage or recomputation. Capacity
across several accelerators also does not establish that each rank fits
its assigned weights and state.

\subsubsection*{A stopping rule is not yet a runtime
saving}\label{a-stopping-rule-is-not-yet-a-runtime-saving}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsubsection}{A stopping rule is not yet a
runtime saving}

Huginn's model card requires a recurrence-aware cache so that later core
passes do not overwrite earlier KV entries. It also labels its per-token
adaptive-compute facility a zero-shot proof of concept. Its stopping
criteria include changes in latent state, output distributions, and
prediction stability.\footnote{Geiping et al., ``Scaling up Test-Time
  Compute with Latent Reasoning: A Recurrent Depth Approach,''
  arXiv:2502.05171, v2, 17 February 2025:
  \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05171} . Huginn checkpoint card, ``KV-cache
  Details'' and ``Per-Token Adaptive Compute'':
  \url{https://huggingface.co/tomg-group-umd/huginn-0125/raw/main/README.md} .}

Ouro's revised paper studies learned exit decisions and cache sharing.
It reports that prefill needs distinct iteration caches in the evaluated
setting, while selected decode-only sharing strategies can reduce the
cache component. These are phase-specific experiments, not a universal
reduction in whole-request memory.\footnote{Zhu et al., ``Scaling Latent
  Reasoning via Looped Language Models,'' arXiv:2510.25741v6, revised 11
  October 2026, especially §5.4.2 and Table 14:
  \url{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.25741v6} . Revision history:
  \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.25741} .}

The released runtime path also matters. In the Ouro implementation
retrieved for this update, the core loop computes all configured
iterations before the language-model wrapper selects an exit
representation. That path does not demonstrate skipped core computation
merely because an earlier representation is selected. The model card
separately states that its vLLM path does not support adaptive exit. A
paper's stopping policy, a reference implementation, and an optimized
serving engine are different evidence
objects.\footnote{ByteDance/Ouro-2.6B, released
  \texttt{modeling\_ouro.py}, \texttt{OuroModel.forward} and exit-logit
  selection in \texttt{OuroForCausalLM.forward}, fetched revision
  archived in source-index.json:
  \url{https://huggingface.co/ByteDance/Ouro-2.6B/raw/main/modeling_ouro.py}
  . This is a static code observation, not an executed performance
  measurement.}\textsuperscript{,}\footnote{ByteDance/Ouro-2.6B model
  card, adaptive-exit and vLLM notes:
  \url{https://huggingface.co/ByteDance/Ouro-2.6B/raw/main/README.md} .
  Card/config layer-count differences remain unresolved:
  \url{https://huggingface.co/ByteDance/Ouro-2.6B/raw/main/config.json} .}

A deployment evaluation would therefore record actual core executions,
state bytes, and per-stage time alongside quality. Variable-depth
scheduling could change batch efficiency and latency tails. That
consequence remains a testable hypothesis until measured on the intended
engine and workload.

release selects the native path. \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}FACT{]}}

mixture of tensor encodings and can promote selected tensors to Q6\_K.

imatrix requirements are type-specific. The complete model's average
precision therefore follows a tensor inventory, not one block's byte
rate. GGML's \texttt{block\_nvfp4} also has a specific scale layout; the
name alone does not establish interoperability with NVIDIA's

Post-training calibration and training-based recovery also describe
different work. PTQ estimates quantization parameters without a full
model-training run; the particular algorithm can search scales or
compensate weight errors. QAT updates model parameters while accounting
for quantization effects. QAD additionally uses teacher targets to
recover behavior. That recovery consumes training compute and memory,
and a favorable fake-quant evaluation still needs confirmation after
export through the real serving kernels. Calibration, recovery and
inference therefore receive separate resource ledgers and separate
quality records. \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

recipe choices, not properties supplied by the four-bit value code.

state and temporary buffers. The unique scale information of a 2D block
is therefore not a prediction of resident training memory, nor a reason
to reuse an inference footprint as the training budget.
