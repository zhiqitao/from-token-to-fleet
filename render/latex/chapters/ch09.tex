\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

Before we can place a model across multiple GPUs or across a cluster, we
need to know what the interconnect can --- and cannot --- move. This
chapter asks: given a model size, a parallelism strategy, and a traffic
pattern (all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter), what is the
communication time, and does it dominate the compute budget? We will
size the hidden bottleneck that often goes unnoticed until the job
stalls.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


\emph{Implementation citations in this chapter were inspected on 28
October 2026 for the separately dated addendum, outside the 30 August
snapshot. They identify documented or source-inspected paths, not a
tested combined environment or a refresh of the August hardware
comparisons.}

Communication in a distributed LLM system follows the deployed topology,
not a universal vendor hierarchy. A server may connect its accelerators
through PCIe, a dedicated scale-up fabric, integrated Ethernet links, or
a combination of paths; NVLink and NVSwitch are NVIDIA's examples of
such a path, while Gaudi organizes a different integrated-network
topology
(\href{https://cdrdv2-public.intel.com/817486/gaudi-3-ai-accelerator-white-paper.pdf}{Gaudi
3 white paper}, retrieved 2026-10-28). Across hosts, Ethernet/RoCE or
InfiniBand may carry the traffic, subject to the actual adapter, routing
and transport support. Each layer still has a distinct bandwidth
ceiling, a distinct latency profile, and a distinct cost per gigabyte,
so the architect must match the collective operation to the layer where
the bandwidth is sufficient: moving the same data over a slower layer
adds minutes to training time or inference latency for no computational
benefit. The relevant quantities are directional bandwidth, latency,
contention, and the physical traffic of the chosen collective --- a
product name alone supplies none of those measurements for a deployment.

A collective operation describes how data from a group of ranks becomes
a result; it does not specify a network, an algorithm, or a hardware
vendor. A rank is an index within a communication group, not a synonym
for a server --- one server can host several ranks, and several
independent groups can occupy the same devices. The communicator
identifies the participants and the context in which their operations
match
(\href{https://www.mpi-forum.org/docs/mpi-5.0/mpi50-report.pdf}{MPI 5.0
standard};
\href{https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/user-guide/docs/api/comms.html}{NCCL
communicator API}, retrieved 2026-10-28).

The collective operations that dominate multi-node training and
inference are:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{all-reduce}: each rank contributes an equal-shaped tensor; a
  reduction such as the elementwise sum is returned to every rank.
  Averaging additionally requires division by the rank count; the
  collective does not imply that division. Data-parallel training
  commonly uses gradient reductions, while tensor-parallel inference
  uses activation reductions.
\item
  \textbf{all-gather}: every GPU contributes a tensor; every GPU
  receives the concatenation of all fragments. Used when a model split
  across nodes needs the full weight or KV cache locally.
\item
  \textbf{reduce-scatter}: ranks contribute equal-shaped tensors, reduce
  corresponding elements, and receive distinct partitions of the reduced
  result. Each output partition has 1/N of the input tensor's elements.
  Unlike all-gather, this operation also performs a reduction; it is not
  its mathematical inverse.
\end{itemize}

The bandwidth each collective consumes depends on three factors: the
data volume (bytes), the number of participating nodes (N), and the
interconnect's effective bandwidth in the presence of contention. A 70B
FP16 model is 140 GB, and its FP16 gradient is the same size --- the
§9.3 worked example all-reduces that 140 GB gradient tensor. The 140 GB
is a logical input size, not the physical traffic of the collective. For
an eight-rank point-to-point ring, each rank sends 245 GB and receives
another 245 GB (§9.3). A one-way transfer of the logical 140 GB alone
would take 56 s at an assumed effective 2.5 GB/s; the ring's send volume
instead gives 98 s. The bandwidth denominator must use the same
direction convention as the counted bytes.

Behind these three workhorses sits one contract. For a fixed-count
all-reduce, each rank contributes an equal-length typed buffer;
corresponding elements are reduced, and every rank receives the result.
SUM returns a sum --- averaging requires division by the group size or
an explicitly selected averaging operation, at any group size, not an
implicit property of the operation. The reduction operation, dtype,
element count, communicator, and operation order form a single contract,
and matching buffer byte counts alone are insufficient
(\href{https://www.mpi-forum.org/docs/mpi-5.0/mpi50-report.pdf}{MPI 5.0

\ntsection{Mental Model}\label{mental-model}


The concentric-ring model represents increasing communication distance
in the actual topology, not a mandatory sequence of fabrics. An
accelerator accesses its local HBM first. Inside a server, PCIe or a
dedicated scale-up fabric connects devices; NVLink and NVSwitch are
examples for the NVIDIA configuration in \hyperref[tab:9.1]{Table 9-1}, not universal server
components. Across servers and racks, Ethernet/RoCE or InfiniBand can
carry traffic according to the deployed links, adapters, and routing. A
useful placement keeps frequent exchanges within a measured low-latency
group where possible and budgets any traffic that crosses its boundary.
Physical distance alone does not establish a bandwidth ranking: rail
count, topology, contention, and transport support remain part of the
measurement.

Directionality comes before division. H100's advertised NVLink bandwidth
is 900 GB/s bidirectional per GPU, or 450 GB/s per direction (NVIDIA
H100 datasheet). This per-GPU port aggregate is not a measurement of a
particular topology's bisection. A one-way 140 GB transfer at the
directional peak takes about 0.31 s \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}. A collective
also incurs reduction, synchronization and routing work:

\[
T_{\text{collective}} \approx \alpha \times n_\text{steps} + \frac{\text{bytes}}{\beta}
\]

where \(\alpha\) is the per-step latency, \(\beta\) is the achieved
bandwidth under an explicit directional convention, and
\(n_\text{steps}\) is the number of communication steps. The byte term
must count that algorithm's physical traffic rather than substitute the
logical tensor size. This expression is a simplified model; overlap,
reduction work and collective offload can change the critical path. For
a flat one-way 140 GB transfer, the nominal 25 GB/s of one HDR port
gives a best-case 5.6 s; an assumed practical-effective 2.5 GB/s
Ethernet path gives 56 s. The eight-rank ring in §9.3 instead sends 245
GB per rank: about 0.54 s at the H100 directional peak, 6.1 s at an
assumed effective two-rail 40 GB/s, and 98 s at 2.5 GB/s. These are
analytical comparisons, not measured NCCL results; deployment choices
require §9.4 measurements.

The reduce-scatter and all-gather rows of \hyperref[tab:9.2]{Table 9-2} become concrete in
\hyperref[fig:9.2]{Fig 9.2}, which follows one SUM through a balanced four-rank ring.
Reduce-scatter leaves a different reduced fragment at each rank, and
all-gather then replicates those already-reduced fragments --- gathering
the original inputs instead would not produce the same result. The two
phases are also the traffic §9.3 counts: each phase sends \((N-1)/N\) of
the tensor per rank, which is where 122.5 GB per phase, and 245 GB per
rank, comes from at eight ranks and 140 GB
(\href{https://www.mpi-forum.org/docs/mpi-5.0/mpi50-report.pdf}{MPI 5.0
standard};
\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/nccl-tests/afd59abab774a6a4b492eb344bd0c845a0b7bb4d/doc/PERFORMANCE.md}{nccl-tests
performance definition, pinned}, retrieved 2026-10-28).

Floating-point SUM adds another constraint. Its mathematical expression
is unchanged when an implementation changes the reduction tree, but
finite-precision addition can produce different bits, and the MPI
standard explicitly recognizes this effect
(\href{https://www.mpi-forum.org/docs/mpi-5.0/mpi50-report.pdf}{MPI 5.0
standard}). A reproducibility claim therefore needs a scope: inputs,
dtype, rank count and mapping, algorithm, kernels, and hardware/software
versions. A fixed random seed does not by itself establish bitwise
collective reproducibility.

The software path above those physical rings has several distinct
decisions, and \hyperref[fig:9.3]{Fig 9.3} separates them from the setup path. The framework
chooses a communication group and operation; its backend connects that
request to a collective implementation; the implementation chooses an
algorithm and uses device execution and transport facilities; those
facilities share HBM, device links, PCIe, NICs, and switches --- the
layers of \hyperref[tab:9.1]{Table 9-1}. Changing a transport can leave the collective
semantics unchanged while changing which resource limits the operation
(\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/nccl/v2.32.3-1/src/nccl.h.in}{NCCL
API header, pinned to v2.32.3-1};
\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/nccl/v2.32.3-1/src/transport.cc}{NCCL
transport selection, pinned};
\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/uxlfoundation/oneCCL/2022.1.0/deps/libccl/src/atl/mpi/atl_mpi.cpp}{oneCCL
MPI transport, pinned to 2022.1.0};
\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/uxlfoundation/oneCCL/2022.1.0/deps/libccl/src/atl/ofi/atl_ofi.cpp}{oneCCL
OFI transport, pinned}, retrieved 2026-10-28).

MPI is a standard, not one library and not a CPU-only transport: Open
MPI, MPICH, and Intel MPI are implementations, and GPU-aware
implementations can accept supported device buffers, so eligible
configurations can move their payload without staging it through host
memory. Device-pointer recognition, direct RDMA, operation support, and
stream integration are separate capabilities --- none follows
automatically from the phrase ``GPU-aware.'' The fair comparison is
specific: Open MPI's v5.0.x CUDA documentation supports device buffers
for several blocking collectives yet explicitly restricts nonblocking
reduction collectives on the documented CUDA-aware UCX path
(\href{https://docs.open-mpi.org/en/v5.0.x/tuning-apps/networking/cuda.html}{Open
MPI CUDA-aware support}), while MPICH v5.0.2 documents CUDA, HIP, and
Level Zero build support --- a build statement, not a guarantee for
every datatype, provider, or persistent device-buffer operation
(\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pmodels/mpich/v5.0.2/README.md}{MPICH
v5.0.2 README}, retrieved 2026-10-28). A nonblocking MPI request allows
overlap; it does not guarantee independent background progress, and a
correct MPI program must tolerate weak progress
(\href{https://www.mpi-forum.org/docs/mpi-5.0/mpi50-report.pdf}{MPI 5.0
standard}).

A typical NCCL call instead submits work to a CUDA stream: stream order
connects the collective to preceding producers and later consumers, a
consumer on another stream needs an explicit dependency, and network
paths can use CPU proxy threads, so GPU-oriented communication does not
imply zero host resource use
(\href{https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/user-guide/docs/usage/streams.html}{NCCL
stream semantics};
\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/nccl/v2.32.3-1/src/proxy.cc}{NCCL
proxy source, pinned to v2.32.3-1}). The submission-versus-completion
contract itself is measurement business, taken up in §9.4.

MPI and NCCL can coexist: NVIDIA's own example uses MPI to launch and
coordinate ranks and distribute the NCCL unique ID, then calls NCCL for
the tensor reduction
(\href{https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/user-guide/docs/examples.html}{NCCL
MPI example}). The presence of mpirun or MPI\_Bcast in an application
therefore does not identify the collective payload path; conversely, an
MPI implementation may itself carry GPU payload, and oneCCL's transport
layer includes MPI as well as OFI paths
(\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/uxlfoundation/oneCCL/2022.1.0/deps/libccl/src/atl/mpi/atl_mpi.cpp}{oneCCL
MPI transport, pinned};
\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/uxlfoundation/oneCCL/2022.1.0/deps/libccl/src/atl/ofi/atl_ofi.cpp}{oneCCL
OFI transport, pinned}). Bootstrap, collective algorithm, and transport
require separate labels.

A ring is one execution strategy, not the meaning of all-reduce. A
balanced point-to-point ring pipelines fragments through reduce-scatter
and all-gather --- the structure \hyperref[fig:9.2]{Fig 9.2} shows --- while trees can
reduce synchronization depth, hierarchical algorithms can keep much of
the traffic inside a fast local group, and pairwise exchanges serve a
different redistribution pattern in all-to-all
(\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/nccl/v2.32.3-1/src/device/all_reduce.h}{NCCL
ring implementation, pinned to v2.32.3-1};
\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/nccl/v2.32.3-1/src/tuning/cost_model.cc}{NCCL
cost model, pinned}). Channels add parallel communication paths, and
protocols such as NCCL's LL, LL128, and Simple govern lower-level
movement and synchronization
(\href{https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/user-guide/docs/env.html}{NCCL
environment variables}); a protocol name is not an algorithm or a
physical fabric.

The useful choice depends on message size and topology. For small
messages, launch and per-step latency can dominate --- the per-step
latency term of the model above; for large messages, sustained
directional bandwidth and load balance matter more --- its byte term.
Communication kernels may compete with model kernels for SMs, HBM, and
cache, while network progress consumes host and NIC resources, so more
overlap can reduce exposed waiting yet slow the computation being
overlapped. The architecture decision follows the combined critical
path, not a library name or an isolated bandwidth score.

\ntsection{Worked Example}\label{worked-example}


For a communication-only illustration, a 70B model has 140 GB of FP16
weights. We separately assume an FP16 gradient tensor of logical size
\(M=140\) GB at each rank. \hyperref[chap:10]{Chapter 10} instead assumes FP32 gradients, so
its logical tensor is 280 GB. Neither a 140 GB weight tensor nor this
gradient buffer fits on one 80 GB GPU. Even two 80 GB GPUs fitting the
weights would not establish training-state fit: gradients, optimizer
state, activations and workspace also require memory. A real deployment
must specify partitioning and chunking before applying this model. For
the arithmetic below, eight logical ranks perform a point-to-point ring
without collective offload. Each phase sends \((N-1)M/N=122.5\) GB per
rank; reduce-scatter followed by all-gather therefore sends 245 GB and
receives another 245 GB per rank. These are endpoint traffic counts, not
a claim that the full buffer can be allocated on an H100.
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

\textbf{Option A --- H100 NVLink directional-peak model.} The advertised
900 GB/s is the sum of both directions at each GPU endpoint. The
corresponding peak in one direction is 450 GB/s. We use that directional
peak to obtain an ideal ring time, not an achieved bandwidth or a
memory-feasible eight-replica training deployment. A flat one-way
transfer alone would take

\[
T_{\text{flat}} = \frac{140 \text{ GB}}{450 \text{ GB/s}} \approx 0.31 \text{ s}
\]

The ring sends 245 GB and receives another 245 GB per rank. With
concurrent send and receive, its ideal time is
\(T_\text{ring} \approx 245/450 \approx 0.54\) s. Equivalently, 490 GB
of sent-plus-received traffic divided by 900 GB/s gives the same time.
Reduction, synchronization, contention and staging can increase the
elapsed time. The ring accounting follows the
\href{https://github.com/NVIDIA/nccl-tests/blob/afd59abab774a6a4b492eb344bd0c845a0b7bb4d/doc/PERFORMANCE.md}{NVIDIA
nccl-tests performance definition}. \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

\textbf{Option B --- InfiniBand across eight logical ranks.} HDR
InfiniBand delivers \textbf{200 Gb/s per link} (≈ 25 GB/s before link
coding; NDR doubles this to 400 Gb/s). InfiniBand uses a 64b/66b line
code, so the useful payload sits a bit below the line rate. A
ring-all-reduce or tree-all-reduce across 8 nodes will see lower
effective bandwidth due to multi-hop forwarding and congestion. A
realistic \emph{effective} throughput is roughly 40 GB/s (aggregated
across 2 HDR rails/ports per node --- the multi-rail effective bandwidth
referenced in §9.2 and \hyperref[fig:9.1]{Fig 9.1}). Using the ring model:

\[
T_\text{ring} \approx 2\frac{N-1}{N}\frac{M}{B_\text{eff}} = 2\frac{7}{8}\frac{140 \text{ GB}}{40 \text{ GB/s}} \approx 6.1 \text{ s}
\]

\Needspace{11\baselineskip}

\textbf{Option C --- 25 Gb/s Ethernet (RoCE2) across 8 nodes.} 25 Gb/s ≈
3.125 GB/s unidirectional. With protocol overhead and multi-node tree
contention, a practical effective bandwidth might be 2.5 GB/s. Ring
model:

\[
T_\text{ring} \approx 2\frac{7}{8}\frac{140 \text{ GB}}{2.5 \text{ GB/s}} \approx 98 \text{ s}
\]

Under these stated assumptions, the InfiniBand time is about 11× the
ideal NVLink ring time, and the Ethernet time is about 180×. A synthetic
iteration containing 100 serialized collectives of this size would spend
about 54 s, 610 s or 9,800 s respectively in communication. These totals
exclude overlap and computation; they do not predict a real training
iteration without its collective schedule.
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

The worked example also highlights why model parallelism and pipeline
parallelism exist: when a single GPU cannot hold the model, splitting it
across nodes moves the communication from the inner rings to the outer
rings, and the architect must budget the extra seconds per collective
against the savings from fitting a larger model.

\ntsection{Measurement}\label{measurement}


Measuring communication in situ is harder than counting tokens, because
the bandwidth numbers above are peaks; real systems see lower effective
bandwidth due to congestion, operating-system interference, and protocol
overhead. Three practical measurements an architect can make:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Correctness and completion.} A known-input test establishes
  the operation, dtype, element count, communicator, reduction, root,
  rank order and output partition. Floating-point comparisons use a
  documented tolerance; bitwise repeatability is a separate result.
  Device producers must finish before the collective reads their
  buffers, and consumers must observe completion rather than host
  submission. A CUDA-stream submission returning successfully does not
  by itself establish that its result is ready on another stream
  (\href{https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/user-guide/docs/usage/streams.html}{NCCL
  stream semantics}).
\item
  \textbf{Collective performance on feasible buffers.} A microbenchmark
  uses resident per-rank allocations that fit after runtime reserves,
  including any separate input/output buffers and scratch space. The
  logical 140 GB example in §9.3 is not a request to allocate 140 GB on
  one 80 GB GPU. The test matrix spans small activation messages and
  larger gradient buckets, group sizes and node mappings. Each result
  records the stack, topology, transport, selected algorithm, warmup,
  sample count, repeated trials and timing boundary. Host enqueue time
  differs from device-completed latency. The original elapsed time stays
  beside \texttt{algbw} and \texttt{busbw}; the latter is an
  algorithm-specific normalization, not a physical-link byte counter
  (\href{https://github.com/NVIDIA/nccl-tests/blob/afd59abab774a6a4b492eb344bd0c845a0b7bb4d/doc/PERFORMANCE.md}{nccl-tests
  performance definitions}). A point-to-point link test answers a
  different question from a collective test.
\item
  \textbf{Service impact under contention.} The same shapes run in the
  actual compute and arrival schedule, first in isolation and then with
  concurrent traffic. Profiler traces separate exposed communication
  stalls, hidden communication and compute slowed by shared-resource
  contention. Summing all collective durations can overstate the exposed
  cost, while subtracting all overlap can understate it. TTFT, ITL,
  errors and SLO-goodput establish the serving consequence; training
  uses device-completed iteration time and its critical path. A
  large-buffer bandwidth result alone does not establish either outcome
  (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}).
\end{enumerate}

Collective tests must also exercise the backend the workload actually
uses. NCCL tests describe an NCCL path; they do not certify XCCL or
HCCL. The examined vLLM XPU configuration selects the XCCL backend with
recent PyTorch
(\href{https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vllm-project/vllm/5df63a7203cdb9328d824a27b682e77c7b9928be/vllm/platforms/xpu.py}{vLLM
XPU platform source, pinned to revision 5df63a7}), while Gaudi exposes
HCCL
(\href{https://docs.habana.ai/en/latest/API_Reference_Guides/HCCL_APIs/index.html}{Habana
HCCL API reference}, retrieved 2026-10-28). The test record therefore
needs rank placement, data type, message size, transport, and a
sent/received bandwidth convention. For Gaudi's documented 24 × 200 Gb/s
port total
(\href{https://cdrdv2-public.intel.com/817486/gaudi-3-ai-accelerator-white-paper.pdf}{Gaudi
3 white paper}), the nominal arithmetic is 600 GB/s per direction or
1,200 GB/s bidirectional aggregate --- derived nominal figures, and
neither is an achieved all-reduce bandwidth. \mbox{{[}1P{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}

The key invariant: always measure on the same hardware and software
stack that the production job will use. A benchmark run on a clean VM
with no other traffic will overstate the achievable bandwidth compared
to a crowded cluster.

Failure tests complete this contract. Missing ranks, mismatched counts
or collective order, network faults and process exits can block a group.
Framework watchdogs, asynchronous library errors and request deadlines
need one recovery policy. An asynchronous success code does not
establish correctness after communicator failure; recovery must be
exercised at the framework and service boundary
(\href{https://docs.nvidia.com/deeplearning/nccl/user-guide/docs/api/comms.html\#ncclcommgetasyncerror}{NCCL
communicator errors and abort}).

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Assuming peak bandwidth is sustained.} PCIe Gen5 x16 peaks at
  \textasciitilde64 GB/s, but a single all-reduce never saturates the
  full link because the traffic is split across multiple GPUs and
  multiple links. Measured effective bandwidth is typically 40--60\% of
  peak; design against the measured number, not the spec sheet peak.
\item
  \textbf{Placing all-reduce on Ethernet without a bandwidth budget.} A
  70B model gradient all-reduce at 25 Gb/s Ethernet (≈ 2.5 GB/s
  practical-effective) takes \textasciitilde56 s per step (a flat lower
  bound; \textasciitilde98 s with the ring factor, §9.3). If the
  training runs 1,000 iterations, that is \textasciitilde56,000 s (15.5
  hours) spent exclusively in communication. Always compute the
  per-iteration communication cost before committing to a
  lower-bandwidth fabric.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring contention from other traffic.} NVSwitch can carry
  many concurrent channels, but if other collectives (e.g., all-gather
  for optimizer state) are running simultaneously, the bisection
  bandwidth is shared. Measure with the full production workload
  pattern, not an isolated micro‑benchmark.
\item
  \textbf{Using InfiniBand as a default without checking the
  cost--performance ratio.} InfiniBand HDR 200 Gb/s gives
  \textasciitilde25 GB/s unidirectional, which is sufficient for many
  workloads, but the price per GB of optics and cables is 5--10× that of
  Ethernet. If the communication time is acceptable on RoCE2, the
  cheaper fabric may be the better architectural choice.
\item
  \textbf{Overlooking protocol overhead.} RoCE and InfiniBand use the
  same 64b/66b line coding but differ in framing and header sizes. An
  NDR 400 Gb/s InfiniBand link delivers a \textasciitilde380 Gb/s class
  useful payload; a 200 Gb/s RoCE2 link delivers \textasciitilde180
  Gb/s. The difference matters when we are computing per-second
  throughput for a fixed data volume.
\end{itemize}

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


The interconnect choice is not a detail to be decided after the model,
the parallelism strategy, and the hardware are selected --- it is a
first-class design variable that shapes all three. If the architecture
calls for data-parallel training of a 70B FP16 model across 8 nodes, the
all-reduce bandwidth dictates whether the nodes are connected by
InfiniBand, 200 GbE, or even 100 GbE. If the budget can accommodate
NVLink‑connected hosts, keeping the all-reduce on-node gives an ideal
ring time about 11× below the assumed InfiniBand case and about 180×
below the assumed RoCE case (§9.3). These ratios apply to this
communication-only model, not to end-to-end throughput.

The architecture consequence also flows in the other direction: a
tighter communication budget (e.g., target \textless0.5 s per
all-reduce) may force a particular parallelism strategy. We might choose
model parallelism (pipeline parallelism, tensor parallelism) to keep the
per-collective data volume smaller, or we might accept a slower fabric
and reduce the frequency of collectives (e.g., gradient accumulation
steps between all-reduce calls). The interconnect bandwidth number is
the anchor that makes these trade-offs quantifiable.

Finally, the architecture consequence extends to cost. A cluster of
8×H100 servers connected by NVSwitch is the highest upfront capital
expenditure, but it delivers the lowest per-iteration communication
time, which translates to higher throughput per dollar over the life of
the cluster. A cluster connected by 200 GbE RoCE has lower capital cost
but higher per-iteration communication time, which may increase the
total cost of ownership if the training job runs for many weeks. The
architect must balance capex vs.~opex by way of the communication
arithmetic shown in this chapter.

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Contention patterns under mixed workloads.} The benchmark
  numbers above assume a dedicated collective on a quiet fabric. In a
  production fleet, other traffic (user requests, other training jobs,
  KV cache movement) shares the same links. The effective bandwidth
  under realistic multi‑job congestion is not yet characterized with a
  standard suite, and the interaction between all-reduce and
  RoCE/Ethernet best‑effort traffic can cause tail latency spikes that
  are difficult to predict.
\item
  \textbf{Adaptive collective algorithms.} New collectives (e.g.,
  gradient compression, sparse all-reduce, error‑bounded approximations)
  claim to reduce the data volume at the cost of a small accuracy loss.
  The bandwidth arithmetic changes when the data volume is 30 GB instead
  of 140 GB, but the latency per byte may increase because of
  compression/decompression steps. The net effect on iteration time is
  workload‑specific and not yet settled.
\item
  \textbf{Optical interconnects and the next generation.} NVLink‑Switch
  and the next‑generation switch fabrics are pushing node‑level
  bandwidth higher (toward 1.8 TB/s aggregate), and optical intra‑rack
  fabrics (400 Gb/s--800 Gb/s per lane) are entering data centers. The
  arithmetic base --- the relative magnitudes of PCIe, NVLink, Ethernet,
  InfiniBand --- is stable in the near term, but the absolute numbers
  will shift as new silicon and cable technologies ship. Keep the
  framework; update the constants.
\item
  \textbf{Cross‑domain generalization.} Most published bandwidth
  measurements use square matrices (all-reduce of a gradient tensor).
  Diagonal dominant tensors, sparse gradients, or structured compression
  may exhibit different effective bandwidths because the traffic pattern
  across links is less uniform. The arithmetic framework applies, but
  the constants need re‑measurement for the specific tensor shape.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: Naming the Parallelism Before the
Arithmetic}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-naming-the-parallelism-before-the-arithmetic}

An architect is asked to design a 70B inference service for a population
of 10,000 users. The request frequency and arrival trace have not yet
been supplied. The team considers replicating an eight-GPU
tensor-parallel engine or partitioning one engine across 32 hosts. The
communication scheme can be assessed before the fleet count is known,
but a user population alone cannot determine that count.

The per-request all-reduce is not the right model for inference, and
neither is a blanket ``all-gather the full weights every decode step.''
It matters which parallelism is in play: ordinary \textbf{tensor
parallelism (TP)} shards each weight matrix across the GPUs, and every
decode step exchanges only the \emph{activations} (not the weights) via
collective ops like all-reduce/all-gather of the small activation
tensors --- weight matrices stay resident and are read in place, they
are not re-fetched each step. A different scheme, FSDP-style
\textbf{weight gathering} (used in training, and in some
sharded-inference arrangements) does all-gather weight shards before the
forward pass. So the architect must name the scheme before doing the
arithmetic. Concretely, for the canonical 8×H100 host the meaningful
per-step data movement is a TP all-reduce of the per-layer activations
(tens of MBs, not GBs) plus the KV cache fragment exchange in
disaggregated serving --- e.g.~\textasciitilde35 GB of KV moved over
NVLink (\textasciitilde0.08 s at 450 GB/s per direction, §9.2) when a
P-D sequence transfers a \textasciitilde13K-token context (35 GB ÷ 2.62
MB/token ≈ 13.3K) between prefill and decode pools. The distinction
changes the answer by orders of magnitude: re-fetching 140 GB of weights
each step is pathological (that is a design smell, an anti-pattern to
flag, not a baseline); exchanging activations or KV fragments is the
normal case that fits comfortably within NVLink.

The architect proposes keeping each tensor-parallel engine within an
eight-GPU host where memory and operator support permit. Additional
independent replicas can then absorb greater traffic without extending
tensor parallelism across hosts. Activation exchange still consumes
bandwidth and synchronization time; it is not free. Cross-host
partitioning becomes a separate candidate when a model or its required
request state cannot fit within the chosen host, or when measurements
demonstrate another benefit. A prefill/decode split also needs a
measured KV-transfer path; NVLink timing only applies when that path is
actually available.

\emph{Scope note.} People, request arrivals and resident requests are
different populations. The 10,000-user setup needs a request-frequency
assumption or trace before \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17} can determine occupancy and fleet
capacity. The separate canonical 10/40 req/s workload can use the
established \hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17} planning analysis. If a deliberately different
storage scenario specifies 10,000 simultaneous full-context resident
requests, the ideal whole-context limit of 17 slots per host gives
\(\lceil 10{,}000/17 \rceil=589\) hosts before throughput, runtime
overhead, placement, SLO headroom and failure reserve. That is a
request-storage lower bound, not a user-derived deployment
recommendation. More replicas do not by themselves require cross-host
model partitioning. \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}DERIVED{]}}
