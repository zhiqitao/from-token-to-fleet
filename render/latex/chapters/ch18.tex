\section*{The Architect's Question}\label{the-architects-question}

\begin{quote}
How does an operator organize a fleet of complementary models---dense
and sparse, quantized and full-precision---so that every request reaches
the right model at the right cost, without over-provisioning or
under-serving?
\end{quote}

This chapter answers that question with real arithmetic, routing logic,
and operational judgment.

\ntsection{Concept}\label{concept}


A mixed-model fleet treats each model family as a resource with distinct
cost, latency, and capability profiles. Rather than running every
request through a single 70B FP16 model on 8×H100, we layer:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Dense FP16} (baseline, high-quality)
\item
  \textbf{Dense 4-bit GGUF} (lowest cost, edge-friendly; highest latency
  --- see §18.3.2)
\item
  \textbf{MoE sparse} (selective activation: fewer FLOPs per token at
  the same weight footprint; the throughput advantage requires batching
  to amortize weight streaming, and per-request latency can exceed a
  dense tier of the same size --- cf.~the 300 ms MoE figure in §18.3.2)
\item
  \textbf{Quantized variants} (8-bit trade-offs; the 4-bit case is the
  Dense 4-bit GGUF row above)
\end{itemize}

The fleet operates on a \emph{routing function} R(query) → model\_id
that maps incoming requests to the cheapest model satisfying quality
constraints.

Key design principles:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Cost-per-token} is the primary unit of comparison across
  quantizations.
\item
  \textbf{Latency-per-token} determines interactive vs.~batch placement.
\item
  \textbf{Capability gaps} (context window, tool-use, multilingual)
  dictate minimum model tiers.
\item
  \textbf{Consolidation} saves capex when overlap in workload profiles
  is high; \textbf{separation} preserves isolation when workloads have
  divergent SLA or security needs.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Routing arithmetic}\label{routing-arithmetic}

Each request carries its full token profile together: 9,200 input + 300
output = 9,500 tokens. \textbf{Request-level routing sends the entire
request --- input and output --- to one model.} A request cannot have
70\% of its input tokens answered by model A and the rest by model B,
unless the architecture explicitly supports model composition, cascade,
or speculative decoding (a different mechanism we do not assume here).
Under ordinary routing, we therefore split by \emph{requests}, not by
in-tokens:

For 1,000 requests/hour (≈0.28 rps --- a light illustrative load; the
arithmetic is scale-invariant in tokens):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{70\% of requests} (700, ≈6.65M tokens) are routine → route to
  70B 8-bit: 6.65M × \$1.25 = \textbf{\$8.31 / hr}
\item
  \textbf{20\% of requests} (200, ≈1.9M tokens) need high-quality
  reasoning → route to 70B MoE 8-bit: 1.9M × \$1.00 = \textbf{\$1.90 /
  hr}
\item
  \textbf{10\% of requests} (100, ≈0.95M tokens) are complex /
  multilingual → route to 70B dense FP16: 0.95M × \$2.50 =
  \textbf{\$2.38 / hr}
\end{itemize}

(Each request's output tokens are generated by the same model that
processed its input. Routing output tokens to a \emph{different} model
would require an explicit cascade/composition stage --- e.g., a
generator plus a separate rewriter --- which is a distinct architecture,
not a free assumption.) The 4-bit GGUF tier (800 ms) is listed for
completeness but is \emph{not} routed to in this 70/20/10 split: at 800
ms it would miss the 500 ms interactive threshold, so the bulk goes to
the 8-bit GPU tier (250 ms) instead --- the same SLO constraint the
§18.8 mini-case works out in full.

\textbf{Mixed-fleet hourly cost} = \$8.31 + \$1.90 + \$2.38 =
\textbf{\$12.59 / hour} vs.~\$23.75 / hour for single-FP16 (all 9.5M
tokens at \$2.50).

\textbf{Monthly savings} ≈ (\$23.75 − \$12.59) × 730\,hr ≈
\textbf{\$8,147 / month} (≈ 47\% reduction) with comparable quality
because the 8-bit model is assumed \mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} to meet quality
thresholds for ≥90\% of requests (no evaluation or benchmark is cited
for this figure).

\textbf{Escalation sensitivity (quality-failure rate → cost).} If a
share \emph{q} of the 700 routine requests fails the 8-bit quality bar
and escalates to FP16, hourly cost rises by \emph{q} × 6.65M × (\$2.50 −
\$1.25)/1M ≈ \emph{q} × \$8.31/hr: at \emph{q} = 10\% the saving falls
from \$11.16/hr to ≈ \$10.33/hr (≈ 43\%), at \emph{q} = 30\% to ≈
\$8.67/hr (≈ 37\%). The ≥90\% quality \mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} above is what
holds \emph{q} at the low end; the savings are conditional on a measured
quality-failure rate, not on the price table alone.

\textbf{Volume basis.} The \$8,147/month figure is the 0.28-rps
illustration above, not a canonical-load figure: at the canonical volume
(6.24M requests/month × 9,500 tokens ≈ 5.93×10¹⁰ tokens) the same
per-token delta scales to ≈ \$69,654/month (\$148,200 single-FP16 −
\$78,546 mixed). The host counts below are sized at the canonical 40-rps
peak (344 requests in flight), so the saving and the host delta sit on
different bases --- compare them at the canonical volume, or re-derive
the saving at 40 rps.

\textbf{Placement assumption, per-tier C and N, and the total-cost
comparison.} The savings above are a token-rate delta only; a
decision-grade comparison must add host amortization and energy. Two
placements are possible: (a) a \textbf{shared} 8×H100 pool holding every
tier's weights + KV, or (b) \textbf{dedicated} host groups, one per
tier. Under the shared pool the co-resident weights (\textasciitilde350
GB, §18.3.1) cut pool KV to 640 − 350 − 64 = 226 GB, so C = ⌊226/24.89⌋
= 9 and N = ⌈344/9⌉ = \textbf{39 hosts} mixed (shared pool, no
utilization derate) / ⌈344/(9 × 0.665)⌉ = \textbf{58 hosts} mixed (with
the 70\%×95\% policy) --- both on the KV-only C = 9; a co-resident 22B
specialist (\textasciitilde22 GB) gives C = 8 and N = \textbf{43 / 65},
against 21/31 single-model under the canonical C = 17 --- 31 (no spare)
/ 32 (with the N−1 spare, the Ch 17 provisioned fleet). Under dedicated
placement each tier gets its own group, and each host holds \textbf{one}
8-bit replica (2 GPUs, \textasciitilde70 GB) with the canonical TP=8
pool: 8-bit C = ⌊506/24.89⌋ = 20 (640 − 70 − 64 = 506 GB) → 13 + 4 + 3 =
\textbf{20 hosts / 32} with policy. \textbf{The mixed fleet needs more
hosts than the single-model fleet under the shared placement (58 vs 31
--- 27 extra hosts; 39 vs 21 --- 18 extra --- on the no-derate pair).}
Under the \textbf{dedicated} placement the no-policy pair falls below
the single-model fleet (20 vs 21) while the policy pair is marginally
above it (32 vs 31) --- the ledger narrows rather than reverses. This
worked example adopts the \textbf{shared pool} (the placement §18.3.1
describes), so the \textasciitilde\$8,147/month (≈ \$98K/yr) token
saving must be weighed against \emph{that} placement's extra hosts'
amortization and energy: total cost = host amortization + energy + token
cost, not token cost alone. The sign of the host delta is a placement
decision, not a property of consolidation.

\subsection{Latency check}\label{latency-check}

The per-request latency is that of the model serving it. The
weighted-average request latency is

\[
\bar{L} = \sum_i p_i \cdot L_i = 0.70 \times 250 + 0.20 \times 300 + 0.10 \times 120 \approx 247 \text{ ms/request}
\]

(no separate output-routing term --- output rides with its requesting
model), where \(p_i\) is the fraction of requests routed to model \(i\)
and \(L_i\) its per-request service latency. Acceptable for interactive
chat (typical threshold: ≤500 ms).

\emph{Column definition (the column's scope note lives in the §3 bases
box):} \textbf{Per-request service latency = time-to-first-token for the
short-prompt routing scenario} (not full generation; not the canonical
TTFT/TPOT SLO, Ch 4) --- under a full-generation reading the CPU tier's
4-bit model (\textasciitilde35 GB ÷ \textasciitilde75 GB/s ≈ 0.47
s/token) would need \textasciitilde117 s for 250 tokens,
\textasciitilde146× the printed 800 ms, so only the TTFT reading is
defensible. The 120/250/300/800 ms values are \emph{illustrative
per-request latencies for a short-prompt routing scenario} (the kind of
request a router triages in milliseconds), used only to compare the four
models against the interactive threshold (≤500 ms). They are
\textbf{not} the \textasciitilde8.6 s service time of the 9.5K-token
canonical RAG request (Ch 17), which is a multi-second, capacity-side
number that governs fleet sizing. The cost column, by contrast,
\emph{is} computed on the canonical 9.5K-token request (6.65M tokens for
700 requests). A deployment benchmark (Ch 14) must supply the real
per-model service times before either column is used for a production
decision.

The latency and capacity must also reconcile with the canonical
workload's KV residency (\hyperref[chap:17]{Chapter 17}): routing 70\% of requests to an
8-bit model that needs ≥2 GPUs (1×H100 holds the weights plus only
\textasciitilde10 GB of KV --- zero concurrent canonical requests,
§18.3.1) changes the per-host KV ceiling accordingly, and the fleet host
count is set by the model each tier runs on. These are
\mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} pricing and latency figures for Q4 2026; the
architect re-runs the same arithmetic with measured per-model
cost/latency from a deployment benchmark (\hyperref[chap:14]{Chapter 14}).

\subsection{Consolidation vs.~separation
decision}\label{consolidation-vs.-separation-decision}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Consolidate} when: workload profile overlap ≥70\%, latency
  tolerance ≥300\,ms, and cost differential \textgreater30\%. These
  thresholds are \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}}\hspace{0pt}\mbox{{[}ASSUMPTION{]}} design criteria for
  the worked example, not universal rules; a deployment should set its
  own consolidation thresholds from its SLO, cost model and workload
  mix. \emph{Satisfied by construction in this scenario}: our scenario
  sits exactly at the threshold --- it consolidates 70\% of traffic to
  the dense 8-bit tier --- 90\% to 8-bit variants once the 20\% MoE
  8-bit share is counted --- at the ≥70\% dense-8-bit boundary, not
  above it, saving \textasciitilde\$11.16/hr (\$23.75 − \$12.59, the
  §18.3.3 derived figure). \emph{Referents:} ``satisfied by
  construction'' speaks to the ≥70\% overlap and \textgreater30\%
  cost-differential criteria; the latency criterion is met against the
  ≤500\,ms interactive threshold of §18.3.4, not the
  \textasciitilde200\,ms chat tolerance quoted in §18.2 --- L̄ ≈ 247\,ms
  clears 500\,ms and exceeds 200\,ms, so consolidation is eligible for a
  ≤500\,ms per-request chat budget and would fail a 200\,ms budget.
\item
  \textbf{Keep separate} when: (a) security/classification policies
  require isolation, (b) workloads have bimodal latency needs (some
  ≤50\,ms, others batch-tolerant), or (c) model versioning cadence
  differs (e.g., frequent fine-tuning on one family, stable other).
\end{itemize}

In our example, consolidation wins on token rate because the 8-bit and
MoE tiers together capture \textasciitilde90\% of request classes at
lower cost than FP16; the FP16 tier still handles the tail without
contention. But under the adopted shared placement of §18.3.3 the 18--27
extra hosts' amortization plus energy (≈\$134--200K/mo at the Ch 16
per-host basis) exceeds the ≈\$69.7K/mo saving, so the total-cost sign
is undetermined until a marginal per-host cost is supplied (break-even ≈
\$69,654/18 ≈ \$3,870/host/mo). The FP16 tier has no separate reserve in
this worked example: per §18.3.1 it shares the 640\,GB pool --- 140\,GB
of weights plus KV --- and at the canonical 9.5K profile it cannot be a
2-GPU reserve, because a 2-GPU shard holds 160\,GB − 140\,GB = 20\,GB of
KV headroom against the 24.9\,GB that one 9,500-token request needs (κ =
2.62\,MB/token, Ch 17). Two reserved GPUs suffice only for the
\textasciitilde250-token support queries of §18.8, not for the canonical
9.5K request. Under the dedicated placement of §18.3.3 the FP16 tier
would instead get its own group (3 of the 20 hosts) --- that group, not
a 2-GPU reserve, is what ``handles the tail without contention'' above.

\ntsection{Common Mistakes}\label{common-mistakes}


\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Treating quantization as free.} Quantization is not free: on
  CPU/GGUF it adds per-token dequantization work (the §18.3.4 arithmetic
  shows why a CPU 4-bit tier cannot meet an interactive SLO); on GPU it
  trades weight memory for lower-precision Tensor Core throughput and
  sometimes a KV-format change, and either can cost quality on
  long-context tasks. Always measure the quality hit before routing en
  masse.
\item
  \textbf{One-size-fits-all routing.} A static rule (``always use
  8-bit'') fails when workload mix shifts (e.g., seasonal increase in
  multilingual queries that need FP16 precision). Use a dynamic router
  with feature-gate checks.
\item
  \textbf{Ignoring capital amortization.} FP16 on 8×H100 carries high
  upfront cost; annualize GPU capex into the per-token rate and compare
  placements at the same volume. A mixed fleet does not by itself reduce
  capital per token --- under the shared placement of §18.3.3 it adds
  18--27 hosts; it lowers cost per token only where the added hosts
  raise utilization of capacity that would otherwise sit idle.
\item
  \textbf{Over-provisioning the top tier.} Keeping a full FP16 GPU idle
  ``just in case'' is expensive. Keep just enough capacity for the tail
  (≈10\% of peak rps --- an \mbox{{[}ILLUSTRATIVE{]}} assumption to be derived
  from the measured tail and the SLO, not a measured figure) and route
  the rest: N\_tail = ⌈λ\_tail·W/(C·u·ρ)⌉ = ⌈4 × 8.6/(17 × 0.70 × 0.95)⌉
  = ⌈34.4/11.305⌉ ≈ \textbf{4 hosts} at the canonical 40-rps peak (u =
  utilization target 0.70, ρ = policy yield 0.95, C = 17 is the
  single-model FP16 pool; the shared mixed pool's C = 9 of §18.3.3 would
  raise this tail count to \textasciitilde6 under the same 0.70×0.95
  policy).
\item
  \textbf{No fallthrough plan.} If the preferred model is at capacity,
  the router must have a next‑best alternative ready; otherwise requests
  time out or queue indefinitely.
\end{enumerate}

A routing candidate is a validated deployment variant, not only a model
family. A lower-precision variant can lower resource use, but its
quality loss may appear selectively in reasoning, code, tool arguments
or long contexts. Retries and longer generations can consume the
apparent savings. The routing table therefore attaches quality and SLO
evidence to the exact precision/backend combination, and the fallback
preserves the task's acceptance conditions. A change to scales, excluded
layers or execution kernels creates a new candidate for validation; it
does not inherit the previous candidate's quality curve automatically.

\ntsection{Architecture Consequence}\label{architecture-consequence}


A mixed-model fleet changes the architecture in three ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Router shim} (HTTP middleware or gRPC interceptor) sits
  between the user API and the model execution layer. It evaluates
  feature requirements, reads current capacity, and selects model\_id.
\item
  \textbf{Capacity pool abstraction} -- rather than ``GPU 0 runs model
  A,'' the system tracks, per placement, weights, reserved KV, free KV
  and the resulting concurrency C;\,the pool manager places on KV
  headroom, not on weights.
\item
  \textbf{Billing/telemetry pipeline} -- each request's cost and latency
  are tagged with model\_id, enabling downstream dashboards and alerting
  (e.g., ``cost per token rising above \$0.0000025 --- population: every
  request routed by R() across all tiers, fleet-wide blended; threshold
  ≈1.9× the §18.3.3 mixed-fleet average of \$12.59/9.5M ≈
  \$0.000001325/token (the §18.3.2 FP16 rate of \$2.50/1M in per-token
  units; §18.8's mini-case FP16 tier is quoted on its own per-token
  basis at \$0.003/token) --- for 3 consecutive hours'').
\end{enumerate}

The fleet operator becomes a \emph{cost‑latency steward} rather than a
single-model optimizer.

A small local model can be a candidate for an Intel GPU or an OpenVINO
client path when its operators, memory budget and service behavior pass

A routing policy also needs the cost of failure. If a local attempt
misses its contract and falls back to a central service, both attempts
contribute resource use and end-to-end delay. The fallback route must
preserve the data policy and still meet any applicable deadline.
Promotion therefore follows a canary on the exact model/backend
configuration, with monitored cache behavior, correctness, tail latency
and rollback. Replacing a driver, kernel package or graph mode can
change that configuration even when the model identifier is
unchanged.\footnote{\raggedright

This is a general qualification procedure illustrated with an Intel
example, not a claim about the mini-case's AMD Ryzen node.

\ntsection{What We Still Don't Know}\label{what-we-still-dont-know}


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Quality--cost curves for new quantizations.} As 3-bit and
  sub-4-bit GGUF models emerge, the trade-off surface shifts; we need
  systematic human evals + automatic benchmarks (MT‑Bench with a named
  judge model; FLAN-style held-out task sets --- protocol to be
  specified) across families.
\item
  \textbf{Optimal router policy for non‑stationary workloads.}
  Reinforcement‑learning--based routing that adapts α,β weights in real
  time is promising but underexplored in production fleets.
\item
  \textbf{Cross-model distillation opportunities.} (a) Does a smaller
  student distilled from a 70B FP16 teacher reach the quality bar that
  forces requests to the FP16 tier, and what is its serving cost per
  token? (b) Separately, what quality is lost by quantizing that
  distilled student? Empirical work needed.
\item
  \textbf{Edge--cloud continuum effects.} When users bring their own
  4-bit‑quantized local models (via Ollama, etc.), the fleet routing
  must account for hybrid inbound/outbound token flows.
\end{itemize}

\section{End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: A Three-Tier Customer-Support Model
Fleet}\label{end-of-chapter-mini-case-a-three-tier-customer-support-model-fleet}

\textbf{Deploy a three‑tier fleet for a customer‑support chatbot.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Tier 1 (4-bit GGUF):} Handles 80\% of queries (FAQ, account
  lookup). Runs on edge nodes (AMD Ryzen 7950X, 64 GB RAM). Cost:
  \$0.0002/token, latency \textasciitilde600 ms. \emph{Memory headroom:}
  35 GB of 4-bit weights in 64 GB RAM leaves \textasciitilde29 GB for KV
  + runtime --- 29/24.9 ≈ 1.16, i.e.~an upper bound of \textasciitilde1
  concurrent 9,500-token request's KV, before any runtime/workspace
  reserve. The §18.3.1 qualification footnotes (OpenVINO, Intel Core
  Ultra X9 388H, vLLM XPU) are a general qualification procedure, not
  evidence for this AMD node.
\item
  \textbf{Tier 2 (70B 8-bit dense):} Handles 15\% of queries (order
  status, policy look-up). Runs on 1×H100 --- unlike the canonical 9.5K
  request (§18.3.1, ≥2 GPUs), these \textasciitilde250-token requests
  carry \textasciitilde0.66 GB KV each, so 1×H100's \textasciitilde10 GB
  headroom holds \textasciitilde15 concurrent requests
  (short-prompt-only placement). Cost: \$0.0015/token, latency
  \textasciitilde250 ms.
\item
  \textbf{Tier 3 (70B FP16):} Handles 5\% of queries (complex
  escalations, multilingual). Runs on 2 GPUs reserved. Cost:
  \$0.003/token, latency \textasciitilde120 ms.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Arithmetic:} A small pilot deployment serving
\textasciitilde1,000 registered users at a modest, deliberately low
volume --- \textbf{5,000 tokens/hour} (each request averaging
\textasciitilde250 tokens gives roughly 20 requests/hour, a very light
load chosen to make the per-token routing economics concrete rather than
to model a busy fleet; the traffic rate is not what drives this
arithmetic, the per-token volume is). Hourly tokens:
\textasciitilde4,500 in + 500 out = 5,000. \emph{(Scope note: these tier
per-token rates are illustrative scenario values that \textbf{differ
from the §18.3.2 per-1M ledger} --- they are not the ledger's \$1.25
(8-bit) / \$2.50 (FP16) / \$0.15 (4-bit) per-1M rates converted, and the
4-bit tier's \textasciitilde600 ms latency differs from the ledger's 800
ms. These per-token rates are pilot-amortized: a dedicated host's cost
spread over \textasciitilde20 requests/hour puts them
\textasciitilde1,200--1,333× above the fleet-scale §18.3.2 ledger
(\$200/\$1,500/\$3,000 per 1M vs \$0.15/\$1.25/\$2.50). Only the
mini-case's internal ratios are meaningful; its absolute dollars are not
comparable to §18.3.3.)}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Tier\,1 processes 4,000 tokens/hr → \$0.80
\item
  Tier\,2 processes 750 tokens/hr → \$1.13
\item
  Tier\,3 processes 250 tokens/hr → \$0.75
\item
  \textbf{Total hourly cost: \$2.68} → \textbf{\$1,956 / month} (× 730
  hr).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Check the SLO before the cost claim.} If we had used a single
70B FP16 model: 5,000 tokens/hr × \$0.003 = \$15/hr → \$10,950 / month
(× 730 hr). The three-tier split looks like an \$8,994 / month (≈82\%)
saving --- but the SLO gates the cost claim, and here it fails.
\textbf{Tier 1 (the cheapest, 4-bit GGUF on CPU, \textasciitilde600 ms)
serves 80\% of queries, so the p95 per-request latency is
\textasciitilde600 ms, which violates the 500 ms SLO for the dominant
tier.} The split saves cost by routing the bulk to a slow-but-cheap
model --- precisely the mean-vs-tail trap: the \emph{average} does not
look fine either --- 0.80 × 600 + 0.15 × 250 + 0.05 × 120 = 523.5 ms
\textgreater{} the 500 ms SLO --- so both the mean and the p95 fail, and
the majority tier misses the deadline. The \$8,994 / month saving is
therefore \emph{conditional} on first making Tier 1 feasible.

\textbf{Recalculated cost after substituting a feasible Tier 1.} Replace
the 4-bit GGUF (CPU, \textasciitilde600 ms) with 8-bit quantization on a
GPU (\textasciitilde250 ms, \$0.0015/token --- the same rate as Tier 2,
which is also 8-bit on 1×H100). \emph{Note the collapse:}
post-substitution Tier 1 is configuration-identical to Tier 2 (8-bit,
1×H100, \$0.0015/token, \textasciitilde250 ms), so 4,750 of 5,000
tokens/hr (95\%) run on one configuration and the three-tier fleet
collapses to two distinct configurations. The 80\% of queries now route
at \$0.0015/token:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Tier 1 (8-bit on GPU) processes 4,000 tokens/hr → \$6.00
\item
  Tier 2 (70B 8-bit dense) processes 750 tokens/hr → \$1.13
\item
  Tier 3 (70B FP16) processes 250 tokens/hr → \$0.75
\item
  \textbf{Total hourly cost: \$7.88} → \textbf{\$5,752 / month} (× 730
  hr).
\end{itemize}

The p95 is now \textasciitilde250 ms (all tiers under 500 ms), so the
SLO is met. The saving narrows from \$8,994 / month (≈82\%) to
\textbf{\$5,198 / month (≈47\%)} --- still a clear win, but the
SLO-constrained figure, not the raw 82\%, is the honest number. The
correct order \emph{within the cost/SLO gate} is: check the SLO, then
size the cost (the capability filter still comes first, per \hyperref[fig:18.1]{Fig 18.1} and
\hyperref[tab:18.1]{Table 18-1}). The routing arithmetic shows the \emph{economic} win; the
SLO check is what keeps it honest, and it must come first \emph{in the
cost analysis}.

The key enabler was the router's feature gate: queries mentioning
``multilingual'' or ``technical specification'' were auto‑escalated to
Tier\,3; the rest flowed to Tier\,1 or Tier\,2 automatically.
