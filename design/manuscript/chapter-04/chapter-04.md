# Chapter 4 — The Anatomy of an AI Workload

## The Architect's Question

After this chapter we should be able to take a vague deployment ask — "thousands of employees asking questions over internal documents" — and turn it into a *measurable workload* across six concrete dimensions. We will trace the arithmetic from user concurrency through traffic, token profile, latency, economics, and operational constraints, ending with a workload characterization that directly enables system architecture decisions. After this chapter, the question "which model should we choose?" can be answered with numbers, not intuition.

## 1. Concept

### The Six-Dimension Workload-Characterization Framework

A workload is defined by six dimensions (Table 4-1 applies the framework to the canonical workload). Together they form a complete specification that — jointly, not taken alone — constrains model selection, system architecture, and economic feasibility. No single dimension is sufficient; the architect must characterize all six before committing to a design.

1. **Quality** — the fidelity, accuracy, and capability the workload demands of the model. Includes reasoning depth, tool-use requirements, modality (text‑only, multimodal, etc.), and any accuracy thresholds (e.g. >85% factual accuracy on internal Q&A). Quality is the *goal*; the other dimensions are the *constraints* within which quality must be delivered.

2. **Traffic** — the request rate the system must sustain, expressed in requests per second (rps) or queries per second, plus its burst shape (sustained level, peak-to-average ratio, burst duration). **Concurrency is not a traffic input:** it is a serving-side quantity derived from the arrival rate and the time in system (Little's law), computed separately in this chapter as execution occupancy (§4.3). Traffic, combined with the token profile and the latency SLO, determines the compute and memory bandwidth required to keep the serving pipeline saturated without excessive queuing.

3. **Token profile** — the distribution of input length, output length, and context length per request. This is the workload's "shape" in token space: how many tokens arrive in the prompt, how many the model generates, and whether the prompt is dense (long context) or sparse. The token profile is the primary driver of memory (KV cache) and prefill cost.

4. **Latency** — the time budget for different stages of request processing. Typically split into *time-to-first-token* (TTFT), which encompasses retrieval and prefill, and *time-per-output-token* (TPOT), which governs decode speed (the rate of token generation, not the number of tokens generated). Latency budgets feed directly into SLOs and serve as the key differentiator between serving configurations (e.g. continuous batching vs. discrete batching).

5. **Economic constraints** — the cost budget denominated in tokens per dollar, dollars per million tokens, or cost per request. This dimension translates traffic and token profile into a recurring cost line, and it determines whether a given infrastructure choice (single node vs. multi‑node, FP16 vs. FP8) is affordable at scale.

6. **Operational constraints** — availability, privacy, locality, and update frequency. Multi‑region deployment for fault tolerance. Data‑privacy requirements that force on‑prem or VPC‑local embedding models. Update frequency that dictates how often embeddings or model weights must be refreshed. These constraints often interact with traffic and economics to force architectural compromises.

The framework is deliberately dimensional: a workload that is "high‑quality, high‑traffic, long‑context, low‑latency, cost‑sensitive, private" is fundamentally different from "low‑quality, low‑traffic, short‑context, high‑latency, cost‑abundant, public," even if the raw text looks the same. The framework prevents the architect from optimizing one dimension at the expense of others — a common failure mode.

## 2. Mental Model

The six dimensions are best read as the axes of a six‑dimensional space in which every AI workload resides. A workload's position in this space is its *fingerprint*. When an architect says "we need a model for enterprise Q&A," that is only a descriptor of the quality dimension. To make the statement actionable, we must project the workload onto all six axes: *what quality level, how much traffic, what token profile, what latency SLO, what economic ceiling, what operational constraints?* The intersection of these projections is the workload point that drives every downstream decision — model selection, infrastructure sizing, serving configuration, and TCO.

The mental model is not a checklist; it is a *lens*. Looking through it, the architect sees which dimensions are tight (binding) and which are loose (permissive). Tight dimensions become the design drivers; loose dimensions offer optimization freedom. The goal is to identify the binding constraints so that resources are allocated where they matter most.

#### Arithmetic Walk‑Through: From Users to Declared Traffic and Derived Occupancy

The step from "~2,000 registered users" to "~10 rps average" is the key connective tissue that makes the continuous scenario concrete. Here is the declaration-plus-occupancy walk-through, traceable line by line:

1. **Concurrency**: a fraction $f$ of the $N$ registered users are active at any moment. With $f = 0.05$ and $N = 2{,}000$ (the active-user population is written $U$; $C$ is reserved throughout the book for a host's KV slot capacity, $C ≈ 17.5$ — §4.6, Ch 17):

$$
U = f \cdot N = 0.05 \times 2{,}000 = 100 \text{ concurrent users}
$$

2. **Request completion time** (service time $W$): the average end‑to‑end time from request submission to answer delivery. We deliberately keep TWO named values, and the distinction matters: a **planning round number of ~10 s** (retrieval ~120 ms + prefill budget cap ~1.08 s + decode ~7.5 s ≈ 8.7 s, plus ~15% planning headroom to ~10 s — an author-chosen planning figure, not a measurement) versus the **derived model-execution service time of ~8.6 s** (prefill 1.08 s + decode 7.5 s, without the retrieval/plumbing term) that the later fleet chapters use in Little's-law sizing. The two differ by the retrieval/pipeline room and by rounding; they are not a numerical contradiction. Whichever we use, we must use it consistently — the canonical ledger pins the fleet-sizing service time at ~8.6 s (Chapters 16–17 use it for Little's-law occupancy; Chapter 8 gives the prefill + decode decomposition), and the ~10 s figure is the coarser planning-round headline. **The 1.08 s prefill term inside $W_{\mathrm{exec}}$ is the two-term budget cap, not a modelled runtime** (Table 4-2: TTFT 1.2 s − retrieval 120 ms, with zero allowance for admission/queue and tokenization/transfer). The modelled host prefill is ~0.16 s at peak and ~0.47 s at 35% MFU (§4.3; Ch 2, Ch 8). So $W_{\mathrm{exec}}$ ≈ 8.6 s is an *upper* bound on the execution window, and the Little's-law occupancies (~86 / ~344) and every host count derived from them (Chapters 7, 16–17) inherit that upper-bound status — conservative, not measured.

3. **Arrival rate — a declared traffic assumption, NOT a Little's-law derivation from the active-user population**: Little's Law, $L = \lambda W$, relates the average number of *requests in the defined system* $L$, the arrival rate $\lambda$, and the mean time in system $W$. It cannot be applied to a count of *active people* — 100 recently-active users is not the same as 100 requests in the serving system. So the ~10 rps average is **declared as a scenario input** (≈ 2,000 registered users × one request ~every 200 s → ~10 rps), not solved for from the 100-user population.

4. **Peak arrival rate**: ~40 rps is the same behavior scaled to a burst/traffic spike — again a declared scenario input (see step 3).

The walk-through makes the *distinctions* explicit: the **~100 active users** are a population/activity measure, not a request-occupancy count; the **~10 rps average / ~40 rps peak** are declared traffic assumptions (scenario inputs), not derived from the user count; the **~86 / ~344 execution occupancy** is derived from those arrival rates via Little's Law (L = λ × $W_{\mathrm{exec}}$, $W_{\mathrm{exec}}$ ≈ 8.6 s). The point of the exercise is to keep population, arrival rate, and occupancy separate, not to claim that a registered-user count empirically determines an arrival rate. Change any one assumption and the others shift accordingly; the architect recomputes them when the requirement changes. If the architect starts from a measured 10 rps instead, the relation runs the other way (it yields ~86 in-flight at W ≈ 8.6 s, not a user count).

Before moving on, it is worth naming the four distinct quantities so they are not collapsed: **registered users** are the population; **concurrent users** are the activity state (active users in the modeled window, not in-flight requests); **arrival rate** (λ) is requests per unit time; and **service time** (W) is the end-to-end duration of a request. Little's Law, L = λW, relates only the request-level quantities — L (in-flight requests), λ, and W — not the registered- or active-user population; the architect must *estimate or measure* request concurrency, arrival rate, and service time — none is a fixed property of the workload. Two further separations matter for KV planning and are easy to collapse, so we hold them apart here too:

**"Concurrent users" here means *active users*, not simultaneous in-flight inference requests.** The 5% figure gives ~100 active users in the modeled activity window — people who are using the tool over the window — not 100 requests that are all mid-execution at the same instant. The in-flight *request* concurrency is a different quantity, computed from Little's Law with the derived service time W ≈ 8.6 s. The two operating points are distinct rows, not one figure reconciled by an unstated multiplier:

| Operating point | Active users (population) | Arrival rate λ | In-flight requests (λ·W, W≈8.6 s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Average | 100 (5% × 2,000) | ~10 rps | ~86 |
| Peak | 400 (20% × 2,000) | ~40 rps | ~344 |

The reason these columns differ in kind is that **active-user population** and **request occupancy** are different state variables. Active users is a *population* measure — how many people engaged the tool over the modeled window. Request occupancy is an *instantaneous* Little's-law quantity — how many requests are resident in the serving system at a moment. They are related only through the arrival rate and service time, not through a per-user request multiplicity. So at the average operating point the population (~100) and the occupancy (~86) happen to be close, while at the peak they differ (400 vs 344); neither gap needs a "one user issues several requests" reconciliation, and we do not claim one. Whenever the book says "concurrent users," read "active users in the modeled activity window"; whenever it needs the request-level occupancy that a host's KV budget must absorb, use the Little's-law λ·W figure for that specific operating point.

**The ~40 rps peak is assumed sustained over the provisioning burst interval, and the interval now has a stated duration: up to ~15 minutes** *[canonical scenario][ASSUMPTION] declared burst-length input, not a measured traffic trace* — long enough for Little's Law and service-time occupancy to reach the modeled steady state (at least the service-time scale, $W_{\mathrm{exec}}$ ≈ 8.6 s — not a one-second or few-second spike), and not a one-request 40-rps spike. The ~15-minute bound is the time basis the peak-fleet sizing inherits (Ch 17): a longer peak keeps the same provisioned count but eats the 95% SLO headroom and the N−1 margin, while a much shorter burst can be absorbed by a burst buffer instead of a provisioned peak fleet — which is why the average-fleet and peak-fleet figures (6 vs 21 hosts) are only comparable once this duration is stated. A one-second spike would not populate the queue to λ·W; the fleet sizing deliberately treats 40 rps as the sustained burst the system must absorb. A load generator reproducing this workload should emit the same sustained arrival process, not merely target the same average rps.

- **Concurrent *users* ≠ concurrent *requests*.** "100 concurrent users" is a statement about the *population's activity*, not about how many requests are in flight at the scheduler at any instant. The two are different state variables, and they need not be close — at the average operating point they happen to be (~100 vs ~86), at the peak they differ (~400 vs ~344).
- **Requests *in flight* ≠ KV-resident sequences.** A request in the queue, or one whose prefill is not yet admitted, is "in flight" from the user's perspective but does not yet hold a KV slot. In-flight concurrency upper-bounds the KV-concurrency the system must serve, but the KV budget is sized by the number of *resident active sequences* — which is what the concurrency ceilings in Ch 17 count. Little's Law gives the in-flight number; the KV budget is a separate, residency-side quantity obtained by multiplying the resident sequence count by per-token KV. Keeping these three apart (in flight vs resident vs per-token KV) is what prevents the "100 users → 100 KV slots" leap.

The "5% active" and the "~10 s planning headline" (the planning service-time assumption — not the $W_{\mathrm{exec}}$ ≈ 8.6 s used in occupancy arithmetic) are deliberately simple scenario assumptions ([canonical scenario][ASSUMPTION]), not externally verified facts; in a real engagement the architect replaces them with observed concurrency, measured latency, and a burst profile from telemetry. Getting this separation right is what turns traffic modeling from a guess into a reproducible arithmetic.

#### Token Profile in Detail

The canonical workload's token profile is input‑heavy, which has direct consequences for system design:

- **Per‑request input**: 1,200 tokens (enterprise query, possibly reformulated) + 8,000 tokens (retrieved context from vector DB) ≈ 9,200 tokens. The [canonical scenario][ASSUMPTION] provenance traces to the canonical scenario (Ch 4, Table 4-2).
- **Per‑request output**: ~300 tokens (the generated answer, possibly with citations).
- **Input‑to‑output ratio**: 9,200 ÷ 300 ≈ 30× more input tokens than output tokens. This ratio is [canonical scenario][DERIVED] from the canonical numbers and sets the relative prefill‑versus‑decode *work* split; the 9,200‑token absolute context length is what sets KV residency per request and therefore the concurrency ceiling. The canonical workload's FLOP share is **prefill-heavy** and its capacity pressure is **KV-heavy**, but its per-request wall-clock is **decode-heavy** — because the 300-token output at ~25 ms/TPOT costs ~7.5 s of the ~8.6 s execution window, not because of the input/output ratio. These are three different senses of load (share of FLOPs, capacity pressure, share of wall-clock), and none contradicts the others (KV residency also scales with context length × concurrency × bytes/token, and decode throughput must still be checked at the target batch size and SLO).
- **At 10 rps**: input tokens/s = 9,200 ×10 = 92,000 tokens/s; output tokens/s = 300 ×10 = 3,000 tokens/s. At peak 40 rps, input spikes to ~368,000 tokens/s and output to ~12,000 tokens/s. These [canonical scenario][DERIVED] numbers are the Canonical Workload box's derived rates (above).

The input-heavy profile means the system's dominant resource demands are **prefill compute and KV-memory capacity**; under the canonical timing assumptions prefill is **not** the dominant wall-clock component of an individual request. The 1.08 s prefill figure is the *two-term budget cap* (TTFT 1.2 s − retrieval 120 ms; Table 4-2), not a modeled prefill runtime: the modeled host prefill is ~0.16 s at peak and ~0.47 s at 35% MFU (Ch 2), and only the budget-cap term enters the execution window used here. On that budget-cap basis the window is ~8.58 s and generating the 300-token answer takes about 7.5 s (300 × 25 ms/TPOT) — so decode is the dominant per-request wall-clock component (~87% of the ~8.58 s window: 7.5 ÷ 8.58 = 87.4%; at the modeled ~0.47 s prefill the share is higher, 7.5 ÷ 7.97 ≈ 94%; the end-to-end user-visible total additionally carries retrieval ~120 ms and admission/queue, so the end-to-end decode share is marginally lower). A model that processes the 9,200-token input inside that 1.08 s prefill budget is important to keep the prefill leg within the 1.2 s TTFT budget, but it does not make prefill the latency bottleneck; that distinction is a resource-regime statement (in the canonical long-prompt, low-batch regime, prefill is compute-bound and decode is bandwidth-bound), not a wall-clock latency ranking. Architecture choices must be based on phase-specific demand, measured utilization, and SLO-goodput, rather than the input/output token ratio alone. This is why the token profile is the primary architectural driver for this workload.

#### Latency from SLO

The latency dimension is specified through Service Level Objectives, which translate user‑experience goals into concrete time budgets:

- **TTFT (time-to-first-token)**: budget of 1.2 s, comprising retrieval (~120 ms for vector search and reranking on a local GPU) + prefill (budget cap 1.08 s for 9,200 tokens across the 8×H100 canonical host — a *budget*, not a modeled runtime. The cap implies ≈8,500 tokens/s (9,200 ÷ 1.08 = 8,519): that is only the budget-implied single-request rate for a request that holds the whole host, not the modeled or measured prefill rate. The modeled host prefill is ~0.16 s at peak and ~0.47 s at 35% MFU, and the host's capacity screen is the ~19,800 tok/s sustained-planning rate at 35% MFU — a planning rate, not a ceiling. The minimum GPU count for this single-request prefill deadline under the stated 35% MFU assumption is four, not eight: the 9.2K prefill is ≈1.29 PFLOP of work at 140 GFLOP/token, implying ≈1.19 PFLOPS of required compute rate over 1.08 s (9,200 × 140 GFLOP ÷ 1.08 s); one H100 sustains ~2,472 tok/s at 35% MFU, so a single GPU needs ~3.7 s for it (9,200 ÷ 2,472 = 3.72 s) and fails the cap, while ⌈1.19 PFLOPS ÷ (0.989 × 0.35)⌉ ≈ 4 GPUs meet it — the 8-GPU host needs ~0.47 s (9,200 ÷ 19,780 = 0.465 s). That four is a per-request prefill-feasibility count under the stated MFU assumption — not a deployable GPU count and not a deployment recommendation. The canonical 8-GPU choice is driven by concurrency, KV capacity and headroom, and the deployable host count is Ch 17's (Table 17-1), not this cap's. Per Ch 2 and Ch 8). The **p95 TTFT ceiling (≤ 2 s)** must hold — a tail ceiling above the 1.2 s design budget, allowing occasional cache misses or network jitter to land between the 1.2 s design budget and the 2 s ceiling. The two thresholds are not interchangeable: 1.2 s is the design budget, ≤ 2 s is the p95 ceiling the budget relaxes to, and any pass/fail judgement on a measured distribution is made against the p95 ceiling, not the budget. **TTFT_e2e convention (used throughout):** $\text{TTFT}_{\text{e2e}} = \text{retrieval} + \text{admission/queue} + \text{tokenization/transfer} + \text{prefill}$. Here the 1.2 s budget names the *two dominant modeled terms* — retrieval (~120 ms) + prefill (~1.08 s) — and the 1.08 s is therefore a **two-term-only upper reach, NOT a feasible deployed prefill target**: it leaves zero allowance for admission/queue and tokenization/transfer, which are positive. Write it as `budget_prefill ≤ 1.08 s − B_other` until measurement supplies B_other; every "meets 1.2 s" statement needs a named tested workload and end-to-end timing (see the "Prefill compute budget — two-term-only upper cap, NOT a feasible deployed prefill target" row in Table 4-2). The 1.2 s is an **aspirational SLO budget** (a sub-2-second user-experience target), not a demonstrated p95 TTFT from a benchmark; the ≤2 s p95 is the budgeted ceiling, and the demonstrated value under load requires an actual measurement.
- **TPOT (time-per-output-token)**: budget of ~25 ms/token. A 300‑token answer therefore takes ~7.5 s of total decode time. p95 TPOT ≤ 35 ms/token provides headroom for batching variability.

These budgets are [canonical scenario][ASSUMPTION] from typical enterprise Q&A user expectations (sub‑2‑second feel) and from the hardware's published prefill/decode rates. They are the latency constraints that every subsequent architectural decision must respect.

The economic dimension translates the token profile and traffic into a cost structure:

- **Infrastructure**: A single host with 8×H100 GPUs (640 GB total GPU memory, 140 GB model FP16 weights fit with room for KV cache). On-demand rental (operating) cost ≈ $20/hour ($2.50/GPU-hr), or ~$2,500/month reserved (illustrative committed-use discount; a ~83% discount on H100 on-demand is unusually deep and differs from the on-demand basis the canonical box uses) — the canonical box uses the on-demand basis.
- **Token pricing**: ~$1.20 per million input tokens and ~$2.00 per million output tokens as a mid-tier managed-API list price (2026 snapshot) — a provider's price for a hosted service, not a rate for the canonical H100 instance, which is priced separately at ~$20/hr on-demand (see the Infrastructure bullet). The frontier-class API used in the Ch 16 TCO comparison is priced higher (~$2/$8 per 1M) — a different provider tier, not the same line item.
- **Throughput per dollar**: At 10 rps average, the system processes ~92,000 input tokens/s + ~3,000 output tokens/s. **The dollar denominator must be the whole fleet that serves this rate, not one host.** At the ~6-host average the whole-fleet spend is ~$120/hour (≈ $0.0333 per second), so ~92,000 / 0.0333 ≈ **~2.76 million input tokens per $1 of fleet spend** and ~3,000 / 0.0333 ≈ **~90,000 output tokens per $1** — the **active-hours basis** (~$120/hour × ~173.33 active h/mo ≈ $20,800/mo, the hour count matching the 6.24M active-hours request volume); on the **wall-clock paid-occupancy basis** ($87,600/mo — the same fleet paid for all 730 h/mo — ÷ the same month's **~57.4B input tokens, an active-hours (173.33 h) volume**) the same volume gives **~655K input tokens per $1** — wall-clock spend over active-hours tokens, an hour-basis mismatch and not a 730-hour token rate. These are *modeled, whole-fleet* figures (before SLO/headroom/failover). These are NOT redundant measures of the same effective cost — they are what-the-customer-pays-the-cloud (basis A) vs what-the-host-cost-spread-across-tokens-costs (basis B). Quoting both without labelling the basis confuses readers into treating basis B as if it were basis A.

**Cost-basis reconciliation for the canonical workload — four figures, four bases, never cross-compared.** The chapter carries four per-request figures for this one workload. Read each on its own basis: **(a) ~$0.0116 per request** — mid-tier managed-API *list price*, 9,200 × $1.20/1M + 300 × $2.00/1M = $0.01104 + $0.0006; **(b) ~$0.0208 per request** — the *frontier-class* API list price (~$2/$8 per 1M), a different provider tier on the same API basis; **(c) ~$0.0033 per request** (≈$3.33 per 1K) — *owned fleet*, 6-host average at full utilization on the hour-matched active-hours basis ($120/hr × 173.33 h ≈ $20,800/mo ÷ 6.24M requests); **(d) ~$0.0140 per request** (≈$14.04 per 1K) — the *same* owned 6-host fleet paid for all 730 h/mo and divided by the same active-hours volume, i.e. the hour-basis artifact (730 ÷ 173.33 = 4.21), not a costlier design. **The comparison rule:** API basis against API basis only (a vs b — the ~1.8× tier gap), owned-fleet basis against owned-fleet basis only (c vs d — a duty-cycle sensitivity), never across. The canonical workload's **economic constraint** is written against the whole-fleet monthly spend on basis (c)'s form — ~$87.6K–$306.6K/mo at full utilization, ~$146K–$467K/mo on the 70% × 95% + N−1 policy fleet, single-region N−1 only — with the ~$3.33/1K (6-host average) and ~$49.13/1K (21-host peak) lines quoted only after naming the fleet; (a) is the figure to quote when the alternative under evaluation is a managed API, and it is the number the ~$0.0030 post-2K-input figure in §4.6 belongs to.

**A note on the canonical example's limits.** The input-heavy enterprise-RAG workload above is one shape, not the whole space. The same six-dimension framework characterizes LLM serving workloads; what changes is *which dimension dominates* and *which decision reverses*. The compact ledger below is a transfer aid, not five parallel case studies — for each shape it names the changed dominant constraint and the single decision that would flip relative to the canonical RAG case.

| Workload shape | Dominant constraint (vs canonical RAG) | Decision that reverses |
|---|---|---|
| Short-context, high-throughput chat | Decode bandwidth + batch occupancy, not KV residency | Batch size and continuous-batching policy dominate; KV quantization is a smaller lever |
| Long-context retrieval (32K–128K) | KV residency per request (linear in context) | Concurrency floor drops; FP8/GQA KV and P/D disaggregation become first-class, not optional |
| Output-heavy reasoning (long generation) | Decode wall-clock (TPOT × output length), not prefill | TTFT budget relaxes; decode throughput and TPOT SLO bind; prefill optimization is a smaller lever |
| Agentic tool use (multi-turn) | Per-turn context accumulation + orchestration overhead | Turn cap, prefix caching, and per-task cost (not per-token) drive sizing; see Ch 19 |
| Embedding-only retrieval (no generation) | Embedding throughput + vector-index locality, not KV | No decode, no KV cache; the bottleneck is the embedding model and the retrieval index, not the LLM serving stack |

The canonical RAG case is *input-heavy* (9,200 in / 300 out), so prefill compute and KV residency dominate. Each row above is a workload where a different dimension takes over — and the architecture that is right for the canonical case is not automatically right for it. This is why the book keeps the six dimensions separate rather than collapsing them into one "throughput" number.


## 4. Measurement

For this chapter, measurement is about **quantifying the six dimensions** so the workload can be communicated definitively and used to drive architecture decisions. The token-counting habits of § 1.4 apply unchanged (run the model's own tokenizer, split input from output, log the distribution not just the mean) — rather than restate them, the three things the *six-dimension* view adds are:

1. **Quantify each dimension with a number, not a label.** For the six dimensions (quality, traffic, token profile, latency, economic constraints, operational constraints), attach a concrete measured or derived figure. "RAG" is not a load; "1.2K prompt + 8K retrieved, 300 out, 40 rps peak, p95 TTFT ≤ 2 s" is.
2. **Tie the token split to the bottleneck it drives.** An input-heavy profile (like RAG's 30× ratio) means prefill compute and KV-capacity pressure dominate; an output-heavy profile would dominate decode. State which side dominates, because it decides where the money and the architecture effort go (Ch 8).
3. **Record the peak and the tail, not just the average.** A workload that averages 9,200 input tokens but has a long tail (e.g. 32K peak contexts) sizes the fleet very differently. Log percentiles (p90, p99) alongside the average and size against the peak.

These habits answer the book's recurring question, "what would I actually measure here?" — and they let the six dimensions be stated as numbers an architecture decision can be defended on.

## 5. Common Mistakes

- **Treating "5% concurrency" as the throughput**. Concurrency is a snapshot of simultaneous users; throughput depends on how long each request takes. 100 concurrent in-flight requests with a 200 s service time yield 0.5 rps, not 10 rps. The binding connection is through request duration (Little's Law). Catch this by always asking "how long is each request in system?" before quoting a concurrency-derived throughput.
- **Using the ~10 s planning round as Little's-law W.** The planning round (~10 s) is not the execution service time (W_exec ≈ 8.6 s); plugging ~10 s into L = λW gives occupancies of 100/400 — exactly the active-user population — and conflates people with requests. Use W_exec for occupancy (the tokenizer-side traps — token ≈ word, context window as free capacity — are § 1.5's; measure there, not re-derived here).
- **Ignoring the input–output asymmetry**. An input-heavy workload like RAG tends to increase *prefill compute* and *KV-capacity* pressure — it does not by itself make the system "memory-bandwidth-bound." In the canonical model, prefill is **compute-bound** under the analytical roofline, while long input raises **KV residency**, which constrains sustainable concurrency (a capacity constraint, distinct from bandwidth). An output-heavy workload is **decode-dominant** (bandwidth/runtime per generated token). Raising *aggregate* decode throughput (output tokens/s across the fleet, e.g. via more batching) on an input-heavy workload moves the needle negligibly — the binding constraints are KV residency and prefill, not output rate; but per-request decode rate and TPOT are reciprocals, so reducing TPOT *does* shorten service time and host count — the same lever read per request, not a different one. The real win is prefill reduction, prefix/KV reuse, or increasing effective carrying capacity.
- **Sizing KV and the fleet from the mean context and the average fleet.** The 9,200-token mean hides the tail (a 32 K context costs ~84 GB of KV per request, §4.7), and the ~6-host average fleet is not the ~21-host peak fleet — size against the tail context and the peak rate, not the mean.
- **Overlooking operational constraints**. Multi–region deployment adds replication cost; privacy requirements may force a more expensive on-prem embedding model. These are often the true binding constraints, not the per-token price.

## 6. Architecture Consequence

The six‑dimension characterization informs and constrains the architectural path for the canonical enterprise‑Q&A RAG workload:

Each arrow means the dimension *constrains/informs* the corresponding architectural choice — it is a heuristic influence, not a deterministic one-to-one mapping. The arrows are **non-exclusive**: in practice a dimension often influences several decisions at once (e.g. latency SLO shapes both model choice and serving configuration), and the six axes are a starting lens, not a partition. The figure draws **one representative influence per dimension** — it does not enumerate every influence it asserts (the latency SLO also shapes serving configuration; the token profile also drives KV sizing and prefill demand). *The six workload dimensions and the architectural decision each one drives: Quality requirements → model size/type; Request rate & arrival pattern → concurrency & batching; Token profile → KV cache size & prefill demand; Latency SLO → TTFT/TPOT & batch window; Economic constraints → host count & cost ceiling; Operational constraints → multi-region vs. single-region.*


- **Model selection**: A 70B FP16 dense model (140 GB weights) fits on a single host with 8×H100 (640 GB GPU memory). The model is large enough to follow instructions and synthesize retrieved context without task‑specific fine‑tuning (factual accuracy still depends on retrieval quality, context, and evaluation), but the 140 GB footprint means KV cache for a 9,200-token context adds ~24.1 GB of GPU memory per request (24.9 GB on the resident slot-counting basis), leaving headroom but not abundance.
- **Serving configuration**: One host is the per-request latency baseline, not the capacity answer: at 10 rps the offered prefill load is ~92,000 tok/s against one host's ~19,800 tok/s sustained-planning rate, so prefill alone screens at ≥5 hosts at average (92,000 ÷ 19,800 = 4.65, ceiled — an *analytical bound on a planning rate, not a capacity result*); the KV-residency screen (86 in-flight ÷ 17 slots = 5.06) is the binding bound and raises this to ~6 hosts (§4.3). On each host, continuous batching (e.g. vLLM) is nearly mandatory under this load to hold the 1.2 s TTFT budget: it reduces queueing and improves utilization under concurrency (it does not shorten a single request's 9,200-token prefill); without admission scheduling, queued prefills can push p95 TTFT past the **p95 TTFT ceiling (≤ 2 s)** — the tail ceiling, not the 1.2 s design budget — chunked prefill and priority scheduling are the levers that address prefill/decode interference directly. The input‑heavy token profile (30× more input than output) makes continuous batching especially effective: with long prefills and short outputs, decode slots would otherwise sit idle between admissions, and continuous batching keeps them filled. The benefit of many requests sharing the same retrieved-context prefix belongs to prefix caching — a distinct mechanism (§ 2.1, developed in Ch 11) — not to continuous batching.
- **Memory planning**: KV cache for a 9,200-token context on the canonical model — a 70B **full-MHA reference model** (every query head has its own K,V, so K/V width = hidden_dim = 8192) — is 2 × layers × hidden × bytes = 2,621,440 B ≈ 2.62 MB/token (Chapter 7), giving ≈9,200 ×2.62 MB ≈ 24.1 GB of initial KV per request; a *resident* request also holds its 300 output tokens, so the slot-counting basis is 9,500 ×2.62 MB ≈ 24.9 GB. This is the MHA upper-bound baseline; a GQA model (8 KV-heads, d_head=128) would carry only ~0.33 MB/token, ~8× less (Ch 7). At 10 concurrent full-context requests the MHA figure is ~241 GB of KV on top of the 140 GB weights — 381 GB of the 640 GB pool — and the pool presses well before concurrency reaches 100 (~17 full-context requests once the ~64 GB runtime is reserved: 436 GB ÷ 24.9 GB ≈ 17.5, floored to 17 — the input-only 24.1 GB figure above would give 436 ÷ 24.1 ≈ 18 before the output tokens are counted). That 17-slot ceiling is a **per-host** figure, and its binding limit is **per-rank**: an 80 GB rank runs 99.97% full at 17.5 slots (Ch 7, Technical note — per-rank fit: 17.5 GB weights + 54.48 GB KV + 8 GB runtime/NCCL = 79.98 GB of 80 GB), so the per-rank test — not this pool-level arithmetic — decides residency. Read the 381 GB-of-640 GB sentence as a pool-level illustration, not as the headroom test. The architecture must therefore limit concurrency, quantize KV (FP8 → ~1.31 MB/token, ~12.06 GB/request at the same 9,200-token context), or use a GQA architecture. This is the same KV-residency discipline developed fully in Chapters 7 and 17.
- **Economic feasibility**: Two cost bases meet here and are not interchangeable: ~$20/hour per 8×H100 host (owned/self-host basis) and ~$0.012 per request at mid-tier API list prices (from $1.20/M input × 9,200 + $2.00/M output × 300 — an API-basis figure, not a self-host TCO). On the **API basis** the request cost is driven by the 9,200 input tokens: if the input token count could be reduced to 2 K (e.g. tighter retrieval/reranking to fewer chunks, context compression), or lower effective prefill cost for the same tokens (prefix caching, if priced favorably), the per-request *API list* cost falls from $0.01164 to ≈$0.0030 — the input leg goes from 9,200 × $1.20/1M = $0.01104 to 2,000 × $1.20/1M = $0.00240 while the output leg stays 300 × $2.00/1M = $0.0006, so the total moves $0.01164 → ≈$0.0030, a ~3.9× reduction ($0.01164 ÷ $0.0030 = 3.88). That is a hosted-API figure and says nothing directly about fleet capacity. On the **owned-fleet basis** the same input reduction lowers prefill work and the KV footprint per request, which changes the host-count screen: a 2,000-input + 300-output slot costs 2,300 × 2.62 MB ≈ 6.03 GB, so one host's 436 GB KV budget holds 436 ÷ 6.03 ≈ 72 slots instead of 17, and the KV screen falls to ⌈86 ÷ 72⌉ = 2 hosts at average and ⌈344 ÷ 72⌉ = 5 at peak; prefill screens at the same counts (10 × 2,000 ÷ 19,800 = 1.01 → 2, 40 × 2,000 ÷ 19,800 = 4.04 → 5). Those are *analytical screens on the planning basis, not capacity results* — Ch 17 recomputes them. Compute the cost on the fleet $/hr ÷ fleet rps basis: (5 × $20/hr) ÷ (40 rps × 3,600 s/hr) ≈ $0.0007 per request at peak, against (21 × $20/hr) ÷ (40 rps × 3,600 s/hr) ≈ $0.0029 at 9.2K input — not from this $/request API number. This is the lever the architect pulls when TCO is the binding constraint.
- **Operational topology**: Multi‑region deployment (active‑active) provides region-failure tolerance at an illustrative ~2× infrastructure cost — a figure that is **UNPRICED / NOT VALIDATED** in this book's TCO/fleet arithmetic, not a priced line (see caveat below); 99.9% availability alone does not require it, since a single-region multi-AZ design can meet 99.9% depending on the failure domains. If the SLA requires region-failure tolerance, the architecture must absorb a separately modeled active-active cost — not necessarily 2×. If it is negotiable, a single-region with graceful-degrading fallback may suffice. The operational constraint thus directly sets the economic floor. **Caveat — this multi-region active-active topology and the `2× cost` here are UNPRICED / NOT VALIDATED in this book's TCO/fleet arithmetic (Ch 16–17), which prices a single-region N−1 deployment instead; multi-region active-active is a different provisioning problem (cross-region capacity duplication, region-failure tolerance, data locality and traffic routing, state/data replication) and must be costed separately before the 99.9% SLA is met — see the canonical-box availability-topology note.**

## 7. What We Still Don't Know

- **Tokenizer exactness for technical domains**: While the "one token ≈ 4 characters" rule of thumb is convenient, enterprise Q&A prompts may contain code, JSON, or domain‑specific terminology that tokenizes differently. The exact per‑token count for a given prompt distribution is [UNRESOLVED] and would require running the model's tokenizer on a representative sample of real user queries.
- **KV cache scaling with longer contexts**: The canonical scenario uses ~9,200 input tokens. If retrieval quality degrades and context lengths grow to 32 K or 128 K, the KV cache memory per request grows linearly. **Be precise about what breaks.** A single 32 K canonical request costs ~84 GB of KV, which — with the ~140 GB weights and ~64 GB runtime/workspace [canonical scenario][ASSUMPTION] (the runtime/NCCL/activation reserve assumed in Ch 7, which subtracts it to reach the ~436 GB KV budget) — still fits the nominal 640 GB aggregate host *as a single request* (~288 GB total, well under 640 GB). What fails is not one-request fit but a particular **concurrency/SLO requirement**: at the canonical ~40 rps peak and ~8.6 s service time, the in-flight occupancy (~344 requests) demands far more than one host's aggregate KV budget once per-request KV grows to 32 K or 128 K (at 128 K a single request alone needs ~335 GB of KV, leaving little for any concurrency). So state the exact breakpoint in terms of the **maximum concurrent requests and the per-rank/aggregate feasibility**, not as "a 32 K or 128 K context no longer fits." The specific breakpoint is workload‑ and SLO‑dependent and must be checked per‑rank (Chapters 17, 20), not asserted as a universal constant.
- **Impact of reranking on latency**: The TTFT budget assumes retrieval (~120 ms) plus prefill. If a late‑stage reranker is added to the pipeline, the retrieval component's latency may increase, eating into the TTFT budget and potentially requiring a wider SLO margin or a faster retriever. The magnitude of this effect is [UNRESOLVED] and should be measured before production deployment.
- **Economic durability at scale**: The tokens‑per‑dollar numbers are derived from 2026 on‑demand H100 pricing. Spot instances, reserved contracts, or custom cloud agreements could shift the economics significantly. The durability of the economic model across provider changes is [UNRESOLVED].

## 8. End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: A Six-Dimension Workload Characterization

An architect is brought into the early design of an internal Q&A platform. The stakeholder says: "We have thousands of employees who want to ask questions over our internal documents. We need it to be accurate and fast, but we don't know how many thousands or how fast is fast enough." Before any architecture can be defended, the architect does the workload characterization that this chapter walks through.

**Label note for this mini-case.** This 5,000-employee growth variant is non-canonical. Its own inputs (5,000 employees, 10% peak activity, 50 rps) carry [ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]. Every host count derived from them (26 / 38 / 39 / 40) carries [ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED]. The ~8.6 s engine response duration is not a variant input: the variant inherits it unchanged from the canonical scenario ($W_{\mathrm{exec}}$ = prefill 1.08 s + decode 7.5 s ≈ 8.58 s, Table 4-2, [canonical scenario][DERIVED]), and it keeps that label here. The canonical-scenario tags in §4.1–§4.7 do not transfer to the rest of the variant.

Using the canonical scenario as a starting point — ~2,000 registered users, ~5% concurrency, ~10 rps, ~9,200 input + 300 output tokens — the architect projects the enterprise's actual headcount. If the company has 5,000 employees and expects 10% concurrent activity during peak Q&A periods (after a policy rollout), that gives **500 active people** — a *population* measure, **not** 500 requests currently occupying the inference service. An arrival rate requires its own traffic assumption or observation. Suppose user research and demand modeling independently set the busy-period peak to **50 requests/s** (a declared scenario input at this growth scale, not a figure derived from the 500-user count — Little's Law cannot convert an active-user population into an arrival rate without knowing in-flight request occupancy). With the illustrative ~8.6 s engine response duration, Little's Law implies approximately 50 × 8.6 ≈ **430 requests in the system** across the fleet at that rate — distinct from the 500 active people. It is the *arrival rate*, not the user count, that sizes the fleet. Using the canonical per-host bound from § 7.3 (the whole-slot floor of 17 KV-resident requests) and Ch 20 (17 slots ÷ 8.6 s = 1.977 rps — a single 8×H100 host serves ~2.0 rps at full modeled utilization — C ≈ 17.5 (= 436 GB ÷ 24.9 GB, the 9.5K-token resident basis of §4.6) is the analytical quotient, floored to 17 deployable whole-context slots per the book's whole-slot convention; applied in Ch 17), the fleet rounds up by the same whole-slot ceiling the canonical 40 rps case uses (⌈344/17⌉ = 21):

$$
n_\text{hosts} = \left\lceil \frac{50 \text{ rps} \times 8.6 \text{ s}}{17 \text{ slots}} \right\rceil = \lceil 430/17 \rceil = \lceil 25.29 \rceil = 26 \text{ hosts} \quad (\text{full utilization})
$$

and on the u-only screen at a 70% target utilization the **normative host-count form** of Ch 17 Table 17-1 gives ⌈26 ÷ 0.70⌉ = ⌈37.14⌉ = **38 hosts** (the occupancy cross-check ⌈430/(17 × 0.7)⌉ = ⌈430/11.9⌉ = ⌈36.13⌉ = 37 sits one host below it) — then **39 hosts** before the spare, **40 under the book's provisioning policy**, which multiplies the independent ρ = 0.95 as well and adds the N−1 reserve (⌈430/(17 × 0.70 × 0.95)⌉ = ⌈430/11.305⌉ = ⌈38.04⌉ = 39; Ch 17 Table 17-1 adds +1 for the N−1 spare → 40). **The same total by the host-count form:** with N full-utilization hosts, total (provisioned) = ⌈N ÷ (u × ρ)⌉ = ⌈26 ÷ 0.665⌉ and working = total − 1 — here N = ⌈430/17⌉ = 26, so ⌈26 ÷ 0.665⌉ = ⌈39.10⌉ = 40 provisioned, 39 working. **Ceiling placement and the normative form.** The single normative rule is the host-count form (stated once in Ch 17 Table 17-1): ceil the load to whole full-utilization hosts first (N = ⌈load ÷ C⌉), then total provisioned = ⌈N ÷ (u × ρ)⌉ and working = total − 1. The occupancy form — ⌈load ÷ (C × u × ρ)⌉ — is a **cross-check**, not the normative rule, and it may differ by one host because it divides the un-ceiled load before the ceiling. Where the screens disagree, the fleet is the **maximum of all screens** — KV-residency/service-time, prefill-throughput, and policy fleet: the binding constraint wins — and the text states which form produced the quoted number. Here the cross-check reproduces the normative result at the policy stage (40 provisioned / 39 working); the intermediate one-host delta is the expected rounding artifact of the cross-check, not a disagreement about policy. A prefill-throughput cross-check from the Chapter 8 model bounds it from below: 50 rps × 9,200 input = 460,000 prefill tokens/s ÷ ~19,800 tokens/s per host (the host's *sustained-planning* prefill rate, 8 × ~2,472 tok/s at 35% MFU — 8 × 0.989 PFLOPS × 0.35 ÷ 140 GFLOP/token = 19,780 tok/s per host, i.e. 2,472.5 per GPU, which Ch 8 Table 8-1 prints as ~2,472 — a planning rate, not a ceiling: the peak-derived ceiling is ~56.5K tok/s; not a measured goodput) = **24 hosts** (460,000 ÷ 19,800 = 23.23, ceiled to a whole host). (Do not divide by the ~8.5K tok/s single-request prefill rate, 9,200 ÷ 1.08 s — that is the rate for one request monopolizing a host, not a per-host aggregate, and using it here would be a unit error.) So prefill-throughput is a *lower* bound on host count; the binding figure is the KV-residency/service-time ~26 (full util) / ~38 (u-only 70%, normative host-count form) / ~39 before the spare, 40 under the 70% × 95% provisioning policy (Table 17-1, +1 N−1 spare) result. The two do not "agree" — the KV-bound dominates, and the prefill model shows why adding hosts for prefill alone is the wrong lever at this scale; both are *analytical* bounds that must be confirmed by benchmark goodput (Chapter 14). Note this mini-case is a 5,000-employee growth scenario at 50 rps, distinct from the canonical 40 rps peak (which sizes to ~21 hosts full-util / ~31 working at the 70% × 95% policy (32 with N−1), Chapters 8/16/17/20/25); the two are separate cases, not a contradiction.

At the canonical instance rate (~$20/hr per 8×H100 host), the canonical 6–21-host fleet on the owned/self-host basis is roughly **$87.6K–$306.6K/month** (~$14.04–$49.13 per 1K requests across the 6-host and 21-host full-utilization fleets; the derivation is the section 3 sidebar table — at the 40 rps peak volume of 24.96M requests/month the 21-host figure is ≈$12.28/1K — and the paid-occupancy hours are an explicit illustrative input, not a measured duty cycle) depending on purchase mode and duty cycle. Chapter 16 develops the TCO properly. The architect can now speak the system's currency — tokens, rps, dollars per million — and can engage the rest of the team on model selection, serving configuration, and TCO with concrete numbers rather than vague assurances.

The specific sizing — turning "thousands of employees" into a characterized workload across all six dimensions — is the payoff of Ch. 4's framework. Each dimension carries an arithmetic (quality → accuracy target and its validation; traffic → offered load and occupancy; token profile → KV and prefill demand; latency → service time and SLO; economics → $/token and $/request; operational → availability topology, data locality, refresh cadence) that the later chapters plug specific numbers into. Until a workload has *numbers* on all six axes, any architecture chosen against it is being chosen against a category, not a workload.
