# Chapter 12 — Designing the AI System: From Constraints to Candidates


Designing an AI system is constraint-driven generation, not menu-picking: the architect extracts the hard constraints from a characterized workload — the three gates this chapter fixes: memory fit, throughput at the SLO, and quality — then synthesizes candidate architectures that must clear them. The payoff is a bounded set of two to three candidates, each designed to probe a different aspect of the constraint set — single-host throughput, phase-split throughput, or quality-vs-size (memory fit is screened for all three) — which the measuring chapters then adjudicate.

## The Architect's Question
Designing an AI system means producing candidate architectures from the substrate — memory, compute, communication, and parallelism — rather than selecting a canned stack. This chapter works through a synthesis method: given a characterized workload from the six-dimension framework (Ch 4), generate two or three candidate architectures that each satisfy the hard constraints — the three gates fixed here: memory fit, throughput at the SLO, and quality — then carry them forward for evaluation in Chs 14–16. The core thesis is that design is constraint-driven generation, not menu-picking. This distinguishes our approach from the abstract meta-method presented in Ch 22, which operates at a level of generality that does not instantiate against concrete substrate constraints. In the language of the book's spine — the decision loop *constrain → synthesize → measure → decide → refine* — this chapter is the **synthesize** step: we turn a constraint-characterized workload into a bounded set of candidate architectures that then enter the measure/decide stages in Chs 14–16.

## 1. Concept
The architect's first step is to reverse-engineer the workload into its constituent constraints. From the six dimensions — quality requirements, traffic profile, token profile, latency budget, economic ceiling, and operational constraints — we extract the three hard constraints — the three gates used throughout this chapter — that any candidate must satisfy: (1) **memory fit** — the weight memory plus the KV cache footprint must fit the target host's aggregate memory; (2) **throughput at the SLO** — sustained prefill and decode tokens-per-second, delivered within the latency budget (prefill + decode), must meet the workload's demand; and (3) **quality** — answer quality on the true evaluation set must meet the quality requirement. These gates are not Ch 11's qualification checks: the qualification checks decide whether a configuration may be measured at all, whereas the three gates decide whether a measured configuration may be advanced. Gate status is a three-value vocabulary — *pass*, *fail* (measured, below requirement), *open* (not yet measured) — and candidates carry one of four mutually exclusive statuses. A *finalist* has passed all three gates and is advanced to evaluation. A *conditional finalist* is advanced to evaluation with a named open gate: it is measured there, not promoted on faith. A *sensitivity case* is retained only to bound a parameter (e.g. a quality-degraded variant) and is never a deployment option. An *eliminated* candidate fails a gate in every layout we can synthesize and returns to synthesis. Any architecture that fails a gate in every synthesizable layout is eliminated from the finalist set — not "before evaluation", since the quality gate is knowable only by evaluation. A layout whose memory fits but whose throughput falls short of demand — as a single host falls against the canonical ~92K input tok/s — is therefore not advanced as-is: it returns to synthesis for a larger layout (the replicated host class is the next candidate generated), and a smaller-model candidate whose quality is unmeasured advances as a conditional finalist whose named open gate is quality. (Two of the six dimensions are dispositioned rather than gated: the economic ceiling is recorded but is not one of the three gates — §12.8 states its consequence at fleet scale — and operational constraints are screened before synthesis, so a candidate that violates a hard operational constraint such as data residency, a required deployment region, or an existing-inventory requirement is eliminated regardless of its gate results. Of the other four, quality requirements feed the quality gate, traffic and token profiles feed the throughput gate, the latency budget feeds the throughput-at-the-SLO gate, and the token profile together with the model's precision and context length sets the memory gate.) This constraint-first posture inverts the typical practice of browsing vendor offerings and then hoping they fit; instead, we generate architectures that we know can physically reside and operate within the specified substrate.

## 2. Mental Model
The substrate is a set of bounded resources, and the architecture design problem is essentially a packing exercise. The resources are: GPU memory (weight residency + KV cache), compute (prefill throughput — prefill is compute-bound, Ch 11, scoped to the canonical architecture × batch × precision × kernel operating point), memory bandwidth (decode throughput — decode is bandwidth-bound, scoped to the canonical architecture × batch × precision × kernel operating point), inter-GPU communication volume and latency (for parallelism schemes — bandwidth, message-size-dependent latency, and the topology those collectives must run on, since the slowest device or weakest link sets the step time, Ch 11), host-level resources (CPU, host memory, PCIe, storage — deferred here because all three candidates are GPU-host-local; they enter at fleet scale, Ch 13/17), and the latency budget split between prefill and decode (a constraint on the packing, not a capacity). A candidate architecture is a valid packing of the workload's weight memory and KV cache into the available GPU memory, paired with a parallelism and caching strategy that delivers the required tokens-per-second throughput within the latency budget. The architect alternates between squeezing the residency footprint (e.g., via KV quantization or precision reduction) and adjusting the parallelism layout (e.g., tensor vs. data vs. pipeline, TP/DP/PP) until all three hard constraints are simultaneously satisfiable. This mental model — substrate as packing problem — is what enables systematic candidate generation rather than ad hoc selection.

## 3. Worked Example
We generate three candidate architectures for the canonical enterprise-Q&A RAG workload: ~10 requests/s average, peak 40 rps, 70B FP16 model, 9.2K token input (1,200 prompt + 8K retrieved context), 300-token output. The canonical weight memory is 140 GB (70B × 2 bytes FP16). The KV cache for 9.2K context at FP16 precision ([ASSUMPTION] canonical (Ch 4, Table 4-2)) is approximately 24 GB (2 × num_layers × hidden_dim × 9,200 × 2 bytes per FP16 element), giving a single-request residency of 164 GB. A single 8×H100 host provides 640 GB aggregate memory (8 ×80 GB), which the chapter's budget splits as 140 GB weights + up to 436 GB of KV + ~64 GB runtime (Ch 7/17). One request fits with ample headroom — but one request is not the workload's KV population, and the memory gate below is stated on that second basis.

*Two memory checks, not one.* *Single-request residency* asks whether one request's weights plus KV fit a host: 140 GB + 24 GB = 164 GB < 640 GB, which passes here. *Workload KV residency* asks whether the fleet holds the KV of every request in flight, which Little's Law sets from arrival rate × service time: at the canonical 10 rps average and 8.6 s service time that is ~86 in-flight requests, i.e. 86 × 24 GB ≈ 2,064 GB of KV — ~4.7 hosts' worth of the 436 GB per-host KV budget, so the ~6-host KV/service-time screen — and at the 40 rps peak, ~344 in-flight requests, i.e. ~8,256 GB of KV and ~21 hosts (40 rps × 8.6 s ÷ 17 slots). For candidate (c), 86 × ~1.2 GB ≈ 103 GB of KV exceeds one card's ~58 GB KV capacity, so it needs ~2 cards at average concurrency and ~7 at peak. The memory gate is the workload check: a "fits memory" claim in this chapter means single-request residency unless a host or card count is given.

Each candidate satisfies the three gates to a different degree.

**Candidate (a): single 8×H100 host with prefix caching (Ch 11).**  This is the per-request-latency baseline — the single 8×H100 host that the early book (Ch 2, 4, 7, 8) establishes as meeting one request's latency SLO. It is *not* a capacity baseline: a lone host cannot carry the arrival rate (~6 hosts are needed for the canonical average, and ~21 at peak — see Ch 11, 13, 17), so its throughput-gate failure below is expected and is *not* a reason to dismiss the host class itself.
All 70B weights and the full 24 GB KV cache reside on the 8-GPU host. Prefix caching from Ch 11 amortizes prefill computation for shared prefixes (attention and MLP work) over repeated requests with the same prompt prefix, reducing effective prefill FLOPs by ~30% at 10 rps average and ~50% at peak 40 rps when many requests share common document roots. (That 30–50% amortization range is a reasoned expectation, not a measured figure — it rests on the untested premise about traffic overlap flagged in §12.7, so it is labeled **[HYPOTHESIS]** and should be confirmed by measuring the actual prefix-hit rate before it is relied on.) The 8×H100 host fits the full 164 GB single-request residency easily, but on *throughput* we must be honest with the Chapter 8 arithmetic: a single 8×H100 host sustains only ~19,800 prefill tok/s at the idealized 35% MFU operating point under TP=8: each GPU carries ~19,780 tok/s of the shared token stream (989 TFLOP/s × 35% ÷ 17.5 GFLOP/token per GPU), so the host aggregate is the same ~19,800 — not 8× a per-GPU rate (Ch 8 scope note); the ~2,472 figure is one GPU's share of the 70B's full-model FLOPs, i.e. a full-model-equivalent rate, never a per-GPU capability. (The 17.5 and 14 GFLOP/token figures are the linear 2N term only, so every rate in this chapter is a lower bound: at the 9.2K context attention adds ~4 × n_layers × L × d_model ≈ 24.1 GFLOP/token full-model for the 70B, ~3.0 per GPU under TP=8, giving ~20.5 GFLOP/token per GPU and ~16.9K tok/s per host, and ~18.8 GFLOP/token for the 7B, giving ~18.4K tok/s per card. On that attention-inclusive basis the prefill screens move to ~6 hosts at average and ~22 at peak for (a)/(b) and ~5 average / ~20 peak cards for (c) — within a host of the KV/service-time screen at peak, and changing no verdict.) That is far below the ~92K input tok/s the workload needs on average. Even if prefix caching (~30% at average load) lifts effective prefill throughput toward ~28K tok/s **[HYPOTHESIS]**, that is still well below 92K, so a single host fails the throughput gate too — prefill is compute-bound at this operating point, and meeting it is exactly the scaling problem Chapters 15–17 take up. Memory fit and throughput fit are different gates, and memory fit itself is two checks: the *single-request residency* check passes with one host (140 GB weights + 24 GB KV = 164 GB < 640 GB aggregate, [ASSUMPTION] canonical (Ch 4, Table 4-2)), while the *workload KV residency* check — the gate — does not: ~86 in-flight requests × 24 GB ≈ 2,064 GB of KV is ~4.7 hosts of the 436 GB per-host budget at average and ~8,256 GB, ~21 hosts, at peak. Candidate (a) is therefore memory-feasible only as a multi-host deployment, not as one host. Throughput at the SLO: per-request prefill + decode are expected to stay within the latency budget at the admitted load thanks to prefix caching and the high bandwidth of 8×H100 — the SLO gate itself is measured in Ch 14 — but the sustained rate fails at one host, as shown above: the *idealized prefill capacity screen* reaches ~5 hosts at average (92K ÷ 19.8K ≈ 4.65 → 5), which bounds idealized goodput only, while the *KV/service-time screen* gives ~6 hosts at average (10 × 8.6 ÷ 17 ≈ 5.06 → 6) and ~21 at peak (40 × 8.6 ÷ 17 ≈ 20.2 → 21) (Chs 15–17). The fleet is the larger of the two screens — fleet = max(prefill screen, KV/service-time screen) — so the KV/service-time screen binds at both average and peak. Quality: the 70B is the workload's quality reference, so candidate (a) inherits the reference quality by definition rather than by measurement — the gate is satisfied relative to the reference, and the absolute requirement still needs the Ch 14 capability screen on the true evaluation set. Economics (recorded, not one of the three gates — §12.8): one host purchase, one software license tier.

**Candidate (b): P/D-disaggregated two-pool.**  
We keep the full 70B model in both pools and split by *phase* — which is what prefill/decode (P/D) disaggregation actually means (Chapter 11): a compute-bound prefill pool and a bandwidth-bound decode pool, each holding the complete 140 GB weights plus the in-flight KV, with the KV cache (K/V tensors) handed between them (Splitwise / DistServe / Mooncake [S4][1P]). Each pool therefore needs its own 8×H100 host, but the two pools' KV populations differ and must not be collapsed into one arithmetic. The *decode* pool holds the KV of every in-flight decode, so at the canonical per-host KV budget (640 − 140 weights − ~64 runtime ≈ 436 GB, Ch 7/17) it runs at 140 GB weights + up to 436 GB of resident decode KV + ~64 GB runtime = 640 GB — exactly the pool, **zero headroom**; co-residency beyond that budget requires reducing the KV allocation or adding memory. The *prefill* pool holds KV only transiently, for requests being prefilled and then handed off, so its working set is bounded by concurrent prefills (40 rps × ~1.1 s ≈ 44 requests, ~1.1 TB spread across ~19 hosts, i.e. tens of GB per host), not by the decode population, which leaves most of the 436 GB as headroom. One request's 24 GB is not either pool's population. The win is that prefill and decode no longer fight over the same host's resources: the prefill pool runs compute-bound at the same 35% MFU basis as (a) on the 9.2K input, the decode pool runs *bandwidth-bound* on the 300 output tokens — bandwidth-bound in the roofline sense, not HBM-saturated: at the canonical 8×H100 TP=8 decode point the sharded weight read is ~0.70 TB/s per GPU against a 3.35 TB/s HBM ceiling, ~21–25% achieved HBM (Ch 6), and the 25 ms TPOT is a latency budget, not a saturation point — and KV crosses between them. That transfer is a sized quantity, not a detail: 24.9 GB per request at 10 rps average is ~249 GB/s, and at 40 rps peak ~996 GB/s, roughly twenty 400G ports on the KV-transfer fabric. On a 200 GB/s link the transfer alone costs ~125 ms of the 1.2 s TTFT budget, which is why the fabric is part of the architecture rather than an accessory.

The decode screen closes the half of the throughput gate that §12.4 asks for. At 17 in-flight slots per host and a 25 ms TPOT, one decode host delivers 17 ÷ 0.025 ≈ 680 tok/s (the 140 GB weight read at 3.35 TB/s = ~41.8 ms/token is amortized over the batch, ~2.5 ms/token at 17 slots, well inside the 25 ms budget), so the 3K tok/s average needs ~5 decode hosts and the 12K tok/s peak ~18. Both sit below the prefill pool's ~19 at peak, so **decode does not bind**; prefill does.

Crucially, P/D disaggregation does **not** raise the per-host prefill *ceiling* (it may raise achieved prefill by removing interference): one 8×H100 prefill host still sustains only ~19,800 prefill tok/s (the same Chapter 8 arithmetic as Candidate (a)), far short of the ~92K input tok/s the workload needs on average. So Candidate (b) passes the *single-request memory* check per pool but still fails the *throughput* gate at a single prefill host — its real advantage is that the prefill pool can be scaled **independently** of the decode pool, so meeting the ~92K demand means scaling the prefill pool to ~5 hosts (92K ÷ 19.8K ≈ 4.65), ~19 at peak before headroom, plus the ~5/~18 decode hosts, rather than forcing every host to serve both phases. Disaggregation buys the *ability to scale each phase separately*, not the throughput itself. The two-pool total is therefore ~19 prefill + ~18 decode ≈ **37 hosts at the canonical peak against the 21-host monolithic screen — ~1.8×, not 2×** — because the decode pool is sized by in-flight concurrency rather than by prefill FLOPs; the ratio moves with the workload's output-to-input balance (longer outputs push it past 2×, shorter outputs toward 1×). SLO: the split targets TTFT, since decode no longer queues behind prefill, but that gain is unmeasured at these pool sizes (Chs 14–17). Quality: both pools serve the same 70B weights, so (b) inherits (a)'s reference quality by construction; the absolute requirement still needs the Ch 14 capability screen. Economics: the two-pool baseline plus the KV-transfer fabric, and ~1.8× the monolithic host count at peak; it is the right architecture *at scale*, not a two-host answer to the canonical case.

**Candidate (c): KV-quantized smaller host.**  
We downgrade to a 7B-class model (7B × 2 bytes = 14 GB weights) with KV cache quantized to 4-bit. The per-token KV depends on the model's own attention shape (layers × KV-heads × head dim × bytes, Chapter 7), NOT the 70B's constant: a typical 32-layer, 4096-hidden, full-MHA 7B carries ~0.52 MB/token FP16, so ~0.13 MB/token at 4-bit → ~1.2 GB for the 9.2K context (vs the ~6 GB that comes from naively reusing the 70B's 2.62 MB/token ÷ 4). Total residency: 14 GB + ~1.2 GB ≈ **~15 GB**, which is a single-request fit on one H100 (80 GB) — but the workload's KV population is not: ~86 in-flight requests × ~1.2 GB ≈ 103 GB of KV exceeds one card's ~58 GB KV capacity, so the memory gate closes at ~2 cards at average concurrency and ~7 at peak. The trade-off is a quality question, and here we must be careful not to assert an architectural theorem where one does not exist. Whether a 7B-class model can match the 70B model's answer quality on *this* workload is **not** derivable from parameter count — a newer or task-specialized 7B can beat an older or general 70B on some tasks. It is an empirical question answered only by running the actual evaluation set. The correct architectural lesson is the **quality gate**: the smaller candidate must pass the capability/quality gate on the true evaluation set; if it fails there, no amount of infrastructure optimization fixes that failure. So candidate (c) is flagged for measurement against the quality gate, not dismissed by parameter counting. The 4-bit KV cache is introduced as a *memory* optimization, not as a quality improvement; it can nonetheless add its own quality penalty, which must be validated on the target workload (§12.5, §12.7). Throughput is genuinely much higher on the smaller model — a 7B prefill costs only ~14 GFLOP/token, so a single card sustains ~24,700 prefill tok/s (~24,725 from the stated inputs: 989 TFLOP/s × 35% MFU ÷ 14 GFLOP/token; vs ~19,780 for the 70B under TP=8 — the 8×H100 host *aggregate*, not a per-GPU rate, Chapter 8) — but that throughput advantage is only useful if the quality gate is first satisfied. The candidate is therefore provisional: its fate depends on the measured quality gate, which Chapter 14 (the capability screen) administers. This candidate varies **two** factors at once — model class (70B → 7B) and KV precision (FP16 → 4-bit) — so a quality result here bounds their combined effect but cannot be attributed to KV quantization alone; the 70B 8-bit-KV case (~12.06 GB at the 9.2K context, §12.4) is the one that isolates precision from model class. The memory footprint is trivially small and throughput is a genuine strength: 24,725 prefill tok/s per card against the 70B's ~19,780, which is the TP=8 *host aggregate* (8 × ~2,472), not a per-GPU rate — the per-GPU ratio against the 70B's full-model-equivalent single-card rate is 24,725 ÷ 2,472 ≈ 10×, and an 8-card 7B node would be ~10× an 8-card 70B node (197,800 ÷ 19,780). But candidate (c) is declared as **one card**, so on its own layout the advantage is ~1.25× per card (24,725 ÷ 19,780): ~4 cards at average load and ~15 at peak (Table 12-2), with a decode screen of ~2 cards average / ~7 at peak (~48 KV slots ÷ 25 ms ≈ 1,900 tok/s per card), leaving prefill as the binding constraint. What keeps it from being competitive is quality, the still-open Ch 14 gate.

*Memory/residency arithmetic summary.* The canonical weights (140 GB) + KV (24 GB FP16 at the 9.2K input context) = 164 GB is a single-request fit on one 640 GB host — and that is all it is: the workload's KV population is ~86 × 24 GB ≈ 2,064 GB at average (~6 hosts of the 436 GB per-host budget) and ~8,256 GB at peak (~21 hosts), so no single host carries the workload. Candidate (a) uses the full host for one request with room to spare and fails workload KV residency by ~5×. Candidate (b) keeps the full model in each pool; the *decode* pool's requirement is 140 GB weights + up to 436 GB of resident decode KV + ~64 GB runtime = 640 GB — zero headroom — while the *prefill* pool holds only a transient KV working set (tens of GB per host) and keeps most of the 436 GB as headroom. Candidate (c) is a ~15 GB single-request fit on one H100, needs ~2 cards at average concurrency / ~7 at peak for its KV population, and is **provisional** — its fate turns on the Ch 14 quality-gate measurement, not on parameter counting.

## 4. Measurement
To validate a candidate architecture, the architect must derive three numbers from first principles and verify them against the substrate:
1. **Weight memory.**

$$
W = N \times \text{bytes-per-param}
$$

where $N$ is the parameter count and bytes-per-param is 2 for FP16 (replace 2 with bit-width/8 for quantization). Derived fact [ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED] on the canonical inputs [ASSUMPTION] canonical (Ch 4, Table 4-2): $70 \times 10^9 \times 2 \text{ B} = 140$ GB.
2. **KV cache footprint.**

$$
KV = 2 \times n_\text{layers} \times n_{\text{KV-heads}} \times d_\text{kv} \times L \times \text{bytes-per-activation}
$$

where $L$ is the context length (full-MHA assumption: $n_{\text{KV-heads}} \times d_\text{kv} = d_\text{hidden}$; for GQA/MQA use the reduced KV-head count). For a 70B model with 8-bit KV caching, this is approximately 12.06 GB for 9.2K context (the raw tensor byte-halving bound: 2.62 MB/token FP16 ÷ 2 = 1.31 MB/token, exactly 50% of FP16; 9,200 × 1.31 MB ≈ 12.06 GB, Ch 7); at FP16 precision it is ~24.1 GB [ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED] on the canonical 9.2K context [ASSUMPTION] canonical (Ch 4, Table 4-2). The architect should compute this for the exact context length of the workload, not assume a fixed value.
3. **Throughput per device — prefill and decode** [ASSUMPTION] canonical (Ch 4, Table 4-2). Tokens-per-second that a single device, and the replica it sits in, can sustain for prefill and for decode, measured separately on the target hardware. The screen multiplies a per-*replica* rate by the number of independent replicas: device rate × devices is valid only for data-parallel replicas, because inside a tensor- or pipeline-parallel group every device advances the same token stream with a fraction of the FLOPs, so the group's aggregate rate equals the per-device rate — not N times it. Worked cross-check on the canonical layout: 989 TFLOP/s × 35% ÷ 17.5 GFLOP/token per GPU = 19,780 tok/s, and the 8×H100 TP=8 host aggregate is the same ~19,780 (≈19,800), never 158,240; that is why ~92K ÷ 19.8K ≈ 5 hosts is the average prefill screen and the KV/service-time screen (~6 at average, ~21 at peak) binds. On the decode side the per-replica rate is weights-read-bound — 140 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 41.8 ms/token unbatched, amortized over the in-flight batch (17 slots → ~2.5 ms/token, ~680 tok/s per host) — and is compared against the 3K/12K output-token demand. The demand to be exceeded (92K input tok/s average, 3K output tok/s average, 368K input tok/s peak, 12K output tok/s peak) is therefore met by replicas, not by piling devices into one group. Vendors publish peak numbers; the architect should treat these as benchmarks to be verified by measuring on equivalent hardware.

All three numbers must be confirmed before a candidate is carried forward, and each gate is recorded as *pass*, *fail*, or *open* in the same vocabulary as §12.1 and Table 12-1. A gate that is measured and fails at every layout we can synthesize returns the candidate to the synthesis step; a gate that is still open (unmeasured) does not — it advances to evaluation as a **conditional finalist** with that gate named first in the test plan. Neither outcome is a "carried forward failure": a candidate is never both failed on a gate and treated as a finalist in the same sentence.

## 5. Common Mistakes
- **Menu-picking without constraint validation.** Browsing vendor offerings and selecting the one that "feels right" rather than generating candidates against quantified hard constraints. This is the opposite of the constraint-driven method described here.
- **Ignoring the KV cache residency arithmetic.** Assuming that the weight memory is the only memory budget item. In input-heavy workloads ([ASSUMPTION] canonical (Ch 4, Table 4-2): 9.2K average context, 30× more input than output), per request the KV is ≈15% of residency at the canonical 9.2K context (24.1/164.1), but at the canonical host concurrency the KV budget is 436 GB of 640 GB (~68%) — which is why KV is often the binding constraint.
- **Over-optimizing prefix caching.** Assuming that prefix caching can amortize prefill across all requests. In practice, cache effectiveness depends on the overlap of prompt prefixes across the traffic workload; sparse prefix sharing reduces the realized savings.
- **Using KV-quantization as a free lunch.** Quantizing the KV cache reduces memory footprint but introduces quality degradation that may be unacceptable for the target workload. The architect must measure the quality impact before committing to a quantized KV layout.
- **Forgetting the latency split.** Treating the latency budget as a single number rather than a prefill/decode split. A candidate may meet the total latency budget by trading prefill for decode or vice versa, which may not satisfy the service-level objectives for either leg.

## 6. Architecture Consequence
All three candidates carry forward into the evaluation chapters (Chs 14–16) as *conditional finalists* — the §12.1 definition: advanced to evaluation with a named open gate, not promoted as passed — each with a distinct test plan: (a) and (b) carry the throughput gate open (and the TTFT SLO unmeasured), (c) carries the quality gate open, and no candidate is excluded pre-measurement. If (c) fails its measured quality gate it is retained only as a labeled sensitivity case; if (a) or (b) fails throughput at every layout we can synthesize, it returns to synthesis rather than being advanced as though nothing failed. All test plans are written against the same three gates — memory fit, throughput at the SLO, and quality. Candidate (a), the single 8×H100 host with prefix caching, is the baseline: we evaluate throughput at the SLO (TTFT/TPOT compliance under load) on a single hardware configuration, with cost-per-token recorded as an economic by-product rather than a gate. Candidate (b), the P/D-disaggregated two-pool, is evaluated on the cost of the cross-pool KV transfer (§12.3 sizes it at ~249 GB/s average / ~996 GB/s peak) and on the pipeline-overlap efficacy; we measure whether the latency budget is maintained under the partitioned layout. Candidate (c), the KV-quantized 7B model, is evaluated on the quality-degradation trade-off; we compare answer quality against the 70B baseline and compute the economic break-even — the host count at which (c)'s lower infrastructure cost offsets its measured quality loss. The synthesis method produces these candidates as a set from which the evaluation chapter selects based on quantitative results, not on gut feeling.

## 7. What We Still Don't Know
As of 2026-08, several questions remain open, each flagged **[HYPOTHESIS]**:

- **KV-quantization quality impact at 4-bit for enterprise Q&A.** The quality loss from 4-bit KV quantization has not been systematically characterized across the diversity of enterprise prompts in the canonical workload **[HYPOTHESIS]**.

- **Prefix-caching effectiveness under realistic traffic overlap.** The assumed 30–50% prefill amortization rests on the premise that many requests share document-level prefixes. Empirical measurement of prefix overlap across a production enterprise traffic trace is needed to confirm these numbers **[HYPOTHESIS]**.

- **P/D-disaggregation KV-transfer latency at 70B scale.** The *volume* is determinate: 24.9 GB per request, ~249 GB/s at 10 rps average and ~996 GB/s at 40 rps peak (~20 × 400G ports), ~125 ms of TTFT on a 200 GB/s link (§12.3). What is unexplored is the *achieved* rate and latency at 70B once routing is included — layer-wise streaming, KV layout, and overlap with prefill all move the number, so the delivered fraction of that fabric bandwidth depends on the routing library and network topology rather than on the arithmetic **[HYPOTHESIS]**.

- **Whether 8-bit KV caching is sufficient for 9.2K context at FP16-equivalent quality.** The arithmetic in §12.4 assumes only the 2× memory reduction; whether 8-bit KV preserves acceptable quality is the open question — the interaction between 8-bit quantization and the retrieval-augmented generation pipeline has not been verified **[HYPOTHESIS]**.

These flags exist because home-lab measurements do not count as reference evidence per the evidence taxonomy; each is an open empirical question a team should validate against its own target workload, not a claim the handbook has settled.

## 8. End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: From Vague Ask to Candidate Architectures
An architect is brought into a design conversation for a company-wide internal Q&A system. The stakeholder states: "We need an AI system that can answer employee questions over our internal documentation, with acceptable quality and within budget." Before any architecture can be defended, the architect applies the constraint-driven method from this chapter.

First, the workload is characterized across the six dimensions. Quality requirements fix the model class: the system must achieve ~70B-model answer quality on enterprise prompts. The traffic profile is the canonical one (Ch 4/5, canonical scenario (Ch 4, Table 4-2)): ~2,000 registered users, ~5% concurrent (~100), ~10 rps average / ~40 rps peak. The token profile is the canonical 1,200-token prompt plus 8K retrieved context (~9.2K input) plus 300-token answers, with TTFT ≤ 1.2 s (p95 ≤ 2 s) and TPOT ~25 ms/token. The stakeholder's $15,000 is a total monthly system budget — the chapter notes it equals one host's cloud-equivalent (one 8×H100 at ~$20/hr × 730 hr ≈ $14.6K/mo; an on-premise host has no $/hr, so this is a budget cap, not a metered cost, and the cloud-equivalent is a proxy at those assumptions). Total fleet cost $155.8K/mo (21 hosts) ÷ $15K = ~10.4× the total budget — the economic constraint fails at fleet scale (Ch 16). The consequence must be stated, not dissolved: the 21-host full-utilization screen fleet (Ch 16 case (i)/KV-screen line) costs ~$155.8K/mo — and the Ch 17 Table 17-1 provisioning policy (70% × 95% + N−1) yields 32 hosts at the same 40 rps peak (Ch 13), i.e. ~$232.2K self-host (32 × [per-host capex/60 + energy] + $10K/mo staff) and ~$467.2K cloud-equivalent (32 × 730 h × $20/h rental only, staff excluded), so the self-host totals do not scale purely per-host ($155,816.93, ≈$24.97/1K on the 6.24M‑req/mo business‑hours basis, Ch 16 — the cloud legs are $87,600 ≈ $14.04/1K at the default paid-occupancy input and $306,600 ≈ $49.13/1K at 21 hosts always-on) — ~10.4× the stakeholder's stated $15,000 total monthly budget for the 21-host screen (~15.5× self-host and ~31× cloud-equivalent for the 32-host policy fleet) — so **the economic constraint, as stated, fails at fleet scale**. On a per-host basis that is ~$7.4K/host/mo self-host ($155.8K ÷ 21) and ~$14.6K/host/mo cloud-equivalent — the basis §12.8 below uses to price candidate (c) per card (÷ 8: ~$928 and ~$1,825 per card). The operational constraint is that the system must run on existing on-premise GPU infrastructure (no cloud add-ons).

From the token profile, the architect computes: 9.2K input tokens + 300 output tokens ≈ 9.5K tokens per request. At 10 rps average and 40 rps peak, the system must sustain ~92K input tok/s average and ~368K input tok/s peak. Three hard constraints follow — the chapter's three gates, verbatim: **memory fit** (weight memory plus KV cache must fit within the on-premise GPU inventory), **throughput at the SLO** (total tokens-per-second across the installed devices, delivered within the TTFT/TPOT budget, must exceed the traffic demand), and **quality** (answer quality on the evaluation set must meet the ~70B requirement).

The architect generates three candidates using the method from §12.3. Candidate (a): a single 8×H100 host with 70B FP16 weights + 24 GB KV cache and prefix caching — the single-request residency check passes (164 GB < 640 GB), workload KV residency does not (§12.3), and one host's ~19.8K prefill tok/s is far short of the ~92K the 10 rps average demands, so memory passes and throughput opens. Candidate (b): a P/D-disaggregated two-pool design on separate 8×H100 hosts, which buys independent phase scaling at the cost of a second pool plus the KV-transfer fabric (§12.3 sizes the fabric and both pool counts). Candidate (c): a KV-quantized 7B model on single H100 cards — a ~15 GB single-request fit whose quality is *provisional*: whether a 7B-class model reaches the required answer quality is a hypothesis to be measured (Chapter 14), not a settled fact. (Exercise: recompute (a)'s per-host prefill rate as 989 TFLOP/s × 35% MFU ÷ 17.5 GFLOP/token, derive the ~5-host average and ~19-host peak screens from the 92K/368K tok/s demand, then repeat with the attention term included — §12.3 — and note which screen binds.)

The architect does **not** discard Candidate (c) by parameter counting alone: it is provisionally moved to the quality-gate evaluation (Chapter 14) to measure whether a 7B-class model actually meets the workload's capability/quality threshold on its evaluation set. Because that gate is still open **[HYPOTHESIS]**, (c) is a *conditional finalist* on the §12.1 definition — advanced for measurement, not excluded pre-measurement — and so are (a) and (b), whose open gate is throughput.

The near-term decision is then framed by the budget rather than by candidate count. On the same per-host basis used above, candidate (c)'s peak screen of ~15 single-H100 cards costs ~15 × $928 ≈ $13.9K/mo self-host (~$27.4K cloud-equivalent), and its ~4–5-card average screen ~$3.7–4.6K — the only one of the three that clears the $15,000 cap at peak on the self-host basis. Candidates (a) and (b) do not: ~21 hosts ≈ $155.8K/mo and ~37 hosts (19 prefill + 18 decode, §12.3) ≈ $274K/mo, ~10.4× and ~18× the budget. The binding constraint is therefore economic, not quality, so the quality gate on (c) is the one worth running first — (c) is the only candidate whose economics could clear the budget. If it fails that gate, the architect reports that no candidate in the synthesized set meets the $15,000 ceiling, and the next move is the return-to-synthesis set: renegotiate the budget, shape peak demand, or revisit the model class again.

Between (a) and (b) — both conditional finalists, both with throughput open — the decision hinges on whether (b)'s ~1.8× host count at peak (~37 vs ~21 hosts, §12.3; not 2×, because the decode pool is sized by in-flight concurrency rather than by prefill FLOPs) is justified by its operational flexibility: independent scaling of the prefill and decode pools, and fault isolation between them. The architect presents all three candidates, with the honest throughput arithmetic of Chapter 8 (no canonical single host meets the full prefill demand alone) and this cost comparison, as the decision foundation.

This matrix asks the harder question: what fleet does the canonical workload actually require at **peak**, and is the candidate a demonstrably viable deployment at that load? Host counts are an *illustrative capacity screen* from the fixed per-host prefill rates (~19,800 tok/s for the 70B candidates; ~24,700 tok/s for the 7B on the single H100 its layout declares — 989 TFLOP/s × 35% MFU ÷ 14 GFLOP/token; the ~197K figure is the 8-GPU node, 8 × 24,725 ≈ 197,800, not this layout) — **not recommended fleet counts**.
