# Chapter 16 — TCO: The Total Cost Reality Check

## The Architect's Question

After this chapter we should be able to answer the question every stakeholder actually asks — *what does this really cost?* — with a defensible number, not a guess. We will build a total-cost-of-ownership model that separates capital, operating, and opportunity costs, then price the canonical workload across three delivery modes: self-hosted on our own cards, cloud GPU instances, and a managed inference API. After this chapter, "we save money by self-hosting" is supported or refuted by arithmetic under the stated assumptions, with sensitivity analysis, and the break-even point between modes is explicit.

## 1. Concept

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) is the sum of everything required to deliver a capability over its lifetime, not just the price tag of the hardware or the per-token rate. It has three cost families:

1. **Capital (capex)** — the one-time cost of the cards, servers, networking, racks, and cooling that make up the system. For self-hosted inference this dominates up front: an 8×H100 server with networking and infrastructure carries a large price. [ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED]

2. **Operating (opex)** — the recurring cost of running it: electricity and cooling, staff, software/licenses, cloud instance rental, and replacement/amortization of hardware. For cloud and managed API this is the whole cost; for self-hosted it compounds on capex.

3. **Opportunity** — the cost of the *alternative not taken*: the team time spent operating vs building, the delay to market, and the engineering hours that do not scale with either card or token counts. This is the most omitted term and can be the largest in engineering-heavy orgs.

The architect's job is not to minimize capex, nor opex, nor provider bill — it is to minimize **the number that matters for the organization's decision**, which usually means total cost per useful request at the required quality and SLO.

## 2. Mental Model

TCO is **a utilization-weighted average over the whole system, not a sticker price.** Three mental moves keep the arithmetic honest:

- **Amortize, don't stare at the price tag.** A $400K server is an asset consumed over ~5 years; spread it into dollars-per-month, then into dollars-per-request, before comparing to a per-token API rate. [ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED]

- **Follow the token, not the GPU.** The thing being bought is completed, SLO-meeting requests. Two systems with the same card count can differ 10× in cost per request when 3× the goodput per card composes with ~3.4× from better utilization and no idle coasting.

- **Include what does not scale.** Staff, power, networking engineering, and the latency of procurement are sunk or recurring whether utilization is 20% or 80%. A low-cost-per-card system that burns engineers can be the most expensive.

The decision shape is a **break-even graph**: at low volume, managed/cloud-and-shut-down wins (no idle capex); at high volume, self-hosted cards amortize below the per-token rate. The crossover point is what we compute.

## 3. Worked Example

### Size the fleet first, then price it

TCO is meaningless until we know **how many hosts the workload actually requires**. A common sizing mistake is to start from a single host: a single 8×H100 host can only clear ~2.0 req/s under the KV-slot-based analytical UPPER bound on admitted request completion rate, conditional on an assumed mean KV residency time (Chapter 17/20), so it cannot serve even the canonical 10 rps average, let alone the 40 rps peak. We therefore size the fleet first (Ch 17) and price it second.

**Step 1 — fleet size.** The canonical workload's KV-residency / service-time screen is N_hosts = ⌈λ·W/C⌉ with the hard-slot floor 17 (436 GB ÷ (2.62 MB/token × 9,500 tokens) = 17.51 → floor 17 (conservative by one host: 344 ÷ 17.516 = 19.64 → 20 unrounded), applied BEFORE any utilization adjustment and BEFORE the host ceil; Ch 17) and W = 8.6 s (the 8.58 s execution sum 1.08 s prefill + 7.5 s decode, rounded) — the **assumed mean KV-residency time** E[W_KV-resident] (Ch 17's conditional scenario proxy, with KV assumed held only during execution; *W_plan* is not a KV-residency quantity: Ch 17's end-to-end W_e2e ≈ 10 s planning round must not be transplanted into KV slots):

Multipliers apply to the unrounded case (i) quotient 368,000 ÷ 19,780 = 18.606 before the host ceil.

| Sizing screen | Average (10 rps) | Peak (40 rps) |
|---|---|---|
| KV-residency / service-time screen (⌈λ·W/C⌉, floor C = 17) | ⌈10×8.6/17⌉ = 6 hosts | ⌈40×8.6/17⌉ = 21 hosts |
| Independent prefill screen — **case (i) linear-only** (⌈tokens/s ÷ 19,780 tok/s/host⌉) | 5 hosts | 19 hosts |
| Independent prefill screen — **case (iii) causal-triangle** (×1.0861 on case (i)) | 6 hosts | 18.606 → ×1.0861 → 20.21 → 21 |
| Independent prefill screen — **case (ii) full-square conservative** (×1.1723 on the unrounded case (i) value (18.604 → 21.81)) | 6 hosts | 18.606 → ×1.1723 → 21.81 → 22 |
| **N_hosts = max of the named screens** | **6 hosts** | **21 hosts under case (i) and case (iii); 22 under case (ii)** |

*Multipliers are applied to unrounded token/s values before the host ceil; applying them to displayed integer host counts gives different results.*

Two things about that table are load-bearing. First, the ~2.0 rps per-host figure (C/W = 17/8.6 = 1.9767) is the **KV-slot-based analytical UPPER bound on admitted request completion rate, conditional on an assumed mean KV residency time** — a capacity screen inside the KV screen, defined in full below (``What kind of number is ~2.0 req/s?'') and in the **Evidence boundary** panel (Preface); and the *lower* bound on host count derives FROM it (⌈40 ÷ 1.9767⌉ = ⌈20.24⌉ = 21 hosts — a per-host ceiling becomes a fleet floor at that one division); the "throughput ⌈40/2.0⌉ row" and the KV row are algebraically the same bound. Second, the prefill rows are a genuinely *independent* compute screen — the scope ledger's 5 avg / 19 peak, ⌈368,000 ÷ 19,780⌉ = 19 at peak **under case (i), linear-only** — and they are deliberately NOT forced to equal the KV row; the same screen under case (iii) (causal-triangle, ×1.0861) gives 6 / 21 and under case (ii) (full-square conservative, ×1.1723) gives 6 / 22, so **which FLOP-counting case is stated decides whether the compute screen (19), the KV screen (21), or the compute screen again (22) governs the peak**. Every host count in this table is an **illustrative planning screen under its stated FLOP-counting case and the 35% MFU assumption — NOT measured SLO-goodput**. The third scope, N_SLO (queueing / goodput under concurrency), has **no number here because it is unmeasured**: it is a named scope, not a row.

So the canonical workload's peak KV screen is **21 hosts** (⌈344/17⌉, zero spare — the headline policy is a minimum integer count, no reserve; 21 × 17/8.6 = 41.51 rps is ~3.8% headroom above the 40 rps peak), and the average needs **6** — a fleet, not one box. Ch 17's whole-slot convention floors C to 17 first and gives 21; equivalently the floored whole-slot rule gives 21 — this TCO prices that 21-host scenario, and a one-host difference changes no mode comparison. FP8 KV quantization changes only the KV arithmetic in this screen: the raw FP8 byte factor 0.5 gives a hard floor of 35 slots BEFORE efficiency and the host ceil, so the FP8 KV screen is ⌈344/35.0⌉ = 10 at peak (11 at the 95% scheduling factor) — KV-residency arithmetic on a screen, **not an assertion that real deployments halve in size**; FP8 weights or FP8 compute would change other screens and are not modeled here; measured allocation, compute and goodput are absent, and the independent prefill and unmeasured SLO scopes do not shrink with it. **Every TCO figure below is scaled to a named peak fleet — 21 hosts (KV screen / case (i) compute screen) or 22 hosts (case (ii) full-square compute screen), both priced in §16.3; pricing a single host would understate the cost by roughly 21–22×.**

**What kind of number is ~2.0 req/s?** The per-host rate `C_KV / E[W_KV-resident] ≈ 17 / 8.6 ≈ 2.0 req/s` is a **KV-slot-based analytical UPPER bound on admitted request completion rate, conditional on an assumed mean KV residency time** — an average-in-system relation under a particular concurrency, timing, and cache geometry, not an observed service rate, not a measured goodput, not a lower guaranteed rate, and not an SLO goodput. It is derived *from* the KV bound, so it does not add an independent constraint; the host-count *lower* bound (21) is what derives *from* it. The scopes behind fleet sizing must be derived and measured separately, and the formula is only as strong as the weakest one:

- **N_KV (KV-memory occupancy ceiling)** — how many requests *reside* in KV cache given the host's usable HBM and the per-token KV footprint. This is an arithmetic allocation bound (the floor-17 figure).
- **C/W (KV-slot upper bound on admitted rate inside the KV screen)** — the ~8.6 s E[W_KV-resident] proxy is an uncontended request-level estimate; C/W inherits both the KV bound and the service-time assumption, so it is not an independent compute bound and not an independently measured throughput.
- **Independent prefill screen** — a separate compute bound (5 avg / 19 peak from ⌈368,000 ÷ 19,780⌉ **under case (i), linear-only**; 6 / 21 under case (iii) causal-triangle; 6 / 22 under case (ii) full-square conservative), legitimately independent of KV size.
- **N_SLO (queueing / goodput)** — the number of hosts needed to hold p95/p99 TTFT, TPOT, and success rate under *concurrent* arrival. **This scope is unmeasured here. Little's law relates average in-system work to elapsed time under stability; it does not independently prove a throughput ceiling or a p99 latency.** At concurrency ~17, TTFT and TPOT can differ substantially from the isolated ~8.6 s used to obtain `C/W`, because batch efficiency, per-request duration, and interference are all coupled to load.

So the 21-host figure is a **scenario-specific KV/service-time screen contingent on the 8.6-second assumed mean KV residency time remaining valid under concurrent execution**, not a deployment-validated estimate — and it is only the *capacity* requirement: the deployable host count is **max(capacity requirement, tail-latency requirement N_SLO)**, with N_SLO the p95/p99 TTFT/TPOT/success-rate count that is unmeasured here (Little's Law is a mean relation — E[L] = λ·E[W] — so a p95 or p99 W must never be substituted for E[W] inside it; tail percentiles are a separate stochastic constraint). Before budgeting against it, measure `λ_goodput` per host at the target p95/p99 TTFT, TPOT, and success rate on the actual workload, and re-derive N_hosts. (See the falsification protocol in Ch 17.)

**Treat both provisioning policies explicitly.** The headline $ figures below use the 21-host ideal-active fleet (zero spare, provisioned exactly at the 40 rps peak). The capacity-planning rule (Ch 17's authoritative provisioning-policy table, **Table 17-1**) is N = ⌈λ·W/(C·u·ρ)⌉ + N_spare with u = 0.70 and ρ = 0.95 **independent** (u × ρ = 0.665, not nested; u and ρ derate slot occupancy and LB/scheduling efficiency for the KV screen — applied to the compute screen they are the same numerical headroom derate against the 35% MFU assumption, identical arithmetic, different quantity), which gives **31 working hosts** at the peak ⌈40×8.6/(17×0.70×0.95)⌉ = ⌈344/11.305⌉ = 31 and **32 total with N−1**; because one host must be able to drain (failover/maintenance) while the fleet still clears the peak, the N−1 policy requires 32 hosts total (31 working + 1 reserve — 31 surviving × 1.9767 × 0.70 × 0.95 ≈ 40.7 ≥ 40 rps). That spare is a *reserve* host, not serving capacity. (A ρ-omitted variant (ρ = 1) would read 29 working / 30 total — Table 17-1 states why ρ is not nested inside the 70%.) **Mode 2 — cloud GPU instances (rent, occupancy-input priced).** An 8×H100 on-demand instance ~$20/hr (canonical $2.50/GPU-hr × 8). Because the workload's *peak* is 40 rps, the fleet must be able to burst to 21 instances, but what the bill actually depends on is **paid host-hours** — an INPUT this chapter does not derive:

$$
H_\text{paid}\,\text{[host-hours] is an input: } H_\text{paid} = 4{,}380 \text{ host-hours for the 6-host example (the default 4,380 is an assumed } H_\text{paid}\text{); Cost} = p_\text{host} \times H_\text{paid}
$$

The honest cloud bill would be the integral of required capacity over time, plus the warm-pool and capacity-reservation terms:

$$
H_\text{required} = \int_0^T N_\text{required}(t)\,dt + N_\text{warm}\cdot T_\text{warm} + R_\text{reserved}, \; \text{where } T = \text{the billing month}, N_\text{required}(t) = \text{hosts required at time } t, N_\text{warm}\cdot T_\text{warm} = \text{warm-pool host-hours}, R_\text{reserved} = \text{reserved host-hours — each term in host-hours}; \; H_\text{paid} \ge H_\text{required} + \text{warm-pool} + \text{reservation terms}
$$

where $N_\text{required}(t)$ is the number of hosts that must be running at time $t$ (a step function that rises with the load curve at that hour of the day/week). This depends on the actual temporal load profile — the **burst duration and frequency**, the **scale-up latency** (how many seconds/minutes to attach and load a fresh instance), the **model-loading/warmup time** (loading a 140 GB model into a new host is not instant), the **minimum warm pool** to serve the base load without cold-starting, **provider capacity/availability**, and **billing granularity**. Only if the provider can attach instances faster than the load curve demands, with a warm pool already holding the model, can the cloud bill approach a pure average-load figure.

**Reading the result.** Once the fleet is sized (21 hosts under case (i)/KV screen; 22 under case (ii) full-square — both priced above), the comparison changes entirely versus the single-host framing. On the included-cost lines above: self-hosted is **$24.97/1K** at 21 hosts ($155,816.93/mo) and **$26.08/1K** at 22 hosts ($162,760.59/mo), the cloud default-occupancy input is **$14.04/1K** ($87,600/mo), and the managed API is **$20.8/1K** ($129,792/mo). The self-host-to-default-cloud ratio is **~1.8** (155,816.93 ÷ 87,600 = 1.78) — a ~1.8 ratio, not a 2× gap — and self-host sits **above** the API at the canonical 6.24M volume, with the API break-even a region of ~6.2–10.4M req/mo — ~7.5M the $20.8/1K point for the 21-host line ($155,816.93/mo) and ~11.2M for the 32-host policy line ($232,197.23/mo). **None of these three numbers is an unconditional ranking.** The $87.6K cloud line is a paid-occupancy INPUT assumption (not a measured duty cycle or a proven feasible serving schedule), the $155.8K self-host line is an always-provisioned 21-host fleet carrying a fixed staff alloc, and the scopes are not equalized — the not-apples-to-apples caveat preceding Table 16-1 names the specific mismatches. **Any "cheaper" reading is conditional on those named inputs, not demonstrated.**

**Apples-to-apples caveat.** The $87.6K cloud figure is an occupancy input; it does not include an N−1 failover reserve, capacity held for failures/maintenance, warm-capacity cost during idle periods, model-startup time, or provider availability variance, and the self-host figure does not charge for the same availability target's redundancy. So the two are **not compared on equal reliability or load-serving assumptions**. Before treating any mode as cheaper, add: the same served-load trace, p95 SLO, peak duration, availability target, failover coverage, warm capacity, model startup time, occupancy evidence, and price period to *both* sides. Under a nonideal elastic case (a reserved warm pool sized for N−1 plus slower scale-up), the cloud cost rises toward the always-on-at-peak figure and any "cloud is cheapest" result may not hold. As above: the $14.04/1K figure is an **assumption-bounded input** — frame the decision as "cloud is cheaper **if** the occupancy input holds and a warm pool can be held cheaply," not as an unconditional ranking. The decisive caveat remains the **staff/ops term and whether it is sunk**, and now also the **fleet requirement** — a single-host TCO understates the real cost by about 21× against the 21-host peak fleet and about 6× against the 6-host average — the multiplier depends on whether the comparison is against peak- or average-provisioned capacity (§16.1).

**Multi-region active-active availability is NOT priced in this TCO.** The canonical workload's stated SLA (Ch 4) is multi-region active-active at 99.9% availability, but **every** `$/1K` figure in this chapter (and the host counts in Ch 17) prices a **single-region** deployment — the 21-host headline rows are the **ideal zero-spare** fleet with **no N−1 reserve priced**, and only the 32-host policy row carries the **31 working + 1 reserve** N−1 single-host failover — **not** multi-region active-active. Multi-region active-active is a **different provisioning problem** from N−1 single-host failover: it requires cross-region capacity duplication (no single region's loss may take the service down), region-failure tolerance, data locality and traffic routing (geo/health-based routing, per-region caches and indices), and consistent state/data replication. **Mandatory vs optional in that replication:** each region must be able to load the model weights and serve its retrieval index — weight/index availability is mandatory, because a region that cannot load the model or answer retrieval cannot serve traffic — whereas **replicating live KV across regions is an optional policy, not a requirement**: clients may instead replay or recompute the context in the surviving region, trading cross-region KV transfer for recovery time inside the SLA's recovery objectives. None of that is priced or validated here, and none of it is implied by the N−1 reserve host above (the 32-host policy row). Accordingly the canonical cost figures **understate the cost of the canonical workload's own stated 99.9% availability requirement** — read the `$/1K` and monthly totals as *single-region* numbers — zero-spare at the 21-host headline, N−1-reserve only at the 32-host policy — and add a separate multi-region capacity + replication cost line (on the order of ≥2× the single-region fleet on the capacity-duplication term alone) before the availability SLA can be considered met.

## 4. Measurement

Four metrics keep the TCO model honest:

1. **Cost per useful (SLO-meeting) request** — the decision number; divide total cost by goodput-meeting requests (Ch 6), not raw tokens.
2. **Effective utilization** — SLO-qualified goodput ÷ sustained SLO-qualified capacity (both measured at the same SLO); low utilization is the hidden tax in both capex and rented instances. **Raw utilization = served ÷ provisioned; effective utilization = SLO-meeting goodput ÷ theoretical capacity — cost per useful request uses the effective definition.**
3. **Break-even volume** — the requests/month where self-host cost-per-request crosses managed API; below it, don't buy cards.
4. **Staff/occupancy adjusters** — the engineering-hours and paid-occupancy assumptions that dominate the comparisons; measure them, don't assume.

### Support lifecycle belongs in service cost

A lower accelerator price does not establish a lower service cost. Migration work, pinned-runtime maintenance, security updates, failed attempts, spare capacity, and fallback execution belong in the numerator. The denominator remains quality-qualified, SLO-compliant work. If a candidate needs more replicas because of worse tail latency or lower usable KV capacity, that requirement can reverse an apparent device-price advantage. This extends the cost model; it does not supply a backend ranking. [ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED]

## 5. Common Mistakes

- **Comparing sticker price to per-token rate.** A $/1K-token API quote already includes amortization; a card price tag does not. Amortize both.
- **Ignoring the staff/ops term.** Self-host "saves money" on paper and loses the moment two engineers are consumed full-time.
- **Assuming 100% utilization.** Idle cards are still burning electricity and depreciation; bake duty cycle in.
- **Forgetting opportunity cost.** A 6-month procurement cycle versus a same-week API integration is a real cost measured in time-to-value.
- **Using peak capacity for cost-per-request.** Peak sizing (40 rps) overstates cost if average (10 rps) utilization is what governs. (The headline $24.97/1K above does divide the peak-sized 21-host fleet by average volume — deliberately: this workload must clear the 40 rps peak, so peak capacity is real cost here; the mistake applies when peak sizing is avoidable, e.g. batch or queueable traffic.)
- **Treating an occupancy input as a measurement.** The cloud default (4,380 paid host-hours) is an assumption, not a trace; never promote it to a measured duty cycle or a guaranteed lower bound.

## 8. End-of-Chapter Mini-Case: TCO Discipline and the Staff Term

A startup's CTO shows the architect a warrant to buy two 8×H100 servers for the new internal RAG assistant, citing "we'll save on token fees." Same 70B FP16 as the canonical case, 2 GPUs per replica → 8 replicas across the two hosts; 2 rps average is 0.25 rps/replica, well inside the 0.48 rps/replica the canonical 21-host fleet sustains at its 40 rps peak. Applying TCO discipline, the architect does not dispute the raw token math. Instead they price it: at this startup's *actual* early volume — ~2 rps average, ~5M requests/month, and only a 0.5 FTE engineer fully available for GPU ops — the break-even against a managed API is this chapter's own division: $40,000 ÷ $0.0208 ≈ **1.9M requests/month**, so the current volume is already ~2.6× past the break-even. This is **a different startup workload with 24×7 traffic**, not the canonical case's business-hours shape: 5M/mo at 2 rps = 2.5M s ≈ **29 days of 24×7 traffic**, versus the canonical **624,000 active s/mo** (8 h × 3600 s/h × 260 workdays ÷ 12 = 624,000 active s/mo; equivalently 6,240,000 requests ÷ 10 rps) — same per-request price, a different duty cycle. The canonical input/output mix is 9,200 in / 300 out; the saving scales with the per-request price — ≈ $35K/mo and a 2.7M break-even at $0.015/req, ≈ $85K/mo and 1.6M at $0.025/req.

The honest number: self-hosting two servers ~$40K/month all-in vs ~$104K/month API at current volume (5M × $0.0208) — a **~$64K/month saving in favor of self-hosting, with the staff term already inside the $40K** (capex ~$11.6K + power ~$2.2K for two hosts leaves ≤ ~$26K for staff — power is the ~$2.2K line already counted; upkeep labour sits in the staff term, so ~$0.2K of the $26.2K remainder is unallocated — too small to dwarf a $64K saving). The staff term is on a different basis than Mode 1: the mini-case prices a startup's 0.5 FTE engineer (~$26K/mo — ≈$624K/yr fully loaded for one engineer, a scenario value rather than a market rate), not Mode 1's mature-operator alloc (~$10K/mo for 21 hosts) — re-price on one staffing basis before comparing. Sensitivity: the ~7.5M break-even assumes the $10K staff alloc (0.19 FTE at the mini-case's $624K/yr rate); at 1 FTE per 8 hosts the break-even rises to ~13.6M req/mo, at 0.25 FTE/host to ~20.1M. Basis: US senior infrastructure engineer, fully loaded at ~1.5× base (market/geography/benefits treatment unstated — scenario value). Sensitivity: at $156K/yr fully loaded (0.5 FTE engineer → $6.5K/mo) self-host = 13.8 + 6.5 = $20.3K/mo, still ≪ the $64K threshold — the verdict is robust to the rate; the $84K saving and the threshold margin are not.

**Which term is which — no double-counting.** The ~$40K self-host cost already carries the ~$26K/mo 0.5 FTE engineer wage, so the ~$64K opportunity cost is an **alternative valuation of the same hours**, not incremental foregone product value stacked on top of that compensation:

| Term | What it prices | ~$/month | Where it is counted |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wages** — 0.5 FTE engineer, fully loaded (≈$624K/yr ÷ 2, scenario value) | cash compensation for the same 0.5 FTE engineer | ~$26K | **inside** the ~$40K self-host cost (already counted) |
| **Time redeployment** — the same 0.5 FTE engineer's hours re-priced as product time | non-cash valuation of the *same* hours | threshold ~$90K (~$1.08M/yr) | an **alternative** valuation of those hours, not an added cost line |
| **Incremental foregone contribution** — product value from hours *beyond* the priced 0.5 FTE engineer (extra product work displaced on top of the compensation already counted) | none in the base case | ~$0 | not counted; it appears only if the two servers consume more than 0.5 FTE engineer |

Reading: the wages are cash already inside the $40K; the ~$90K threshold is what those freed hours must be worth, with the wage counted in both columns ($104K + $26K − $40K = $90.2K/mo). Adding the two would price the same 0.5 FTE engineer twice.

The token arithmetic alone therefore does not send this startup to the API; it points to self-hosting. Within this equal-capability assumption set, the only remaining **MODELED** term that can flip the decision is the *opportunity* cost of the GPU-ops time the two servers consume — the better part of the only engineer the startup can spare (the hypothetical 0.5 FTE engineer label priced above). This is a statement about the model, not about the decision: quality parity, peak throughput, SLO-goodput, regional availability, burst behavior, procurement lead time, resilience and capital outlay are all decision-relevant too, and none of them is priced in this mini-case — any of them can flip the real decision from outside the assumption set. To justify paying the API premium, that term must exceed **~$90K/month** (wage counted in both columns: $104K + $26K − $40K = $90.2K/mo), about $1.08M/year of product work the startup is not doing. The architect's verdict: **start on the managed API now (conditional on the startup's $26K/mo 0.5 FTE engineer staffing basis), and revisit self-host the moment the consumed engineer's opportunity cost falls below ~$90K/month — for example when a second engineer frees up** — then re-run the same model. On Mode 1's ~$10K/mo staff alloc the self-host cost is ~$23.8K/mo and the gap ~$80.2K/mo. Request volume is not the lever: Within the two-server capacity headroom, more volume widens the gap; beyond that bound, additional servers step the self-host cost up (state the assumed peak rps for these two hosts). The CTO did not buy the servers. The break-even graph did not make the answer self-evident; it narrowed the whole decision to one labeled assumption — this startup values its half-engineer's product time above $90K/month. A team that cannot say that, or that has a second engineer to spare, runs the same numbers and buys the servers.
