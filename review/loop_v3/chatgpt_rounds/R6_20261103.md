## 1. Executive assessment

The manuscript has a coherent token → workload → system → architecture → fleet → architect organization and is unusually deliberate about distinguishing analytical capacity limits, illustrative scenarios, actual measurements, and unresolved evidence. The work in Chapters 4, 7, 8, 16 and 17 uses a consistent teaching geometry and many calculations reconcile. The explanations of all-reduce semantics, KV scaling, prefill/decode phases, and the limits of single-region TCO are generally careful. The cover is attractive and technically relevant; fonts are embedded; internal navigation is extensive.

The remaining highest-impact issues are **not** dozens of broken equations scattered across the book. They are concentrated in the capstone worked example (Chapter 26), which is supposed to demonstrate the architect's final method. Several assertions there either contradict the book's earlier measured-versus-modeled discipline, mix cost bases, silently replace latency assumptions, or use a cache design that is incomplete for privacy-sensitive enterprise RAG. This concentration matters: the last worked example carries disproportionate pedagogical weight, and readers may reuse its numbers and patterns as recommendations.

**Release gate:** Rework and re-audit Chapter 26, correct the Preface's TTFT definition, repair the edition-history ledger, and complete a print/eBook-specific QA pass. The core system chapters do **not** require a wholesale rewrite on the evidence found in this review.

### Severity definitions

- **P0 — Blocker:** invalid worked conclusion, unsafe reusable architecture, or contradiction central to the book's thesis. Requires correction before publication.
- **P1 — Major:** significant consistency, epistemic, methodology, or decision-quality problem. Requires correction before publication.
- **P2 — Moderate:** reader-facing ambiguity, figure/caption problem, or incomplete release control. Fix in the final production pass.
- **P3 — Polish:** optional clarity, style, or whitespace improvement.

**Review method and limits:** All 449 pages were text-extracted/indexed; the full table of contents and figure list were checked; all **57 numbered figure captions** were located and all their pages were inspected in figure-page contact sheets; representative high-resolution pages, diagrams, tables, financial scenarios, and appendix pages were opened separately. The canonical workload, KV arithmetic, TCO, capstone, source ledger and dated appendix claims received closer calculation/consistency checks. PDF metadata, fonts, hyperlink counts, and placeholder scans were checked. This is a substantive targeted editorial/technical review, **not** a claim that each sentence, every plotted coordinate, or every external citation of all 449 pages was independently reproduced or visually checked at full zoom. Source-code execution and direct checkout of the release repository were not part of this pass.

## 2. Confirmed blockers and major defects

### R-01 — P0 — Chapter 26's claimed 48% reduction confuses variable generation cost with fixed self-hosted TCO

**Where:** PDF pp. 397–398 and 400–403 (printed pp. 377–383); especially §26.8, Table 26-2, Fig. 26.2. Compare Chapter 16, PDF pp. 259–261.

**What the book says:** The capstone reports about **48% lower generation cost**, obtained as `0.35 + 0.65 × 0.20 = 0.48`; then it applies the complementary `0.65 × 0.80 = 0.52` to Chapter 16's **$0.02497066 per request** self-hosting result, yielding ~$0.013/query.

**Why the calculation is not valid as stated:** The Chapter 16 $0.02497066 figure is **not** a marginal per-generated-request charge. Its numerator is an always-provisioned fleet including five-year **hardware amortization**, site power, and a fixed **$10,000/month staffing allocation**. At the same 21-host fleet and 6.24M served queries/month, those included expenses stay roughly $155,816.93/month even if a cache reduces GPU generation work. The blended *compute consumed* may fall; the all-in, fixed-fleet *dollars per end-user request* cannot simply be multiplied by 0.52. The capstone silently changes the economic basis.

**Checkable counterexample:** Using the book's own simplified host model ($350,000/60 months + $1,110.33/month power per host, plus fixed $10,000 staff), and **assuming** 35% true cache hits also allow deterministic re-sizing of peak host load from 40 to 26 rps, the Chapter 16 case-(i)/KV screen changes from 21 hosts to approximately `ceil(26 × 8.6 / 17) = 14` hosts. The illustrative new monthly cost is `14 × ($350,000/60 + $1,110.33) + $10,000 ≈ $107,211.29`, or **$17.18/1K requests**, a **31.2%** savings against $155,816.93, not 48%. The full-square case-(ii) screen could require ~15 hosts and must be re-evaluated independently. These figures are *not* a proposed deployment budget: they are a counterexample showing that re-sizing, fixed expenses, compute scope, queueing, and service guarantees matter. If the original fleet remains in place, Chapter 16's included monthly numerator does not decline with cache hits.

**Required correction:** Distinguish (A) marginal generation work, (B) variable metered API spend, and (C) all-in self-hosted TCO. Either relabel 48% as a **hypothetical reduction in marginal generation work**, with no TCO claim, or recompute the fleet, fixed/variable expense ledger, cache-serving costs, and SLO-qualified end-user cost using one consistent denominator. Add a unit test asserting that a cache-hit ratio alone cannot shrink the cost of an unchanged fixed fleet.

### R-02 — P0 — Capstone TTFT silently contradicts the canonical 9.2K-input prefill geometry

**Where:** PDF pp. 74–76, 80; PDF p. 397, §26.3; PDF pp. 400–401, §26.8 and Fig. 26.2.

**What the book says:** The canonical workload is ~9,200 input tokens, ~1.08 s prefill and ~120 ms retrieval, yielding a 1.2 s TTFT *budget* (Chapter 4). In Chapter 26 the text says the canonical service time and 70B/8×H100 host still hold, yet declares an **uncached baseline p99 TTFT of 450 ms**, decomposed into **320 ms model compute + 80 ms I/O + 50 ms retrieval**, and an optimized miss path of 250 ms.

**Why this is not reconciled:** Under the same stated prompt geometry and canonical prefill estimate, 450 ms client-facing TTFT cannot be derived without **changing the prefill performance/amount of work**. The capstone calls itself a 'retrieved-context/short-prompt leg' but gives no new token length, prefix-cache hit, performance mode, throughput, scheduling trace, or explicit substitute for the canonical 1.08 s. The p99 is additionally presented as the sum of illustrative component estimates, not a percentile computed from a modeled distribution. The text acknowledges the latter issue, but this does not repair the workload/latency discontinuity.

**Required correction:** Choose explicitly: (A) keep the 9.2K canonical workload and use a defensible, labeled latency input/model consistent with the 1.08 s prefill component; or (B) declare the capstone as a **different** short-prompt/multi-stage workload with exact prompt length, cache state, hardware, model configuration, and measurement scope. If the 450/250 ms figures are retained as pure illustrative numbers disconnected from the canonical workload, remove prose implying they are derived from the earlier host geometry. State client TTFT as `network + admission/queue + retrieval + tokenization + prefill/first-token path + transport` with measurement boundaries.

### R-03 — P0 — Reusable enterprise RAG response-cache design omits authorization and document-version isolation

**Where:** PDF p. 397, §26.3 Pattern B; PDF pp. 400–402, §26.8, Table 26-2.

**What the book says:** A completed answer and retrieved context are stored in a cache 'keyed by question hash', and cache hits skip generation and embedding/retrieval. The broader canonical workload explicitly contains privacy-sensitive documents and organizational access constraints (Chapter 4).

**Why this is unsafe/incomplete as a pattern:** A raw question hash is insufficient to ensure that two callers asking the same question are authorized to receive the **same source passages or answer**. Entitlement changes, tenant identity, document ACL/version, retrieval-index generation, policy changes, model/prompt version, answer provenance, and freshness must be part of the cache's scope and/or revalidation gate. Otherwise a cross-user hit can leak another user's authorized response or return stale, now-disallowed information. The five-minute TTL does not repair an authorization mismatch. This is a missing design control, not a claim that the manuscript documents an actual exploit.

**Required correction:** Add a threat model and a safe example cache key/policy such as `(tenant, authorization-scope digest, normalized query or semantic key, corpus/index snapshot, policy/model/prompt revision)` with a recheck at serving time; explicitly avoid caching secret-bearing responses across privilege boundaries. Demonstrate invalidation on document permission changes, deletion/withdrawal, index refresh, and source-update events. Track cache-hit **quality/authorization compliance** in SLO-goodput. If the design deliberately relies on exact hash matching, call it an **exact-match response cache**, not a semantic cache, until the semantic lookup mechanism and cost are described.

**External corroboration checked:** vLLM's security documentation warns that shared server-process caches are not tenant-isolation boundaries and recommends explicit gateway/tenant scoping (`https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/usage/security/`). This does not prove the same implementation vulnerability here; it reinforces the need for an explicit isolation design.

### R-04 — P1 — 'Apply inference sharding' is not a new optimization over the stated baseline

**Where:** PDF pp. 397–398, 400–401.

The capstone baseline is a 70B FP16 model on a single 8×H100 host. Chapter 26 itself states that the 140 GB weights are **already tensor-parallel over the eight H100s**. Section 26.8 then applies Pattern A, described as 'inference sharding across an 8×H100 node', and later credits 'sharding' for some of the performance/cost comparison. No changed sharding configuration is named. This is a before/after comparison with no demonstrated before/after difference in Pattern A. Specify an actual control intervention (e.g. TP2→TP8, replica count, parallelism placement, or batch/operator configuration), and recompute latency, communication and memory for both cases; otherwise treat sharding as **baseline configuration**, not an incremental gain.

### R-05 — P1 — The capstone rejects deeper batching but also uses it to claim a cost benefit

**Where:** PDF p. 398, 'Alternatives considered'; PDF p. 401, §26.8 savings decomposition.

One paragraph rejects **deeper continuous batching** because it lengthens the queue and harms p99 TTFT. The numerical ledger nonetheless assigns the remaining 65% of requests **~20% lower per-token cost from deeper continuous batching on the already-sharded engine**. If these are different operating points, they must be named and shown separately. If the same lever is both rejected and used, the modeled result is internally inconsistent. Remove the 20% term, replace it with a different explicitly supported lever, or supply a throughput/queueing/Pareto comparison demonstrating why one batching setting is accepted and another rejected.

### R-06 — P1 — Rejecting a second replica because the bottleneck is 'concurrency/KV residency' reverses the architectural logic

**Where:** PDF pp. 397–398, Pattern A and alternatives.

The text hypothesizes a second serving host would improve per-request latency by under 5% *because* concurrency/KV residency is the bottleneck. But an independent replica adds **KV slots and capacity**, which is precisely what helps when a queue is caused by insufficient resident-request capacity. A lightly loaded 2-rps host might see little latency improvement from another host; a host offered 3–4 rps with the same ~2-rps screen may see significant queueing relief. The answer is a load-curve and SLO-goodput question, not a property of the phrase 'KV-bound'. The <5% statement can remain an untested scenario hypothesis but should **not** be used as the basis for rejecting the alternative. Compare one versus two replicas over an actual offered-load interval with realistic availability/failover requirements.

### R-07 — P1 — Capstone misuses the book's own FACT/MEASURED terminology

**Where:** PDF pp. 393, 397–403; notably §26.8 Step 1 and Fig. 26.2.

The chapter carefully defines FACT as a **verified runtime quantity**, then writes 'FACTs collected' for ~2 rps, 45% SM utilization and an invented latency decomposition. The text later calls them scenario inputs and says real measurements are absent. Figure 26.2's `1 · FACT` panel and Table 26-2's modeled example can be read as evidence the book does not have. Keep a clear distinction between `assumed baseline for worked example`, `computed result of those assumptions`, `proposed instrumentation`, and `actually observed FACT`. If no author-run load test occurred, reserve 'FACT collected' for the future validation template and mark Fig. 26.2's first box `ILLUSTRATIVE INPUTS`.

### R-08 — P1 — TTFT and prefill latency are not interchangeable, despite the Preface saying they are

**Where:** PDF p. 19, Preface 'Reading conventions'; PDF p. 80, Table 4-2 ('Prefill latency (TTFT)'); compare PDF p. 35, Fig. 2.1 and associated explanation.

The Preface states: 'TTFT, time-to-first-token and prefill latency are likewise interchangeable'. Chapter 2 correctly states that prefill is **a component of**, not identical to, user-observed TTFT. These are not synonyms in the operational definition required for an architecture/SLO handbook. TTFT may include queueing, request preprocessing, networking and first-token generation and transport. The canonical 1.2 s budget already uses retrieval + prefill, so the Preface wording is doubly misleading. Correct the definition globally, rename Table 4-2's field `Prefill compute budget (derived from simplified TTFT budget)`, and expose missing budget terms instead of treating them as zero measured costs.

**External corroboration checked:** NVIDIA NIM benchmarking metrics (`https://docs.nvidia.com/nim/benchmarking/llm/metrics`) and NVIDIA's inference benchmarking article (`https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/llm-benchmarking-fundamental-concepts/`) explicitly distinguish TTFT from prefill and include queueing/network latency in TTFT.

### R-09 — P1 — Capstone model population grows from 2,000 to 3,000 without a new traffic/arrival model

**Where:** PDF pp. 397, 400–401.

Section 26.8 holds the single host near ~2 requests/sec while asking whether p99 remains safe as users grow from ~2,000 to ~3,000 (1.5×). User population alone does not give arrival rate, cache-hit ratio, peak correlation, or queueing distribution. The book repeatedly teaches this distinction in Chapters 4, 9 and 17, so the capstone should apply it. State a concrete 3,000-user offered-load trace or scaling assumption, then test capacity and fallback quality. A user count without an arrival law does not validate scaling.

### R-10 — P1 — Quality of circuit-breaker fallback is not accounted for in the headline improvement

**Where:** PDF pp. 397–403, Table 26-2 and Fig. 26.2.

The modeled circuit breaker routes approximately 7% of requests to a **lightweight intent classifier** when the RAG generator is saturated. That is not equivalent to delivering a correct full RAG answer. A low TTFT on a downgraded task can make latency statistics look better while **quality-qualified goodput** falls. Make the fallback response a separately labeled degraded outcome, include response quality/success and denominator rules in the p99 and cost metrics, and state the policy for whether fallback counts as SLO success. The book's own Chapters 6 and 14 establish that the right denominator is completed useful requests, not any quick output.

## 3. Moderate corrections / release controls

### R-11 — P2 — November 1 appendix revision is absent from the final edition-history table

**Where:** PDF pp. 18, 405–406 and 411 versus PDF p. 445 ('Edition history' panel, 'Appendix addenda' row).

### R-12 — P2 — Subjective Fig. 26.1 score values are labeled DERIVED

**Where:** PDF p. 396, Fig. 26.1 caption; compare Preface evidence taxonomy PDF pp. 17–18 and Sources/Method PDF pp. 439–441.

The caption says every normalized bar value is **author-selected subjective 0–1 intensity** and that no measured data or min–max rescaling produced the values. Yet its evidence marker is `[ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED]`. The actual input scores are **`[ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]`**; a later computed total or ratio could be `[ILLUSTRATIVE][DERIVED]`. This is a locally small but important self-contradiction of the book's core evidence rule. Also make clear the six fingerprint axes in this chart differ from Chapter 4's six architectural workload dimensions.

### R-13 — P2 — Semantic response cache is described as a question-hash cache

**Where:** PDF p. 397.

A hash of the exact question string implements an **exact-match** cache unless a semantic normalization/embedding-and-nearest-neighbor method is supplied. The capstone says embeddings/retrieval are skipped on hits; a true semantic similarity cache still incurs lookup and usually encoding costs unless another keyed canonicalization system is provided. Choose one algorithm, describe its hit/miss semantics, and charge the lookup path. Distinguish response caching from exact-prefix/KV caching and retrieval-result caching.

### R-14 — P2 — Table 16-2's title promises a matched comparison that it explicitly does not provide

**Where:** PDF pp. 267–268, 'Table 16-2 — Matched SLO/availability comparison (decision view)'.

The table explicitly leaves multi-region active-active **unsized, unpriced, and not comparable**; its self-host and elastic-cloud rows remain single-region examples. The body correctly warns against ranking them as equal-scope. Rename it to **'Availability-scope reconciliation / remaining gaps'** (or comparable) rather than 'Matched ... comparison'. This avoids a reader quoting the table title without seeing the caveat.

### R-15 — P2 — Figure A.1 interrupts a numbered four-family list

**Where:** PDF pp. 406–407.

The appendix's 'four families at a glance' list begins with items 1–3 before the page break, then Fig. A.1 occupies the top of the next page, and item **4** appears **after** the figure and its caption. The list is semantically intact but visually severed. Move Fig. A.1 after the complete numbered list or force the list to finish before floating the chart. The current layout makes a central scope statement unnecessarily hard to scan.

### R-16 — P2 — Fig. 11.3's 35 GB transfer case needs its distinct ~13K-token scenario printed directly in the figure

**Where:** PDF p. 202, Fig. 11.3; PDF p. 180, §9.8.

The 35 GB KV transfer is explicitly derived for a **separate ~13.3K-token** example in §9.8; the canonical ~9.5K request is ~24.9 GB. The figure itself prints '35 GB / request' and '1.4 TB/s at 40 rps' without stating **13K-token transfer assumption** in its visible labels. The caption points to §9.8, but this is a self-standing figure likely to be shared. Add `illustrative 13.3K-token state, FP16 full-MHA` immediately under the 35 GB label, and keep the aggregate-shared-link caveat. The current arithmetic is correct **for its specified example**; this is a context-legibility defect, not a miscalculation.

### R-17 — P2 — Internal examples still tempt readers to use availability-incomplete TCO as a decision

**Where:** PDF pp. 73–77 and 256–272, especially the canonical operational requirement versus the single-region price examples.

The manuscript explicitly and correctly says the canonical 99.9% active-active multi-region requirement is **not priced or validated**, and later marks the decision `screened; not committable`. Preserve these strong caveats. For publication, make them more prominent in the *summary* charts and copy-ready work products, including Chapter 22/25 reuse, so no short excerpt appears to establish an actionable cloud-vs-API-vs-self-host winner. A successful sign-off is a **conditional teaching illustration**, not a valid procurement recommendation without equalized region-failure/SLO/operational scope.

### R-18 — P2 — Print figure density varies: some diagrams are much smaller than available whitespace

**Where:** Fig. 1.1 (PDF p. 27), Fig. 6.1 (110), Fig. 7.2 (127), Fig. 9.3 (172), Fig. 19.3 (324), Fig. A.2 (414), among others.

The vector diagrams are generally clean and not visibly clipped in the checked renders, and captions use normal body-size text. However, figures with multiple small nodes, dense callouts and vertical whitespace occupy modest fractions of the page; their internal label sizes can be the bottleneck in a physical print. Audit these on a **100% physical-size proof** (not merely a 200%-zoom PDF). Prefer enlarging the graphic to the text-width limit and reducing explanatory detail **inside** the diagram rather than expanding already-long captions. Distinguish actual illegibility from contact-sheet shrinkage: this pass did not establish a blanket font-size failure.

### R-19 — P2 — Make the first-page cover/interior packaging explicit for KDP

**Where:** PDF p. 1, followed by blank p. 2 and title p. 3; overall PDF is 449 pages at US Letter.

The first page is a designed full-color cover-like page, while p. 3 is the conventional title page. This may be intentional as a digital edition or an illustrated half-title, but **KDP print interiors and exterior full-wrap covers are separate deliverables**. Confirm whether p. 1 is meant to be the first printed interior page; otherwise export an interior-only PDF with the correct parity/blank-page adjustments and a separate print cover at the correct trim/spine/bleed dimensions. This is a production-asset check, not a verified formatting defect. The PDF is **not tagged** for accessibility/reflow; if used as an accessible electronic edition, a separate tagging/accessibility pass is required.

### R-20 — P3 — Lower rhetorical/repetition burden where caveats are repeated at full length

**Where:** Most visibly Chapter 16's adjacent 21/22/32/34-host tables and explanations, and repeated 'snapshot not measured' paragraphs in Appendix A.

The qualifiers are valuable and prevent real errors; do not delete them globally. Replace repeated page-long restatements with a succinct invariant/evidence panel followed by cross-references, while preserving unique boundary conditions near figures and copy-ready outputs. The manuscript's strong epistemic discipline should be easier to *use*, not watered down.
