## 1. Executive decision

The work is technically ambitious, unusually careful about evidence, and much improved in separating assumptions, analytical ceilings, modeled rates, and deployed measurements. In particular, the baseline full-MHA KV arithmetic, the distinction between per-host and fleet-wide economics, the explicit 21-host versus 30-host policies, and the single-region versus multi-region disclaimer have been handled thoughtfully. Those improvements should be **preserved**, not undone by superficially simplifying the book.

However, a book built on the thesis that architectural conclusions can be *traced and defended with arithmetic* cannot publish multiple incompatible arithmetic paths for the same agentic request. The most important problem is not typography: **Ch. 19 reports a roughly 9.3 s T=3 end-to-end agent response while Ch. 17 prices an approximately 13.8 s T=3 response because it correctly includes additional generated tokens.** Those are not merely alternate rounding conventions. The example's call-input ledger also crosses its own boundary between *input* and *input-plus-final-output*. The red/green-team loop should catch both.

The other significant mathematical/pedagogical defect is **Fig. 20.1's false explanation of the post-saturation gap** between theoretical fleet capacity and the model's capped served demand. This is a data-interpretation mistake even though the plotted lines themselves are arithmetically reasonable. The TCO crossover likewise needs a clear rule about what changes on its x-axis: average duty cycle at a fixed peak, or a proportionally scaled workload with a growing peak. The same fixed 21-host fleet does not have unchanged feasibility under both.

**Recommended disposition:** Correct the P1 findings, regenerate the specific tables and plots, execute the release acceptance tests in §10, and then perform a physical print proof. Do **not** lower the bar by merely inserting more caveats. Where the model is internally wrong, correct the model and its dependent text/figures.

### Severity convention

- **P0 / fatal:** demonstrably wrong safety-critical or foundational derivation requiring immediate withdrawal. **None confirmed in this review.**
- **P1 / release blocker:** incorrect computation, contradiction, or a graph likely to teach a materially wrong architectural decision.
- **P2 / should fix before print:** serious readability, specificity, provenance, or pedagogy weakness with a bounded correction.
- **P3 / polish:** cosmetic or optional improvement.
- **GATE / incomplete:** intentionally deferred, explicitly identified limitation that prevents declaring an end-to-end production recommendation validated, but is **not** misrepresented as measured.

## 2. Detailed technical findings and suggested fixes

### FTF-R01 — Agentic latency model contradicts its own generated-token count (**P1**)

**Location:** Ch. 19, §19.4.1–§19.4.2, §19.8 and Table 19-1 (printed pp. 278–289; PDF pp. 298–309); cross-reference Ch. 17 §17.8 (printed p. 260; PDF p. 280).

**Evidence in this edition:** The agent model defines each additional turn as δ = 800 retrieved tokens plus γ = 65 *model-generated* reasoning tokens, T ≤ 3, and a final answer of 300 tokens. Ch. 19 assigns only approximately 120 ms to the model's per-turn 'agent step', 100 ms to its tool, and reports T=3 response time `8.6 + 3 × 0.22 ≈ 9.3 s`. However, the canonical token-generation rate is **25 ms per generated output token**. Just the T=3 intermediate reasoning generation, before extra prefill and scheduling, costs:

```text
Three turns × 65 generated tokens/turn = 195 generated tokens
195 tokens × 0.025 s/token = 4.875 s of additional decode
Baseline final response = 1.08 s prefill + 300 × 0.025 s = 8.58 s
Three tool trips at 100 ms = 0.30 s
Conservative lower-order T=3 total = 8.58 + 4.875 + 0.30 = 13.755 s
```

This reconstruction is close to Ch. 17's **~13.8 s** agentic planning time, not Ch. 19's **~9.3 s**. It is favorable to the chapter: it assumes one base prefill and does not add any repeat-prefill cost, queueing, failures, or extra per-call setup. If the 120 ms 'agent step' is separate from producing γ, it adds further cost; if it *includes* producing γ, it contradicts the declared 25 ms/token generation rate. Define the measurement boundary before choosing either interpretation.

**Minimal sensitivity (not measured, for the book's own stated assumptions):**

| Agent turns T | Table 19-1 E2E approximately | Conservative generation + 100 ms/tool estimate | Understatement |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 8.8 s | 10.305 s | 1.5 s |
| 2 | 9.0 s | 12.030 s | 3.0 s |
| 3 | 9.3 s | 13.755 s | 4.5 s |
| 4, out of policy | 9.5 s | 15.480 s | 6.0 s |

**Action:** Declare separate quantities for (a) model-call count; (b) model-output tokens generated in tool-choice/short-reasoning phases; (c) new full/uncached prefill tokens for each call; (d) external tool wall time; (e) queue/scheduler; (f) final answer generation. Make Ch. 19's E2E model match Ch. 17's W model for the same T and cache policy. Then regenerate **Table 19-1**, the §19.8 performance narrative and any SLO judgments. Keep a separate small `T × L_agent` variable *only* for orchestration excluded from model generation, and say so prominently.

**Acceptance test:** At T=3, the sum of recorded per-call decode durations must account for all 495 generated tokens (300 final + 195 intermediate), not just the final 300. A 25-ms/token model cannot produce 65 intermediate tokens in 120 ms under the same stated serialized-generation model.

### FTF-R02 — “Input” and “total including output” are merged in a three-call example (**P1**)

**Location:** Ch. 19 §19.4.1, printed p. 279 / PDF p. 299, and Table 19-1 printed p. 289 / PDF p. 309.

The page correctly gives the *end-of-task token extent* at T=3 as `9,200 + 3 × (800 + 65) + 300 = 12,095`. Here **300 is the final output**, not input for the model call that generated it. The same page then describes the third call's **input** as 12,095 and sums `9,200 + 10,365 + 12,095 = 31,660` as *three-call input*. At least the 300 final-output tokens have crossed an input/output boundary. More fundamentally, the example has not specified whether T counts *tool turns*, *model invocations*, or *completed outputs*, so it is not possible to repair this by changing one number alone.

**A coherent counterexample to use in the correction:** If three agent turns each append 865 tokens before a distinct final-answer model call, then four model calls start with the following input lengths:

```text
Call 1:  9,200
Call 2: 10,065
Call 3: 10,930
Call 4: 11,795   [the call generating the final 300-token output]
Total task input without prefix caching = 41,990 tokens
Task output generated by the model = 3 × 65 + 300 = 495 tokens
End-of-task max context extent = 11,795 + 300 = 12,095 tokens
```

Alternatively, if a three-call execution is intended, show its *three actual call inputs* and use the appropriate number of completed intermediate turns. For example, two completed intermediate turns followed by a final call give `9,200 + 10,065 + 10,930 = 30,195` input tokens, with different final context and model-output counts. These are **alternative execution conventions**, not interchangeable estimates. The original 31,660 figure is not supported by either illustrated convention.

**Action:** Replace the prose calculation with a call-trace table having `invocation`, `input before call`, `new model output`, `tool output`, `post-call retained context`, `prefix-cache reused`, `actual prefill processed`, and `API billed tokens`. Reconcile this table with Figs. 19.2/19.3 and Ch. 17's per-request max-KV reservation. For a T=3 simulation, both the task-total and peak-residency numbers must be derivable from that single ledger.

### FTF-R03 — Fig. 20.1 labels unused capacity as overhead (**P1**)

**Location:** Fig. 20.1 and its caption, printed p. 299 / PDF p. 319.

The diagram plots a rising unconstrained analytical capacity line and a served-load line capped at 40 requests/s. Its caption correctly calls 40 rps the **workload demand ceiling**, then immediately calls the growing gap between the lines **scheduling + tail/queueing overhead**. This is a category error. For N=40 hosts, the analytical C/W bound is `40 × 17/8.6 ≈ 79.1 rps` and the capped served demand is 40 rps. The roughly 39.1-rps gap is overwhelmingly **unused theoretical capacity due to insufficient offered demand**, not 39.1 rps spent scheduling.

**Action:** Model/plot three distinct lines, with explicit units and labels:

```text
Analytical host capacity:              C_analytic(N) = N × 17/8.6
Efficiency-adjusted serving capacity: C_eff(N) = 0.95 × C_analytic(N)
Actually served demand:                S(N) = min(40, C_eff(N))
```

Below saturation, the 5% difference between C_analytic and C_eff is the *assumed throughput-efficiency derate* (not directly measured queueing). Above saturation, `C_eff − S` is *unused capacity*. Queueing and tail-latency penalties are **not determined by this two-line calculation** and must be shown separately if claimed. Replace the offending caption sentence and the corresponding §20.8 discussion. This is a strong case for a clearer three-curve diagram rather than a longer caveat.

### FTF-R04 — The TCO break-even x-axis is not a single feasible workload path (**P1**)

**Location:** Ch. 16 §16.3 and Fig. 16.1, printed pp. 233–243 / PDF pp. 253–263.

The arithmetic `$155,816.93 / $0.0208 ≈ 7.49 million requests/month` **is correct for a frozen 21-host cost**. The problem is its **interpretation as a generalized volume crossover** while the graph runs from 10⁴ to 10⁸ requests/month, with flat self-hosted and cloud lines, without defining how the traffic profile changes. This is not a criticism of the already explicit statement 'illustrative — not apples-to-apples'; it is a request that the illustration depict feasible, decision-relevant comparisons.

Under Ch. 4's 8-hour × 260-day annual activity calendar, 6.24M monthly requests corresponds to 10 rps average. At the plotted midpoint of 7.49M/month, the average becomes ≈12.0 rps. If the canonical **40-rps peak / 10-rps average = 4:1 ratio** scales with it, peak demand becomes **≈48 rps**, needing at least:

```text
N_KV, zero reserve = ceil(48.02 × 8.6 / 17) = 25 hosts
```

Not the original 21. A realistic 70% utilization + N−1 design needs even more. Thus the fixed 21-host cost is not a feasible owned-fleet quote for that **proportionally scaled** workload. If instead the author intends higher average load at a **fixed 40-rps peak**, say explicitly that the x-axis varies the *duration/duty cycle of utilization* rather than the peak (and restrict the x-domain to feasible served volume). The current 10⁸-request endpoint is also above a 40-rps service operating continuously over the canonical **active 624,000 seconds/month** (maximum 24.96M at 40 rps during those active hours), unless the activity calendar itself changes.

**Recommended replacement:** Give two graphs or two clearly labeled scenarios. **A:** fixed peak 40 rps and fixed owned 21-/30-host fleet, varying peak duration/traffic duty cycle; mark its feasible monthly bounds. **B:** scale both average and peak together, re-run `N_hosts(volume)` with integer slot floors and show self-hosted *stair-step* costs plus independent compute/SLO gate. The first graph legitimately illustrates amortization; the second informs procurement decisions. Annotate both for multi-region and quality/SLO comparability gaps.

### FTF-R05 — Fallback's work must appear in whole-task expectations (**P1**)

**Location:** Ch. 19 §19.8, printed pp. 287–288 / PDF pp. 307–308.

The 2% fallback category is described as requests that **exceed a turn limit and fall back to single-shot**. The text also recognizes that failed attempts may already have consumed model/tool work, but assigns them zero *added* turns in the displayed expectation. That can describe the *work performed by the post-fallback path*, but not the *full transaction's* resource footprint if an agent attempted turns before triggering the fallback. It is especially consequential because that expectation then informs token amplification, median/p95 latency and fleet economics.

**Action:** Distinguish two mechanisms and calculate each separately: (i) preemptive single-shot routing, before any agent work (legitimately zero added work), and (ii) agent timeout/turn-limit exhaustion followed by fallback (accumulated failed model calls, tool calls, queue time and retry must all remain in task totals). For actual workload-level p95, include fallback failures rather than condition away the hardest 2% except in an explicitly labeled *conditional completed-agent* metric. Show unconditional p95 and cost-per-completed-request as well. A 30-day 'telemetry' example tagged `[ILLUSTRATIVE][ASSUMPTION]` should not be visually confused with observations from a real deployment.

### FTF-R06 — Canonical SLA architecture remains deliberately unclosed (**GATE**, not hidden error)

**Location:** Ch. 4 Table 4-1 printed p. 51; Ch. 16 Table 16-2 printed pp. 238–239; Ch. 17 failover sections.

The canonical workload **requires multi-region active-active, 99.9% availability**. Ch. 16 explicitly states its priced alternatives are single-region and calls the multi-region row `NOT PRICED / NOT VALIDATED`; the 30-host configuration covers a **single host loss within one region**, not loss of an entire region. This distinction is *correctly acknowledged* and should not be called a numerical mistake. However, the book's central claim of a fully defensible architecture from all six workload dimensions remains incomplete for that canonical example.

**Action:** Keep the deferral as a genuine gate, not a general disclaimer buried near the pricing table. The canonical recommendation is conditional until at least two regional topologies, capacity/failure-domain placement, per-region index freshness, data-residency constraints, failover/RTO, traffic failover and their TCO sensitivity are worked or explicitly labeled an out-of-book exercise. If the intent is a **single-region teaching case**, change the canonical requirement accordingly and present active-active as a separately scoped extension; do not mix the two.

### FTF-R07 — Tables 16-1 / 16-2 are difficult to read in print (**P2**)

**Location:** printed pp. 236–239 / PDF pp. 256–259.

The `When it wins` column in Table 16-1 becomes a narrow vertical stack of short phrases; bold text such as 'only if the occupancy input holds' occupies many lines. This causes one comparison to span pages, wastes white space, and makes numeric comparisons hard. Table 16-2 repeats long status and cost notes in narrow cells.

**Action:** Separate numeric comparison from explanation. Keep only `mode / provisioned capacity / monthly included cost / $ per 1k / qualification` in the numeric table, and use a compact A/B/C note immediately below for **assumptions and failure points**. Give a specific 'NOT COMPARABLE' marker to the unpriced multi-region row. At final trim size, numeric columns should be readable without repeatedly tracking back across page breaks. Avoid a continuation page with nothing but a few long word-wrapped rows.

### FTF-R08 — Figures 19.2/19.3 need stronger print-scale hierarchy (**P2**)

**Location:** printed pp. 285–286 / PDF pp. 305–306.

Both are legible when enlarged, but Fig. 19.3's generated-reasoning γ is a **thin orange slice**, with some labels near or below 6 pt in the embedded chart. It is theoretically represented in the legend but visually negligible, precisely when the book's latency arithmetic depends on those small generated-token counts. The green final-output strip at each 'Turn T' is conceptually appropriate **if each column represents a separate completed scenario that ends at T**, but it is easy to interpret the columns as successive internal states of a single live agent that have already generated the final output at Turn 1, 2, etc.

**Action:** Add a zoomed annotation for δ and γ with precise per-turn token counts and a callout `each column = counterfactual completion after T turns, including one final 300-token answer`. Or switch to an actual within-task timeline and place terminal output only at task completion. Ensure all chart labels are readable at 100% physical trim; no 5.9-pt legends or terminal labels.

### FTF-R09 — Fleet unit-cost generalization needs a denominator (**P2**)

**Location:** Ch. 20 §20.1, printed p. 290 / PDF p. 310.

“Average cost per query declines as the fleet grows” is true only under a **specified load path** that increases completed requests enough to amortize the fixed cost. At a fixed 10-rps demand, doubling a homogeneous owned fleet with the same labor/energy policies increases cost per served request, not decreases it. The paragraph later gives the correct distinction (constant marginal per-host cost, fixed-term amortization), but the opening unconditional claim should be fixed.

**Suggested text:** “As served demand grows and fixed overhead is spread across more SLO-good requests, cost per request may decline **until additional capacity steps dominate**; simply adding idle hosts at fixed demand increases unit cost.”

### FTF-R10 — Permanent source records vs unstable raw URLs (**P2**)

**Location:** 2026-10-28 notes in Appendix A, printed pp. 375–380 / PDF pp. 395–400; references/source ledger.

The new technology notes are much better than unsupported 'latest model' claims: many sources pin a revision and clearly say code inspection is not a deployment benchmark. However at least one referenced `huggingface.co/.../raw/main/generation_config.json` uses a moving `main` ref rather than a commit, and many machine URLs wrap into dense 7–8 pt footnotes. A later reader cannot reproduce the original source bytes from a moving path alone.

**Action:** Pin all interpretation-bearing raw model configs, vendor readmes and backend code to immutable revision IDs, including generation configs; include retrieval date separately. Move full URLs/hashes to the source ledger and use short reference identifiers in the chapter. For print, use stable human-readable short source names with appendix source numbers. Ensure digital PDF footnote links remain active and printed identifiers can be typed reliably.

**External sample verification (not an exhaustive source audit):** The Google Gemini 3.8 Flash model card identifies a 2026-09-02 publication; Intel's IPEX-LLM repository is archived and carries a known-security-issues notice; the public source commit `d7f6638a70eff36b9ba9d0f6e97a62b9d9c24c5c` cited by the PDF exists. These spot-checks support the relevant provenance statements; they do **not** establish correctness of every 2026-10-28 implementation detail or historical snapshot. Links: <https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/gemini-3-8-flash/>, <https://github.com/intel/ipex-llm>, <https://github.com/zhiqitao/from-token-to-fleet/commit/d7f6638a70eff36b9ba9d0f6e97a62b9d9c24c5c>.

### FTF-R11 — Caption architecture and page economy (**P2/P3**)

Some captions are effectively mini-essays with **takeaway + scope + independent derivation + caveat + repetition of adjacent body text**, especially Figs. 16.1, 19.2, 19.3 and 20.1. Several pages give a relatively small figure/caption substantial standalone space, while dense tables are fragmented. The book correctly states a three-part caption convention in Sources/Method; apply it more strictly.

**Action:** Keep captions to (1) the one-sentence diagram takeaway, (2) essential assumption/evidence/status and (3) a single validation caveat. Put derivations and alternate modeling assumptions in surrounding numbered paragraphs rather than a second paragraph masquerading as caption. Figure-centered pages may intentionally have whitespace, but verify page cost and neighboring float behavior against a print proof; do not squeeze the diagrams simply to reduce page count.
