20.2. Mental Model
The mental model for fleet optimization replaces the single-host query pipeline with a two-level 
hierarchy. At the lower level, each host processes queries using its GPU/CPU pipeline. At the upper 
level, a scheduler routes incoming requests across hosts, handling load balancing, model parallelism 
where needed, and graceful degradation under overload.
The scheduler’s decisions have direct impact on the tail of the latency distribution. Poor placement 
can cause some hosts to saturate while others remain underutilized, inflating the 99th-percentile 
latency even when aggregate throughput is ample. Conversely, intelligent scheduling—such as 
consistent hashing for session affinity or predictive placement based on historical patterns—keeps 
utilization flat across the fleet and minimizes tail latency.
A critical assumption in this model is that the workload follows a known arrival process. For the 
canonical scenario (Ch 4, Table 4-2): ~10 rps average and ~40 rps peak. With ~9,200 in-tokens and 
~300 out-tokens per query, a ~8.6 s service time per request (1.08 s prefill + ~7.5 s decode), and a 70B 
dense FP16 model running on 8×H100 (640 GB total HBM3), the fleet must sustain ~40 requests/s 
at peak, which — by Little’s law — corresponds to λ·W ≈ 40 × 8.6 ≈ 344 requests in flight, not 
merely the ~100 concurrent users or the nominal 40 rps. The distinction between “concurrent users” 
(a population), the arrival rate (rps), and in-flight requests (λ·W) is exactly what Chapter 17 develops; 
it is the difference between sizing for a population and sizing a fleet against a latency bound.
20.3. Worked Example
Take a fleet of N hosts, each with 8×H100 running a single 70B FP16 model instance. Two 
capacities must be reconciled — throughput (tokens/s) and KV residency (concurrent requests) — 
and neither can be reduced to a single “rps” constant, because rps is workload-specific (per-request 
input/output tokens, context length, batch).
Latency floor first, then reconcile the two capacity bounds. A 300-output-token request at 
a ~25 ms/token decode rate (Chapter 8) takes ~7.5 s of generation alone, plus 1.08 s prefill, so the 
service time is ~8.6 s per request (before tool calls). Two separate ceilings bound how many requests
