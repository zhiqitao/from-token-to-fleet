Designing the AI System: From Constraints to 
Candidates
Designing an AI system is constraint-driven generation, not menu-picking: the architect extracts 
the hard constraints from a characterized workload — the three gates this chapter fixes: memory fit, 
throughput at the SLO, and quality — then synthesizes candidate architectures that must clear them. 
The payoff is a bounded set of two to three candidates that each fail a distinct, inspectable gate — 
memory fit, throughput, or quality — which the measuring chapters then adjudicate.
The Architect’s Question
Designing an AI system means producing candidate architectures from the substrate — memory, 
compute, communication, and parallelism — rather than selecting a canned stack. This chapter works 
through a synthesis method: given a characterized workload from the six-dimension framework 
(Ch. 4), generate two or three candidate architectures that each satisfy the hard constraints — the 
three gates fixed here: memory fit, throughput at the SLO, and quality — then carry them forward 
for evaluation in Chs. 14–16. The core thesis is that design is constraint-driven generation, not 
menu-picking. This distinguishes our approach from the abstract meta-method presented in Ch. 22, 
which operates at a level of generality that does not instantiate against concrete substrate constraints. 
In the language of the book’s spine — the decision loop constrain → synthesize → measure → decide 
→ refine — this chapter is the synthesize step: we turn a constraint-characterized workload into a 
bounded set of candidate architectures that then enter the measure/decide stages in Chs. 14–16.
